# Disturbance of Memory on the Acropolis

## Summary

In this 1936 letter to Romain Rolland, Freud analyzes his own experience of derealization on the Acropolis. He argues that his disbelief at actually seeing Athens ('So all this really does exist') masked an earlier, unacknowledged sense that his success had surpassed his father's, creating unconscious guilt about outstripping his father.

## Key facts

- Freud experienced a strange thought on the Acropolis: 'So all this really does exist, just as we learnt at school!'—expressed as incredulity about the reality of what he was seeing. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p7056]
- At Trieste, before proceeding to Athens, Freud and his brother were inexplicably depressed despite having wanted to visit Greece, displaying resistance to the fulfillment of a wish. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p7057]
- Freud concluded that his incredulity represented an example of 'too good to be true'—a repudiation of pleasurable reality based on internal frustration from a severe superego. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p7057]
- The sense of guilt was attached to the satisfaction of having gone further than his father, as though to excel one's father was still something forbidden. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p7062]

## Inferences

- Inference: The experience demonstrates derealization as a defense mechanism against feelings that would be unbearable to acknowledge consciously—specifically, the forbidden pleasure of surpassing one's father.
- Inference: The phenomenon of 'too good to be true' involves the superego treating external good fortune as undeserved, similar to the mechanism of people 'wrecked by success.'

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Freud acknowledges that derealization is still 'little understood' scientifically and presents this as an exploratory analysis.
