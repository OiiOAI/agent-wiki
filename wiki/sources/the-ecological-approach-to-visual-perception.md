# The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception

## Summary

This book, first published in 1979, presents James J. Gibson's ecological approach to visual perception. It examines how we see the environment around us—including surfaces, their layout, colors, and textures—and how we perceive our position within it, our movement, the affordances of objects, and how to perform actions. Gibson argues that natural vision depends on the eyes in the head on a body supported by the ground, with the brain as merely the central organ of a complete visual system.

## Inferences

- Inference: The book's structure suggests a progression from basic stimulus information (chapters 4-5) to more complex perceptual phenomena including events (chapter 6), self-perception (chapter 7), and the overarching theory of affordances (chapter 8).
- Inference: Gibson's ecological approach fundamentally rejects the assumption that perception involves processing sensory inputs, instead proposing that information is directly picked up from the environment.
- Inference: The theory asserts that perceiving and moving are mutually dependent—one must move to perceive fully, and one must perceive to move effectively.
- Inference: Gibson's ecological approach fundamentally challenges the stimulus-response model of perception by emphasizing direct perception of environmental invariants rather than mediated cognitive processing.
- Inference: The ecological approach represents a paradigm shift in perception research, replacing sensation-based theories with information-based theories grounded in ecological optics.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Gibson notes he is uncertain about the kinds of culturally transmitted knowledge, acknowledging discussions of 'media of communication' are 'glib and superficial' and that there are 'many kinds merging into one another, of great complexity'.
