# The Master and His Emissary: The Divided Brain and the Making of the Western World

## Summary

A comprehensive examination of cerebral hemisphere differences and their implications for Western cultural history. McGilchrist argues that the two hemispheres of the human brain process reality in fundamentally different ways—the right hemisphere apprehends the world holistically, in context, while the left hemisphere abstracts and manipulates. The book traces how this duality has shaped philosophy, science, and art from ancient Greece through the modern era.

## Inferences

- Inference: The chapter's argument that Romanticism emerged 'seamlessly' from the Enlightenment (rather than as a reaction against it) suggests that the transition was driven by the right hemisphere's compensatory response to left-hemisphere dominance, not by deliberate intellectual rejection.
- Inference: McGilchrist's claim that the 'spots of time' experiences involve right hemisphere mediation is supported by their association with religious awe, large visual forms, and childhood—all linked to right hemisphere function.
- Inference: The parallel drawn between Descartes' philosophy and schizophrenia suggests that excessive left-hemisphere dominance may produce phenomenology resembling psychotic states.
- Inference: The breadth of the bibliography suggests the work draws on an unusually wide range of disciplines beyond neuroscience proper, including philosophy, literature, art history, and cultural history

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The claim that Bentham had 'mild autism' is presented as an inference by McGilchrist, not as a clinical diagnosis.
