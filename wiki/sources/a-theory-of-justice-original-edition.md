# A Theory of Justice (Original Edition)

## Summary

A Theory of Justice by John Rawls presents a systematic alternative to utilitarianism, Rawls's theory of 'justice as fairness' generalizes and carries to a higher order of abstraction the traditional theory of the social contract as represented by Locke, Rousseau, and Kant. The book is divided into three parts: Theory, Institutions, and Ends. Rawls argues that the principles of justice would be chosen in a hypothetical 'original position' behind a 'veil of ignorance,' leading to two principles: equal basic liberties and inequalities only if they benefit the least advantaged. The work develops a comprehensive account of justice for the basic structure of society, addresses the priority problem through lexical ordering, and examines the stability of just institutions through the sense of justice.

## Key facts

- The book presents 'justice as fairness' as a contractarian theory that generalizes the social contract tradition of Locke, Rousseau, and Kant. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p10]
- Rawls argues that the dominant systematic theory in modern moral philosophy has been utilitarianism, which he seeks to replace with a workable and systematic moral conception. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p10]
- The book contains three main parts: Theory, Institutions, and Ends, covering fundamental justice theory, applications to political economy and civil disobedience, and the relation between justice and the good. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p2]
- The revised edition published in 1999 included changes in response to criticisms concerning primary social goods and the concept of a person. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p9]
- The difference principle holds that social and economic inequalities are justifiable only if they benefit the representative man who is worse off. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p80]
- A 'perfectly just' arrangement is one in which the expectations of the least advantaged are maximized and no changes can improve their situation without making someone else worse off. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p81]
- Pure procedural justice occurs when there is no independent criterion for the right result, only a correct or fair procedure such that the outcome is correct or fair provided the procedure has been properly followed. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p87]
- The veil of ignorance excludes particular facts: no one knows his place in society, class position, natural assets, conception of the good, or particular circumstances of their society. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p132]
- The parties in the original position are mutually disinterested, taking no interest in one another's interests. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p126]
- The maximin rule instructs one to rank alternatives by their worst possible outcomes. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p148]
- Rawls argues the two principles of justice are preferred over the principle of average utility based on strains of commitment, psychological stability, and self-respect. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p168]
- The four-stage sequence applies principles of justice through original position, constitutional convention, legislature, and rule application. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p183]
- Equal liberty of conscience requires that limitations be based only on evidence and reasoning acceptable to all, without presupposing particular metaphysical doctrines. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p199]
- The First Principle states that each person is to have an equal right to the most extensive total system of equal basic liberties compatible with a similar system of liberty for all. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p275]
- The Second Principle states that social and economic inequalities are to be arranged so they are to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged (consistent with the just savings principle) and attached to offices and positions open to all under conditions of fair equality of opportunity. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p275]
- The First Priority Rule states that liberty can be restricted only for the sake of liberty: a less extensive liberty must strengthen the total system, or a less than equal liberty must be acceptable to those with lesser liberty. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p276]
- The Second Priority Rule states that the second principle of justice is lexically prior to the principle of efficiency and maximizing the sum of advantages, and fair opportunity is prior to the difference principle. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p276]
- Civil disobedience addresses the sense of justice of the majority and serves as a stabilizing device of a constitutional system. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p345]
- The thin theory of the good secures premises about primary goods needed to derive principles of justice without circularity. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p353]
- Goodness as rationality defines good in three stages: good X, good X for K, and good X for K with rational plan. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p357]
- The Aristotelian Principle states that human enjoyment increases with the complexity of realized capacities. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p379]
- Self-respect is perhaps the most important primary good, comprising sense of one's own worth and confidence in one's ability. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p391]
- A well-ordered society is one effectively regulated by a public conception of justice whose members have a strong desire to act justly. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p404]
