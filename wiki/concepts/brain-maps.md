# Brain Maps

## Summary

Brain maps are organized representations of the body and sensory surfaces in the cerebral cortex. Wilder Penfield first mapped these in the 1930s, showing they are topographical (adjacent body parts map adjacently in the brain). Merzenich demonstrated that these maps are not immutable but change throughout life based on experience, use, and competition between brain regions.

## Key facts

- Wilder Penfield discovered that sensory and motor brain maps are topographical—areas adjacent on the body's surface are generally adjacent on brain maps. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p57]
- Merzenich discovered that brain maps vary in their borders and size from person to person and change depending on what one does over a lifetime. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p58]
- When a monkey's median nerve was cut, the brain maps for the radial and ulnar nerves almost doubled in size and invaded the unused median nerve map space. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p68]
- After finger amputation, the brain map for the amputated finger disappeared and maps for adjacent fingers grew into that space. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p70]
- Brain maps for normal body parts change every few weeks, showing plasticity is a normal ongoing phenomenon. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p70]
- Topographical organization emerges because everyday activities involve repeating sequences in fixed order—thumb and index finger often touch simultaneously, so their maps form close together. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p76]
