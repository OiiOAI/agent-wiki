# Axiomatic Decision Theory

## Summary

Axiomatic decision theory uses mathematical axioms to characterize what observable behaviors are consistent with theoretical constructs like utility maximization. The approach, pioneered by Samuelson, von Neumann, and Morgenstern, defines latent variables (such as utility or beliefs) only in relation to their implications for observable data. This methodology ensures precise testing of theoretical claims without auxiliary assumptions.

## Key facts

- The axiomatic method requires stating precisely how theories restrict the behavior of data, by writing down complete necessary and sufficient conditions that data must satisfy. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p21]
- Axioms capture exactly what models imply for a particular data set - no more and no less - removing the need for spurious precision in relating latent variables to observables. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p21]
- In the von Neumann-Morgenstern framework, the utility function on lotteries is only defined up to linear (affine) transformations, not arbitrary monotonic transformations. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p35]
- Three critical axioms for dopaminergic reward prediction error are: Coherent Prize Dominance, Coherent Lottery Dominance, and No Surprise Equivalence. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p29]

## Inferences

- Inference: The axiomatic approach is particularly valuable in neuroeconomics because it allows testing whether brain activity has the formal structure required by economic theories without assuming specific neural implementations.
- Inference: Axioms enable a 'nested' technique of modeling and experimentation where successively richer versions of models can be tested one step at a time.
