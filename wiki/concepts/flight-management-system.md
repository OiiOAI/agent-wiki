# Flight Management System (Intent Communication Example)

## Summary

The Flight Management System example illustrates how computer systems also need to communicate their intent to human operators. In a studied incident, the system compensated silently for a rudder control malfunction until it reached its limit, then handed control back to pilots without warning, causing the aircraft to stall. This demonstrated that automation requires interface design that conveys machine intentions.

## Key facts

- When a rudder control blade was accidentally kicked to an extreme position, the flight management system compensated with other controls to keep the aircraft in straight flight without notifying the crew. [raw/books/strategy/Sources of Power How People Make Decisions by Gary A. Klein .epub.pdf#p281]
- When the flight management system reached its limit, it turned off and handed controls back to the pilots with the aircraft in an out-of-tolerance condition, causing an unannounced stall. [raw/books/strategy/Sources of Power How People Make Decisions by Gary A. Klein .epub.pdf#p281]
- Pilots first believed they had an engine problem and took ineffective actions that made the situation worse before eventually regaining control. [raw/books/strategy/Sources of Power How People Make Decisions by Gary A. Klein .epub.pdf#p281]
- According to Earl Wiener, the most common questions about automated systems during nonroutine events are: 'What is it doing? Why is it doing that? What is it going to do next?'. [raw/books/strategy/Sources of Power How People Make Decisions by Gary A. Klein .epub.pdf#p282]
- The study was funded by NASA with George Kaempf managing the project that examined how computer systems need to communicate intent. [raw/books/strategy/Sources of Power How People Make Decisions by Gary A. Klein .epub.pdf#p280]

## Inferences

- Inference: Sophisticated automated systems need to identify appropriate times and formats to convey their intent to human team members.
