# Problem of Other Minds

## Summary

The problem of other minds is the philosophical challenge of demonstrating that any system other than oneself is genuinely conscious. Solms addresses this directly in the context of artificial consciousness, arguing that science requires only reasonable hypotheses tested with realistic chances for falsification, not absolute proof.

## Key facts

- Wikipedia states that because qualia are inherently first-person phenomena and there is no empirical definition of consciousness, a test for artificial consciousness may be impossible. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p260]
- Solms argues science does not require absolute proof; it requires a reasonable hypothesis accounting for available evidence that can be realistically falsified. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p260]
- Solms proposes that damaging the neural correlate of consciousness in the artificial system should obliterate its voluntary behaviour, analogous to how lesioning the PAG does in vertebrates. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p263]
- Evidence for artificial sentience would include the system working to achieve feelings even when those feelings bestow no adaptive advantage, similar to addiction in biological organisms. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p263-264]
- Solms states that if he remains substantially undecided whether the artificial self is conscious, that would be a remarkable outcome. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p264]

## Inferences

- Inference: Solms applies the same epistemological standards to artificial consciousness as to animal consciousness, acknowledging there is no consensus on hydranencephalic children or non-human animals.
