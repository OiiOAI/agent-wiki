# Information Overload and the Speed-Up

## Summary

The volume of information flooding our lives has increased dramatically, causing us to focus on any one thing for shorter periods. Scientists Hilbert and Lopez found that information exposure grew from 40 newspapers' worth per day in 1986 to 174 newspapers' worth by 2007. This 'Great Acceleration' affects not just technology but pace of life broadly.

## Key facts

- Scientists Hilbert and Lopez calculated that information exposure grew from the equivalent of 40 newspapers per day in 1986 to 174 newspapers per day by 2007. [raw/books/productivity/Stolen Focus _ Why You Can't Pay Attention - and How to Think.pdf#p38]
- Lehmann's research showed that flooding a system with more information causes faster rise and fall of topics, reducing time spent on any individual topic. [raw/books/productivity/Stolen Focus _ Why You Can't Pay Attention - and How to Think.pdf#p37]
- People talk significantly faster now than in the 1950s, and in 20 years, city pedestrians have started walking 10 percent faster. [raw/books/productivity/Stolen Focus _ Why You Can't Pay Attention - and How to Think.pdf#p41]
- Speed-reading studies show that faster reading always comes at the cost of comprehension, even for trained professional speed-readers. [raw/books/productivity/Stolen Focus _ Why You Can't Pay Attention - and How to Think.pdf#p40]
- Guy Claxton's research shows that deliberately slow practices (yoga, tai chi, meditation) improve ability to pay attention by training at a pace compatible with human nature. [raw/books/productivity/Stolen Focus _ Why You Can't Pay Attention - and How to Think.pdf#p41-42]

## Inferences

- Inference: The information overload is structural and economic—consumer capitalism depends on maximizing consumption, which includes information consumption.
