# Principle of Fairness (Obligations)

## Summary

A principle for individuals stating that a person is required to do his part as defined by the rules of an institution when: (1) the institution is just (satisfies the two principles of justice), and (2) one has voluntarily accepted the benefits of the arrangement or taken advantage of the opportunities it offers. The principle accounts for all requirements that are obligations as distinct from natural duties. It is not possible to be bound to unjust institutions.

## Key facts

- The principle of fairness holds that a person is required to do his part as defined by the rules of an institution when: the institution is just (satisfies the two principles of justice), and one has voluntarily accepted the benefits of the arrangement or taken advantage of opportunities to further one's interests. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p111]
- All obligations arise from the principle of fairness; obligations arise as a result of our voluntary acts such as giving express or tacit undertakings, accepting benefits, or joining a practice. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p112]
- By the principle of fairness it is not possible to be bound to unjust institutions, or at least to institutions which exceed the limits of tolerable injustice. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p112]
- The necessary background for obligations to arise from consensual acts does not exist for obligations to unjust regimes; obligatory ties presuppose just institutions. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p112]
- It is a mistake to argue that justice as fairness has the consequence that citizens are under obligation to unjust regimes which coerce their consent. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p112]
- The principle of fairness holds that a person is under an obligation when they have voluntarily accepted the benefits of a just institution. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p310]
- The principle of fairness has two parts: one states how obligations are acquired through voluntary acts, and another lays down that the institution must be just. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p311]
- Acquiescence in or consent to clearly unjust institutions does not give rise to obligations. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p311]
- The principle of fidelity to promises is a special case of the principle of fairness applied to the social practice of promising. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p313]
- A bona fide promise arises in accordance with the rule of promising when the practice it represents is just. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p313]

## Inferences

- Inference: Since obligations presuppose just institutions, political obligation as it applies to the average citizen (who may not have performed a clear binding action) is not covered by the principle of fairness and may not exist strictly speaking.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Rawls notes that political obligation for citizens generally and the obligation to keep promises are problematical cases requiring further discussion.
