# Critical Period for Language

## Summary

The critical period for language refers to the observation that children acquire language more easily and completely than adults, and that early language deprivation produces lasting deficits. Deacon argues this may be a 'spandrel'—an incidental by-product of maturation that languages evolved to exploit—rather than evidence of a dedicated language acquisition device.

## Key facts

- Children's perceptual responses to speech sounds become committed to first-language features by the first year, producing permanent difficulty distinguishing unheard phonemes. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p132]
- Feral children raised with minimal human contact show persistent language deficits, attesting to the cost of lacking early language exposure. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p148]
- Deacon argues Kanzi's critical-period-like advantages in language acquisition argue against a species-specific language learning mechanism, since apes lack language-ancestral adaptations. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p135]
- The critical period may be a 'spandrel'—an incidental feature of brain maturation that languages evolved to exploit, not a specialized adaptation. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p147]

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Whether the critical period is truly incidental or represents some specialized neural mechanism is not definitively resolved.
