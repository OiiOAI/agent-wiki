# Strategic Planning

## Summary

Strategic planning as practiced since the 1970s has been a disappointment for many businesses. Most senior executives report being unhappy with both the process and outcomes, with plans often failing to predict competitive outcomes and instead functioning as elaborate budgeting exercises.

## Key facts

- Since the 1970s, most businesses have adopted the language of strategic planning. [raw/books/strategy/The Crux - Richard Rumelt.pdf#p256]
- A 2006 McKinsey survey found fewer than half of eight hundred executives surveyed were satisfied with their strategic-planning process. [raw/books/strategy/The Crux - Richard Rumelt.pdf#p256]
- In 2014, Bain partner James Allen noted that many corporate leaders are fed up with their strategic planning processes, with general agreement that 97 percent of these efforts are a waste of time. [raw/books/strategy/The Crux - Richard Rumelt.pdf#p256]
- A common complaint is that strategic plans don't work out; they cannot predict competitive outcomes. [raw/books/strategy/The Crux - Richard Rumelt.pdf#p256]
- Most so-called strategic-planning exercises do not produce strategies but are actually attempting to predict and control financial outcomes—a form of budgeting. [raw/books/strategy/The Crux - Richard Rumelt.pdf#p257]
- Royalfield's CEO used financial performance terms to shape options and shifted strategic thinking away from technology, product, customer, and competition toward tactics designed to achieve desired accounting results. [raw/books/strategy/The Crux - Richard Rumelt.pdf#p283]

## Inferences

- Inference: The critique suggests that strategic planning fails because it conflates financial projections with strategic thinking, avoiding confrontation with fundamental competitive challenges.
