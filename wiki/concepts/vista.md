# Vista

## Summary

A vista is what is seen from a place, a set of unhidden surfaces forming a semienclosure. Gibson distinguishes vistas from points of observation—a vista's 'here' is an extended region rather than a geometric point. Vistas are serially connected, with each one leading to the next through occluding edges.

## Key facts

- A vista is what is seen from here, with the proviso that 'here' is not a point but an extended region—a semienclosure, a set of unhidden surfaces. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p220]
- Vistas are serially connected since at the end of an alley the next alley opens up; at the edge of a doorway the next room opens up; at the corner of the street the next street opens up. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p220]
- In a terrestrial environment of semienclosed places, each vista is unique—each is its own landmark since the habitat never duplicates itself. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p220]
- Going from one place to another involves the opening up of the vista ahead and closing in of the vista behind through a continuous set of reversible transitions. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p220]

## Inferences

- Inference: The serial connectivity of vistas means that the environment provides its own inherent spatial framework—orientation does not require internal cognitive maps but rather knowledge of how vistas connect.
