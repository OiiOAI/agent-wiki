# Religious Practices

## Summary

Freud draws parallels between obsessional neurosis and religious practices, suggesting both involve suppression of drive impulses and ritualistic observances. Religious ceremonials share with neurotic ceremonials the anxiety produced by their neglect and their stereotyping. However, religious ceremonials have recognized symbolic meaning while neurotic ones appear senseless until interpreted. Freud suggests religion represents a universal form of what obsessional neurosis represents individually.

## Key facts

- The formation of a religion is based on the suppression of certain drive impulses, though in religion these are egoistic rather than exclusively sexual as in the neuroses. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p3006]
- A sense of guilt following upon continual temptation and expectant anxiety in the form of fear of divine punishment characterizes religious life. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p3006]
- In religious life, suppression of drives proves to be inadequate and interminable, with backslidings into sin giving rise to acts of penance. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p3006]

## Inferences

- Inference: Freud suggests that the difference between obsessional neurosis and religion lies partly in the nature of the repressed drives—sexual in neurosis versus egoistic/social in religion.
