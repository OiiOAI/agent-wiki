# Weismann Doctrine

## Summary

The Weismann Doctrine, named after August Weismann, holds that there is a strict distinction between the germ plasm (genome) and the somatic tissues of organisms. It conveys three related ideas: (1) the segregation doctrine — germ and somatic lines are strictly segregated during ontogeny; (2) the inheritance doctrine — there is no nongenetic inheritance; (3) the causal asymmetry doctrine — extragenetic elements depend on genes, but genes do not depend on them. Thompson argues all three ideas are problematic.

## Key facts

- The segregation doctrine fails because the division between germ line and somatic line does not exist in all animals and is not applicable to plants; the most common mode of development is somatic embryogenesis where all cells can participate in both body and gamete formation. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p172]
- The inheritance doctrine is mistaken because heredity in the widest sense is transgenerational conservation of developmental resources, and there is considerable evidence for epigenetic inheritance systems involving mechanisms other than DNA replication. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p173]
- The causal asymmetry doctrine is false because genes cannot exist without membranes, and genes never occur apart from the epigenetic elements of the intracellular environment; 'naked replicators' are never transmitted. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p175]
- Careful examination of the Weismann Doctrine leads to the conclusion that heritability is controlled by processes of development in the 'somatic ecology' of the organism. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p176]
