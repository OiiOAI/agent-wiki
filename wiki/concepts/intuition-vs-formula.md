# Intuition vs. Formula

## Summary

The finding, originating from Paul Meehl's 1954 work, that simple statistical formulas consistently outperform expert clinical judgments in predicting outcomes across diverse domains. This counterintuitive result has been replicated in over 200 studies and persists despite professional resistance, because human judgments are inconsistent, affected by irrelevant contextual factors, and subject to the same cognitive biases as laypeople.

## Key facts

- Paul Meehl's 1954 review of 20 studies found that statistical prediction beat clinical prediction in approximately 60% of cases, with subsequent research confirming roughly 200+ studies showing similar results. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p288]
- Simple unit-weighted formulas often perform as well as optimally weighted regression equations, a finding Robyn Dawes called 'the robust beauty of improper linear models in decision making.'. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p292]
- Humans are highly inconsistent in making judgments: radiologists showed 20% disagreement when re-evaluating the same X-rays, and similar inconsistency was found across auditors, pathologists, and other professionals. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p290]
- Orley Ashenfelter developed a simple formula using weather variables to predict Bordeaux wine prices with correlation over 0.90, outperforming expert wine critics. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p289]

## Inferences

- Inference: The superiority of algorithms over experts occurs because algorithms are consistent (same inputs always produce same outputs) and can detect weak valid signals that humans fail to exploit consistently, whereas humans are swayed by irrelevant contextual factors and mood changes.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The text notes that in rare cases where human override is justified (the 'broken leg' rule), it typically involves genuinely exceptional information that would also be captured by well-designed algorithms.
