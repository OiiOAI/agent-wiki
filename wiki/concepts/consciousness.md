# Consciousness

## Summary

Consciousness is a multifaceted phenomenon that includes the capacity for self-awareness, being awake and aware, and awareness of specific mental states. For the question of cognitive control, the most relevant aspect is consciousness as access to one's own thoughts and reasoning processes. Conscious perception requires the activity of prefrontal regions, which become active slightly later than early visual areas when stimuli are consciously perceived. The selective filtering of what reaches consciousness is itself actively controllable, allowing us to choose what we attend to and hold in working memory.

## Key facts

- Consciousness requires the activity of prefrontal regions, which become active slightly later than early visual areas when stimuli are consciously perceived versus subliminally processed. [raw/books/neuroscience/Free Agents _ How Evolution Gave Us Free Will - Kevin J_ Mitchell.pdf#p262]
- The key to control is precisely the selectivity of conscious awareness—we are configured so that most cognitive processes operate subconsciously, with only certain types of information bubbling up to consciousness on a need-to-know basis. [raw/books/neuroscience/Free Agents _ How Evolution Gave Us Free Will - Kevin J_ Mitchell.pdf#p262]
- Conscious exercising of cognitive control involves selectively bringing together information about disparate aspects of the world, the state of our bodies, and our current cognitive processes with metacognitive signals of certainty into a common global neuronal workspace. [raw/books/neuroscience/Free Agents _ How Evolution Gave Us Free Will - Kevin J_ Mitchell.pdf#p264]
- Consciousness is defined as a state of perceptual awareness, or selective attention writ large, and in people is fundamentally an awareness of self and awareness of being aware. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p386]
- Francis Crick turned to studying consciousness at age 60 in 1976 after solving the problem of the gene and cracking the genetic code. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p387]
- Crick and Koch distinguished between the 'easy problem' of consciousness (neural correlates, unity) and the 'hard problem' (how subjective experience arises from objective neural processes). [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p390]
- The unity of consciousness refers to how various sensory modalities are melded into a single, coherent, conscious experience, and can break down in patients with severed brain hemispheres. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p388]
- Subjectivity, the second characteristic of conscious awareness, poses the more formidable scientific challenge because each person experiences a world of private and unique sensations. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p388]
- Nagel argues science cannot take on consciousness without discovering the elements of subjective consciousness, which are likely basic components of brain function existing in a form not yet imaginable. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p390]

## Inferences

- Inference: Conscious experience enables imagination—we are aware not of all neural firing but of the meanings carried by select patterns, allowing us to manipulate ideas in truly novel, creative ways.
- Inference: The evolution of the ability to model our own thoughts likely went hand in hand with the ability to model other people's thoughts, as both involve recursive self-modeling capabilities.
- Inference: The binding problem in visual perception is directly related to the unity of consciousness problem, as both concern how separate brain regions and sensory inputs are integrated into coherent perceptual experiences.
- Inference: The claustrum hypothesis represents Crick's final scientific contribution, proposing this brain structure as a coordinator analogous to an orchestra conductor.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: How conscious experience arises from neural processes remains unexplained—this is the 'hard problem' of consciousness.
- Uncertain: Whether consciousness can ever be explained in physical terms remains debated among philosophers, with some like Colin McGinn arguing the human mind may be incapable of solving this problem.
