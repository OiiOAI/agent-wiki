# Oedipus Complex

## Summary

The Oedipus complex is the universal event of early childhood identified by Freud through his self-analysis in 1897—falling in love with the mother and jealousy of the father. This discovery, revealed in letters to Fliess, provided the key to understanding both classical tragedy and the development of neuroses.

## Inferences

- Inference: Freud first explicitly formulated the Oedipus complex in Letter 71 of October 15, 1897, following intensive self-analysis.
- Inference: The Oedipus complex represents a universal stage of psychosexual development that, when inadequately resolved, contributes to neurosogenesis.
- Inference: Freud's analysis of Hamlet suggests that the play's effectiveness stems from the audience recognizing their own repressed Oedipal impulses through the hero's paralysis.
- Inference: Freud links the deepest and most invariable motive for estrangement between people of the same sex to rivalry in love established in early childhood through the Oedipus complex.
- Inference: The Oedipus complex connects to totemism, the first socio-religious institution, where the two great crimes (mother incest and parricide) are also those prohibited by totem law.
- Inference: The Oedipus complex represents a universal psychological stage, as Freud states it affects nearly all human beings.
- Inference: The resolution of the Oedipus complex creates the superego, which then becomes a permanent internal agency admonishing and repressing libidinal impulses throughout life.
- Inference: The asymmetry between how males and females relate to the Oedipus complex (termination versus entry) suggests a fundamental role for genital difference in structuring psychic development.
- Inference: The claim that Hamlet represents a failure to act on Oedipal wishes indicates that literary heroes can unknowingly embody unconscious conflicts.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Freud later expressed doubt about Shakespeare's authorship of the plays attributed to him, suggesting the author was not the man from Stratford.
- Uncertain: Freud notes the reaction to castration threat 'does not always have these dreaded consequences' and depends on quantitative relations.
