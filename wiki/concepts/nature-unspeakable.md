# Nature as the Unspeakable / History as the Speakable

## Summary

Nature is the realm of the unspeakable, having no voice of its own and exhibiting utter indifference to human culture. Its silence is the very possibility of language. The attempt to master nature by explaining it fully is actually a refusal of metaphor and language itself. History, by contrast, is the realm of the speakable—where genuine narrative invites us to rethink what we thought we knew, setting necessities into the context of the possible. Explanation settles issues by showing matters must end as they have; narrative raises issues by showing matters do not end as they must but as they do.

- Nature is the realm of the unspeakable; it has no voice of its own and nothing to say; we experience its unspeakability as its utter indifference to human culture. [raw/books/philosophy/Finite and Infinite Games_ A Vision of Life as Play and - Carse, James.pdf#p119]
- The unspeakability of nature is the very possibility of language; by confronting us with radical unlikeness, nature becomes the source of metaphor. [raw/books/philosophy/Finite and Infinite Games_ A Vision of Life as Play and - Carse, James.pdf#p123]
- History is the realm of the speakable; no speaking is possible that is not itself historical. [raw/books/philosophy/Finite and Infinite Games_ A Vision of Life as Play and - Carse, James.pdf#p124]
- Explanations set all apparent possibilities into the context of the necessary; stories set all necessities into the context of the possible. [raw/books/philosophy/Finite and Infinite Games_ A Vision of Life as Play and - Carse, James.pdf#p125]
- The meaning of finite speech lies in what precedes its utterance, what is already the case; the meaning of infinite speech lies in what comes of its utterance. [raw/books/philosophy/Finite and Infinite Games_ A Vision of Life as Play and - Carse, James.pdf#p131]
