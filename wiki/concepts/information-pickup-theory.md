# Information Pickup Theory

## Summary

Information pickup theory proposes that perception occurs through direct detection of information in the ambient energy array, not through processing of sensory inputs. Gibson distinguishes his concept from Shannon's communication theory—the information for perception is not transmitted but simply exists in the environment. The theory requires perceptual systems with active exploration, not passive sensory channels.

## Key facts

- Information, as used in this theory, refers to specification of the observer's environment, not specification of receptors or sense organs—qualities of objects are specified by information, not by sensations. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p263]
- The information in ambient light is inexhaustible—a perceiver can keep noticing facts about the world to the end of life without reaching a limit, unlike Shannon information which has defined channels and signals. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p263]
- Information is not energy-specific—stimuli are not always imposed on a passive subject; in life one obtains stimulation to extract information, and information can be the same despite radical changes in stimulation. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p264]
- Perception involves registering definite dimensions of invariance in the stimulus flux together with definite parameters of disturbance—invariants specify persistence, disturbances specify changes. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p239]
- The process of pickup is hypothesized to be highly susceptible to development and learning—the opportunities for educating attention, exploring, and extracting are unlimited. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p271]

## Inferences

- Inference: The theory rejects cognitive processing of inputs as unnecessary—information does not have to be stored in memory because it is always available in the environment for direct pickup.
- Inference: Gibson argues that the common assumption that cognition must process sensory inputs to yield knowledge is circular—knowledge of the world cannot come from stored knowledge or innate ideas.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: How exactly the visual system extracts invariants while still noticing the flux remains difficult to explicate according to Gibson himself.
