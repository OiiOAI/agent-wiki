# Checklist Design Principles

## Summary

Principles for creating effective checklists, as articulated by Boeing's Dan Boorman, distinguishing good checklists from bad ones.

## Key facts

- Bad checklists are vague and imprecise, too long, hard to use, impractical, made by people with no awareness of deployment situations, and treat users as dumb. [raw/books/strategy/The checklist manifesto _ how to get things right - Atul Gawande.pdf#p89]
- Good checklists are precise, efficient, to the point, easy to use even in difficult situations, and provide reminders of only the most critical steps—the ones that even highly skilled professionals could miss. [raw/books/strategy/The checklist manifesto _ how to get things right - Atul Gawande.pdf#p89]
- The power of checklists is limited: they can help experts remember complex processes, clarify priorities, and prompt teamwork, but cannot by themselves make anyone follow them. [raw/books/strategy/The checklist manifesto _ how to get things right - Atul Gawande.pdf#p89]
- After about sixty to ninety seconds at a given pause point, checklists often become a distraction; steps get missed as people start shortcutting. [raw/books/strategy/The checklist manifesto _ how to get things right - Atul Gawande.pdf#p91]
- Checklists should focus on killer items—the steps that are most dangerous to skip and most often overlooked, typically keeping to five to nine items. [raw/books/strategy/The checklist manifesto _ how to get things right - Atul Gawande.pdf#p91]
- No matter how carefully designed, a checklist must be tested in the real world; first drafts always fall apart and need iterative refinement. [raw/books/strategy/The checklist manifesto _ how to get things right - Atul Gawande.pdf#p92]

## Inferences

- Inference: The emphasis on testing and iteration suggests that checklist design is an empirical process rather than a purely rational one—the real world reveals problems that theoretical design cannot anticipate.
