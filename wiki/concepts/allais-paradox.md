# Allais Paradox

## Summary

The Allais paradox (1953) provides systematic violations of the substitution axiom of Expected Utility theory. The common ratio effect (choices 1-2) and common consequence effect (choices 3-4) both demonstrate that people are more sensitive to probability differences near certainty and impossibility than in the intermediate range.

## Key facts

- The common ratio effect shows most people choose $3000 for sure over 80% chance of $4000 (Decision 1), but 20% chance of $4000 over 25% chance of $3000 (Decision 2), violating the substitution axiom. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p141]
- The common consequence effect shows most people choose $2400 for sure over 33% chance of $2500 with 66% chance of $2400 (Decision 3), but prefer 33% chance of $2500 over 34% chance of $2400 (Decision 4), violating the sure-thing principle. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p141]
- Both effects resonate with the notion that people are more sensitive to differences in probability near impossibility and certainty than in the intermediate range. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p141]

## Inferences

- Inference: The Allais paradox was a major motivation for developing Prospect Theory as a descriptive alternative to Expected Utility theory.
