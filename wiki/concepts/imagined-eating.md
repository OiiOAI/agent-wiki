# Imagined Eating

## Summary

Imagined eating refers to the phenomenon whereby imagining the consumption of food can produce satiety and reduce actual consumption. Research demonstrates that mental simulation of eating engages similar psychological and physiological mechanisms as actual consumption, with potential applications for weight management and understanding mind-body effects.

## Key facts

- In a Carey Morewedge study, people who imagined eating cheese many times consumed less when offered actual cheese compared to those who imagined eating fewer times. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p115]
- Holocaust survivors in concentration camps engaged in elaborate discussions about food and recipes, and researchers noted that women seemed to feel satiated by recalling tastes of special foods. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p116]
- Women in concentration camps wrote cookbooks as a testament to how talking about food and sharing recipes helped them survive and affirm they had a future. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p116]
- Early psychologist Mary Cheves West Perky found in 1910 that participants could not distinguish between real and imagined images when told to imagine objects like bananas or tomatoes. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p115]

## Inferences

- Inference: The brain may not be wired to differentiate between real and imagined experiences, since beliefs influence perceptions constantly and context changes what we see.
