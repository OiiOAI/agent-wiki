# Causation in Neuroscience

## Summary

Chapter 2 of the book examines the challenges of applying causal reasoning to neuroscience. The author discusses how correlations are often mistaken for causation, and how in self-organized systems with feedback loops, events are not caused but emerge from interactions. The chapter addresses issues of probabilistic causation, mutual causation, and self-causation in brain networks.

## Key facts

- In neuroscience, the most common practice is testing hypotheses by models of associations between observed properties, using correlations to infer causation. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain from Inside Out - György Buzsáki MD, PhD.pdf#p34]
- Interpreting correlations is a two-step process: first describing facts (the correlation), followed by an assumption of cause-and-effect relationship from a symmetric statistical relationship. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain from Inside Out - György Buzsáki MD, PhD.pdf#p50]
- In complex interconnected systems like the brain with amplifying-damping feedback loops and emerging properties, cause and effect can be circular or multidirectional. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain from Inside Out - György Buzsáki MD, PhD.pdf#p47]
- Events in the brain are often not caused but are 'released' or 'permitted', as illustrated by sleep spindles that can be induced by stimuli but most often occur because decreased neuromodulator activity does not prevent them. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain from Inside Out - György Buzsáki MD, PhD.pdf#p47]
- Statistical correlations are similar to associations; in neither case can one rely on causal grounding, which requires active manipulations. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain from Inside Out - György Buzsáki MD, PhD.pdf#p49]
- In a densely interconnected dynamical system like the brain, even minor perturbations may induce unexpected activity in downstream and distant structures. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain from Inside Out - György Buzsáki MD, PhD.pdf#p48]

## Inferences

- Inference: The difficulty of applying causation as the sole tool for inference suggests that correlational and perturbation methods should be combined whenever possible.
