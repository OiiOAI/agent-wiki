# Three Psychological Laws

## Summary

Rawls proposes three psychological laws (or tendencies) that describe how moral sentiments develop through the stages of moral development. These laws refer to institutional settings as being just and publicly known to be such, showing how moral notions enter into the explanation of moral development.

## Key facts

- First law: given that family institutions are just, and that the parents love the child and manifestly express their love by caring for his good, then the child, recognizing their evident love of him, comes to love them. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p435]
- Second law: given that a person's capacity for fellow feeling has been realized by acquiring attachments in accordance with the first law, and given that a social arrangement is just and publicly known by all to be just, then this person develops ties of friendly feeling and trust toward others in the association as they with evident intention comply with their duties and obligations. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p435]
- Third law: given that a person's capacity for fellow feeling has been realized by his forming attachments in accordance with the first two laws, and given that a society's institutions are just and are publicly known by all to be just, then this person acquires the corresponding sense of justice as he recognizes that he and those for whom he cares are the beneficiaries of these arrangements. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p435]
- The three laws are not merely principles of association or of reinforcement; they assert that the active sentiments of love and friendship arise from the manifest intention of other persons to act for our good. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p438]

## Inferences

- Inference: The basic idea underlying the three laws is one of reciprocity, a tendency to answer in kind, which is a deep psychological fact without which fruitful social cooperation would be fragile or impossible.
