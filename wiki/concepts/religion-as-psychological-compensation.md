# Religion as Psychological Compensation

## Summary

A concept in Freud's analysis of religion wherein religious beliefs serve as psychological compensation for human helplessness before natural forces and fate. The gods function to exorcise terrors of nature, reconcile humans to the cruelties of fate (especially death), and compensate for the privations imposed by civilized life. The father figure in religious belief represents a projection of the primal father and satisfies the longing for protection that originated in childhood.

## Key facts

- The gods have a threefold task: to exorcise the terrors of nature, to reconcile men to the cruelty of fate, particularly as shown in death, and to compensate them for the sufferings imposed by civilized life. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p6558]
- When man treats the forces of nature as he treats persons, giving them the character of a father, he turns them into gods, following an infantile prototype. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p6558]
- The longing for a father is identical to the need for protection against human weakness. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p6564]

## Inferences

- Inference: Religious belief systems are psychologically intelligible as attempts to restore the protective relationship of childhood with one's father on a cosmic scale.
