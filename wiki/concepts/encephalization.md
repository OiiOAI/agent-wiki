# Encephalization

## Summary

Encephalization refers to the degree to which an animal's actual brain size exceeds what would be predicted for a typical animal of its body size. The concept has been central to theories linking brain size to intelligence, but Deacon argues it is often misinterpreted because both brain and body size can change independently, and the same encephalization value can result from different developmental processes with different functional implications.

## Key facts

- Encephalization is typically assessed by comparing brain to body size ratios against predicted values from allometric scaling relationships. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p163]
- Primates have nearly twice as much brain for their body size as other typical mammals, and humans have almost three times more than typical primates. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p181]
- The chihuahua demonstrates that small dogs appear hyper-encephalized and large dogs hypo-encephalized, yet this variation does not reflect differences in cognitive ability. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p177]
- Primate encephalization is primarily a consequence of reduced body growth during fetal development, not increased brain growth rate. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p183]
- Human encephalization differs qualitatively from both chihuahua dwarfism and the general primate shift, involving disproportionate expansion of dorsal forebrain structures. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p185]

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Whether encephalization correlates with intelligence depends critically on how it is achieved developmentally, suggesting 'equivalent encephalization at different scales is not a reliable index of equivalent cognitive capacity.'
