# The Devil as Father Substitute

## Summary

Freud argued that in the demonological neurosis of Christoph Haizmann, the Devil functions as a direct substitute for the father lost to death. Haizmann's pact was signed not for wealth, magical powers, or sexual enjoyment—all of which the Devil offered and Haizmann rejected—but for the promise of 'help and support' during his melancholic depression following his father's death. The Devil first appeared as an 'honest elderly citizen' with a beard and walking stick, bearing physical resemblance to a paternal figure. Freud connected this to the broader psychoanalytic finding that God and the Devil share a common prototype in the individual father, reflecting the ambivalent (both loving and hostile) relation of the child to the father, as elaborated in Totem and Taboo.

## Key facts

- Freud interpreted Haizmann's pact with the Devil as motivated by longing for a father substitute following the death of his father, not by ordinary worldly desires. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p5963]
- The Devil first appeared to Haizmann in the form of an 'honest elderly citizen' with a brown beard, red cloak, black hat, and a black dog—visually resembling a paternal figure rather than a terrifying demon. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p5967]
- Freud argued that God and the Devil share a common prototype in the individual father, reflecting the ambivalence (simultaneous love and hostility) inherent in the child's relation to the father. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p5968]
- Freud cited Ernest Jones's work connecting witch trials to repressed ambivalence toward the father, noting that the Devil as father duplicate had not been shown as clearly elsewhere as in Haizmann's case. ## Inferences

- Inference: Freud's analysis of the Devil as father substitute drew on Totem and Taboo (1912–13), which argued that both God and the Devil derive from the primal father of prehistoric humanity.
