# Heat-of-Passion Defense

## Summary

The heat-of-passion defense is a legal argument that a defendant acted in the heat of sudden anger or excitement, which supposedly reduces criminal responsibility from first-degree murder to second-degree murder. This defense is based on classical view assumptions that anger has a universal biological fingerprint (flushed face, clenched jaw, increased heart rate) and that emotions cause people to lose control of their rational minds. Barrett argues these assumptions are myths unsupported by neuroscience.

## Key facts

- The heat-of-passion defense assumes anger is one universal type with a specific fingerprint including flushed face, clenched jaw, flared nostrils, and increased heart rate, blood pressure, and perspiration. [raw/books/psychology/How Emotions Are Made_ The Secret Life of - Lisa Feldman Barrett.pdf#p244]
- The alleged fingerprint for anger is merely a Western cultural stereotype not supported by data; heart rates go up in anger but with tremendous variation. [raw/books/psychology/How Emotions Are Made_ The Secret Life of - Lisa Feldman Barrett.pdf#p244]
- In twenty years of creating anger in Barrett's lab, no test subject has ever killed anyone; the repertoire includes swearing, threatening, pounding tables, leaving rooms, crying, and resolving conflicts. [raw/books/psychology/How Emotions Are Made_ The Secret Life of - Lisa Feldman Barrett.pdf#p244]
- The case of Gordon Patterson, who shot John Northrup after catching his wife 'in a state of semiundress' with him, illustrates the heat-of-passion defense resulting in second-degree rather than first-degree murder. [raw/books/psychology/How Emotions Are Made_ The Secret Life of - Lisa Feldman Barrett.pdf#p243]
- Rational killing is considered worse than emotional killing in the U.S. legal system, all other circumstances being equal. [raw/books/psychology/How Emotions Are Made_ The Secret Life of - Lisa Feldman Barrett.pdf#p243]

## Inferences

- Inference: The heat-of-passion defense perpetuates the false dichotomy between reason and emotion, treating emotional actions as less culpable despite neuroscientific evidence that all actions involve both.
