# Two-stage model of choice

## Summary

The two-stage model proposes that primate decision making consists of two dissociable stages: (1) a valuation system that learns and represents the values of objects and actions, and (2) a choice system that uses these values to select from a current choice set. The valuation system is associated with learning and representing values, operating even passively without choice. The choice system transforms the values of available options into a stochastic plan of action. Critically, valuation must be at least partially autonomous of choice — choice must be complete before actions are planned and executed — and this architecture has been validated by a wealth of neuroscientific data. The model predicts that neural activity in valuation areas should be linearly related to value even during passive tasks, while choice areas should show competitive dynamics between options.

## Key facts

- The two-stage model consists of a valuation system that learns and represents the values of objects and actions, and a choice system that transforms option values into stochastic action plans. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p504]
- Evidence for dissociable valuation comes from Platt and Glimcher (1999), who found that posterior parietal neurons encoded the mean expected utility of targets linearly in firing rates, independent of choice on that trial. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p505]
- Delgado et al. (2000) and Knutson et al. (2000) found that during passive viewing of gains and losses, striatal and fronto-cortical activation was linearly correlated with reward magnitudes, suggesting valuation operates without choice. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p505]
- The anatomical boundaries between valuation and choice mechanisms are not entirely discrete; choice structures influence valuation structures and vice versa. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p505]
- The valuation system involves the ventral striatum and medial prefrontal cortex; the choice system involves large portions of the parietal cortex and interacts with frontal and striatal areas. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p506]

## Inferences

- Inference: The striking linearity between neural activation and subjective value across diverse conditions (risky, delayed, certain rewards) suggests the valuation system is more unitary than would be expected from a highly modular architecture.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Whether orbitofrontal cortex and dorsal striatum neurons participate directly in the choice process (action selection) or only in valuation remains unknown.
