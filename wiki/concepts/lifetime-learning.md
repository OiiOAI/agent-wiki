# Lifetime Learning

## Summary

Munger views the acquisition of wisdom as a moral duty requiring lifelong learning. He notes that people who are learning machines—who go to bed a little wiser than they were in the morning—consistently rise in life even when not the smartest or most diligent. Berkshire Hathaway's success required continuous learning; Warren Buffett spends about half his waking time reading.

## Key facts

- Munger states: 'The acquisition of wisdom is a moral duty. It's not something you do just to advance in life.'. [raw/books/strategy/Poor Charlie's Almanack_ The Wit and Wisdo - Charles T. Munger, Peter D. Kaufman, Ed We.pdf#p432]
- Munger observes: 'I constantly see people rise in life who are not the smartest, sometimes not even the most diligent. But they are learning machines. They go to bed every night a little wiser than they were that morning.'. [raw/books/strategy/Poor Charlie's Almanack_ The Wit and Wisdo - Charles T. Munger, Peter D. Kaufman, Ed We.pdf#p433]
- Warren Buffett spends about half his waking time reading, with the rest spent in one-on-one conversations with highly gifted people he and they trust. [raw/books/strategy/Poor Charlie's Almanack_ The Wit and Wisdo - Charles T. Munger, Peter D. Kaufman, Ed We.pdf#p434]
- Munger describes Dr. Joseph M. Mirra, who made himself know more about bone tumor pathology than almost anyone else, then spent a sabbatical working seventeen hours a day for a year to create one of the two great bone tumor pathology textbooks of the world. [raw/books/strategy/Poor Charlie's Almanack_ The Wit and Wisdo - Charles T. Munger, Peter D. Kaufman, Ed We.pdf#p434]

## Inferences

- Inference: Munger distinguishes between cramming for tests (passing but not retained) versus learning in ways that create a mental latticework for automatic lifelong use—only the latter represents true education.
