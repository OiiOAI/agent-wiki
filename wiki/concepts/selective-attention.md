# Selective Attention

## Summary

Selective attention is a powerful factor in perception, action, and memory that acts as a filter selecting some objects for further processing while ignoring others. Kandel describes two types: involuntary attention (supported by automatic neural processes, particularly evident in implicit memory) and voluntary attention (a specific feature of explicit memory arising from internal needs). Attention stabilizes spatial maps; when mice are forced to pay attention to a new environment, spatial maps remain stable for days.

## Key facts

- William James noted in 1890: 'Everyone knows what attention is. It is the taking possession by the mind, in clear and vivid form, of one out of what seem several simultaneously possible objects or trains of thought.'. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p322]
- Even ambient attention is sufficient to allow a spatial map to form and become stable for a few hours, but such a map becomes unstable after three to six hours. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p322]
- Long-term stability of spatial maps correlates strongly and systematically with the degree to which an animal is required to pay specific attention to its environment. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p322]
- Blocking dopamine action in the hippocampus blocked the stabilization of the spatial map in animals paying attention; conversely, activating dopamine receptors stabilized the spatial map of animals not paying attention. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p323]
- In implicit memory storage, the attentional signal is recruited involuntarily from the bottom up; in spatial memory, dopamine is recruited voluntarily from the top down. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p324]
