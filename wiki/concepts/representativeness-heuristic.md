# Representativeness Heuristic

## Summary

The representativeness heuristic causes people to judge the probability that an object belongs to a category by how much it resembles (is representative of) the typical member of that category, rather than by statistical base rates. In the 'Tom W.' problem, participants predicted Tom W. was most likely a computer science graduate based on a personality description, ignoring that computer science is a small field with a low base rate. Representativeness leads to violations of Bayesian logic, neglect of base rates, insensitivity to sample size, and the belief that chance is self-correcting. It underlies stereotyping and intuitive predictions that ignore statistical base information.

## Key facts

- In the Tom W. problem, 114 psychology PhD students ignored base rates and ordered fields by similarity to Tom W.'s description, placing computer science first and humanities last, despite knowing the base rate statistics. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p195]
- Robyn Dawes, a statistician, instantly predicted 'computer expert' for Tom W., only realizing his error when Kahneman mentioned 'base rates'—demonstrating that even experts use representativeness automatically. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p195]
- When asked to list 12 assertive behaviors, people who frowned (not smiling) judged themselves less assertive than people who smiled, because cognitive effort (not ease) was used as a cue, showing that representativeness judgments can be manipulated. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p175]
- Statistical base rates are often neglected when specific case information is available, but causal base rates (those implying mechanisms) are easily integrated into judgments, as seen in the taxi problem where '85% of accidents involve Green taxis' caused appropriate weight to be given to base rates. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p219]
- Nisbett and Borgida found that students who learned statistical results of the bystander effect did not update their predictions about individuals, but were convinced by vivid individual cases—demonstrating that people readily generalize from cases but not from statistics. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p222]

## Inferences

- Inference: The representativeness heuristic is deeply tied to WYSIATI: System 1 builds a coherent narrative from available information and mistakes that coherence for probability. System 2 often accepts this substitution without applying statistical corrections.
