# Herd Instinct

## Summary

Freud critically examines Trotter's concept of the herd instinct, arguing that it is not irreducible or primary but rather derived from other psychological factors. Freud proposes that the herd instinct (group feeling) arises from the reversal of initial hostility into identification, motivated by the impossibility of maintaining exclusive access to parental love in sibling rivalries.

## Key facts

- Trotter derives the mental phenomena of groups from a herd instinct which is innate in human beings. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p5692]
- Freud argues that social feeling is based upon the reversal of what was first a hostile feeling into a positively toned tie in the nature of an identification. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p5695]
- Freud corrects Trotter's pronouncement, asserting that man is 'a horde animal, an individual creature in a horde led by a chief.'. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p5695]
- Group feeling grows up out of children's relation to their parents as a reaction to the initial envy with which the elder child receives the younger one. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p5693]

## Inferences

- Inference: Freud's critique of Trotter's herd instinct theory leads him to prefer the concept of 'horde animal' over 'herd animal.'
