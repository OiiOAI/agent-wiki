# Predatory attack

## Summary

Predatory attack (also called quiet-biting attack) is a form of aggression distinct from affective attack, characterized by quiet, efficient hunting behavior directed at prey. It is typically evoked by electrical stimulation of the dorsolateral hypothalamus. Unlike affective attack, it does not involve emotional rage responses, supports self-stimulation, and is not directed at conspecifics. The predatory attack system is thought to emerge from the SEEKING system and shares neurochemical and functional properties with lateral hypothalamic self-stimulation circuits.

## Key facts

- Quiet-biting attack is typically evoked during ESB of the dorsolateral hypothalamus, while affective attack sites are more concentrated in the ventrolateral and medial hypothalamus. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p106]
- Quiet-biting attack and self-stimulation are facilitated by antianxiety agents, and both behaviors are reduced by dopamine-blocking agents. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p106]
- Rats exhibiting quiet-biting attack will, in addition to attacking live prey, also bite dead mice, while stimulation of affective attack sites does not support this behavior. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p106]
- When confronted by conspecifics, no attack is generated from quiet-biting attack sites, while intense attack is still evoked from affective attack sites. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p106]
- The most effective quiet-biting attack electrodes in rats always evoke self-stimulation. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p106]
- Predatory attack and quiet-biting attack are thought to emerge from a homologous brain system related to the SEEKING system, with species-typical expressions leading to foraging in some species and predatory stalking in others. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p106]
- Minor tranquilizers such as chlordiazepoxide (Librium) reduce affective attack and increase quiet-biting attack. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p106]
- Stimulation of the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis facilitates affective attack while suppressing quiet-biting attack. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p106]

## Inferences

- Inference: The commonality between quiet-biting attack and self-stimulation suggests that predatory attack may be a foraging-related behavior that has been co-opted for hunting, rather than a distinct aggression system.
- Inference: The pharmacological similarities between quiet-biting attack and self-stimulation suggest they share underlying neurochemical mechanisms.
