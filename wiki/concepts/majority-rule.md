# Majority Rule

## Summary

Majority rule has a subordinate place as a procedural device justified by the political ends the constitution is designed to achieve. Rawls distinguishes between the ideal legislative procedure and the actual procedure, arguing that even ideal legislatures are imperfect procedures with no guarantee of leading to just legislation. The intensity of desire or conviction is irrelevant when questions of justice arise.

## Key facts

- The procedure of majority rule has a subordinate place as a procedural device justified by political ends defined by the principles of justice. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p322]
- There is nothing to the view that what the majority wills is right; the outcome of voting is subject to political principles. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p323]
- Even the ideal legislature is an imperfect procedure; there seems to be no way to characterize a feasible procedure guaranteed to lead to just legislation. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p325]
- In the ideal legislative procedure, no special weight is given to opinions held with greater confidence. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p326]
- The intensity of desire or the strength of conviction is irrelevant when questions of justice arise. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p327]
