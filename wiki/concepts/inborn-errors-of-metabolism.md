# Inborn Errors of Metabolism

## Summary

Inborn errors of metabolism are genetic conditions involving defective enzymes that result in individuals needing larger-than-normal amounts of specific nutrient cofactors for those enzymes to function. In 2002, biochemist Bruce Ames identified fifty human genetic mutations causing elevated requirements for vitamin cofactors, each associated with a known physical ailment correctable by high-dose supplementation. The authors hypothesize that some psychiatric symptoms may represent the brain-specific manifestation of these inherited metabolic inefficiencies, particularly given the brain's high metabolic demands. This would explain why some individuals and families (such as Autumn Stringam's family with multiple generations of mental illness) respond dramatically to broad-spectrum multinutrient supplementation despite having normal baseline diets.

## Key facts

- In 2002, Bruce Ames identified fifty human genetic mutations causing excessively high needs for vitamin cofactors, each resulting in a known physical ailment correctable by additional vitamin supplementation. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Better Brain - Bonnie J. Kaplan.pdf#p67]
- Linus Pauling predicted in 1968 that what is inherited in mental illness are genes that regulate brain metabolism of essential nutrients, suggesting that some people may need higher-than-average micronutrient intake for optimal brain function. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Better Brain - Bonnie J. Kaplan.pdf#p30]
- Autumn Stringam's family showed a three-generation pattern of approximately 50% prevalence of mental health issues (5 of 10 in her generation, 4 of 8 in her mother's, 4 of 9 in her grandfather's), yet all improved to normal mental health on multinutrient supplementation, illustrating a genetic predisposition potentially correctable by micronutrients. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Better Brain - Bonnie J. Kaplan.pdf#p57]
- Julia's ADHD research found that MTHFR gene variants were not more prevalent in children with ADHD compared to the general population, and their presence or absence did not predict response to multinutrients, suggesting single-gene explanations for mental disorders are insufficient. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Better Brain - Bonnie J. Kaplan.pdf#p68]

## Inferences

- Inference: The three-generation family data and Bruce Ames's research together suggest that many mental health conditions may have a metabolic component—specifically, genetically influenced micronutrient requirements—that broad-spectrum supplementation addresses. This would explain why the same nutritional intervention helps diverse psychiatric presentations.
