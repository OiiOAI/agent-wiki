# Obsessive Thinking

## Summary

Obsessive thinking (German: Zwangsdenken) is Freud's proposed alternative term for what is commonly called 'obsessional ideas'. Freud argues that obsessional structures can correspond to every sort of psychical act—wishes, temptations, impulsions, reflections, doubts, commands or prohibitions—not merely ideas in the narrow sense. Patients typically try to reduce these heterogeneous psychical structures to the level of mere 'obsessional ideas' by removing their affective index. Obsessive thinking is characterized by excessive energy expenditure normally reserved for actions, representing a regression from acting to thinking.

- A thought process is obsessive when, due to inhibition at the motor end of the psychical system, it is undertaken with an expenditure of energy normally reserved for actions. ## Inferences

- Inference: Obsessive thinking represents a regression from action to thought, where the energy dammed up in inhibited intentions seeks discharge through mental rather than motor channels.
- Inference: The displacement mechanism allows substitutive acts to become preparatory acts, and thinking replaces acting in obsessional neurosis.
