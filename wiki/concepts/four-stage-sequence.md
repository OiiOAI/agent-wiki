# Four-Stage Sequence

## Summary

The Four-Stage Sequence is a methodological device in Rawls's theory that elaborates the original position to clarify how principles of justice are applied to institutions. It proceeds through: (1) the original position where principles are chosen, (2) a constitutional convention where a just constitution is designed, (3) the legislative stage where laws and policies are assessed, and (4) the application of rules to particular cases. Each stage progressively lifts the veil of ignorance as appropriate for the decisions made at that stage.

## Key facts

- In the constitutional convention, delegates know the general facts about their society (level of economic advance, political culture) but not particular facts about themselves. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p184]
- The first principle of equal liberty is the primary standard for the constitutional convention, while the second principle comes into play at the legislative stage. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p186]
- The four-stage sequence is a device for applying principles of justice, not an account of how constitutional conventions and legislatures actually proceed. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p187]
- In the original position, only particular facts inferable from the circumstances of justice are known; in later stages, general facts about society become available but particular facts about individuals remain excluded.
