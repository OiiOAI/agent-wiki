# Superorganism

## Summary

An organism composed of smaller organisms that function as a unit for reproduction and survival. The concept emerged from studying 'major transitions' in evolution—moments when previously competing units began cooperating and dividing labor, creating new levels of biological organization. Examples include: prokaryotic cells joining to form eukaryotes, cells forming multicellular organisms, and eusocial insects forming colonies. Human societies represent another major transition, where individuals cooperate sufficiently to function as superorganisms capable of building cities, raising armies, and transforming ecosystems.

## Key facts

- Major transitions occur when ways are found to suppress free riding so individual units can cooperate, work as a team, and divide labor. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p221]
- Eusocial insects (bees, ants, termites) represent just 2% of insect species but claim the best feeding and breeding sites and now constitute the majority of insect biomass. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p222]
- Humans are the only ultrasocial primate, having crossed the threshold that enabled large groups with division of labor. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p224]
- The three factors enabling ultrasociality in both wasps and humans were: defensible nests, extended parental care, and intergroup conflict. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p224]

## Inferences

- Inference: Human civilizations and beehives may be analogous products of major transitions, both representing solutions to the free rider problem that enabled cooperation at scales previously impossible.
