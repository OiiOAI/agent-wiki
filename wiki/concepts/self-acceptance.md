# Self-Acceptance

## Summary

Self-acceptance is distinguished from self-affirmation. Where self-affirmation involves telling oneself 'I can do it' even when something is beyond ability (a form of self-deception), self-acceptance means accepting 'one's incapable self' as is and moving forward to do whatever one can. It involves accepting what is irreplaceable, accepting 'this me' just as it is, and having the courage to change what one can change. This concept is linked to affirmative resignation—ascertaining what one can and cannot change.

## Key facts

- Self-acceptance means if one cannot do something, one is simply accepting 'one's incapable self' as is and moving forward so that one can do whatever one can. [raw/books/psychology/The courage to be disliked_ the Japanese phenomenon that - Ichiro Kishimi, and Fumitake Koga.pdf#p232]
- Self-affirmation is a notion that can bring about a superiority complex and may be termed a way of living in which one lies to oneself. [raw/books/psychology/The courage to be disliked_ the Japanese phenomenon that - Ichiro Kishimi, and Fumitake Koga.pdf#p232]
- Affirmative resignation is ascertaining the things one can change and the things one cannot change, and focusing on what one can change. [raw/books/psychology/The courage to be disliked_ the Japanese phenomenon that - Ichiro Kishimi, and Fumitake Koga.pdf#p233]
- Courage is the essence of self-acceptance—'We do not lack ability. We just lack courage.'. [raw/books/psychology/The courage to be disliked_ the Japanese phenomenon that - Ichiro Kishimi, and Fumitake Koga.pdf#p233]
