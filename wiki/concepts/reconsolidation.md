# Reconsolidation

## Summary

Reconsolidation is the memory-updating process by which retrieved memories become labile and can be modified by new information before being stored again. This concept, formalized in the late 1990s, explains why memories change every time we retrieve them and has therapeutic potential for treating conditions like PTSD.

## Key facts

- Reconsolidation occurs when memories are retrieved: they become labile, vulnerable to distortion and interference, and can be modified by new information before being restabilized. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p140]
- Memories are changed every time they are retrieved; the process of calling them up and engaging them alters their content, and consolidation occurs anew. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p140]
- Henry had consolidated information before his operation but could not reconsolidate it after, so his childhood memory traces remained unrefreshed. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p139]
- Reconsolidation has therapeutic potential for PTSD: Propranolol can selectively block reconsolidation of emotional content while preserving factual memory. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p143]

## Inferences

- Inference: The reconsolidation hypothesis explains why consolidated memories can be edited and why repeated retrieval strengthens memories.
