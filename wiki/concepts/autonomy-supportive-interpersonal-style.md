# Autonomy-Supportive Interpersonal Style

## Summary

An interpersonal approach common to effective parenting, teaching, managing, and health-care provision, characterized by listening openly to understand situations from others' perspectives rather than imposing control.

## Key facts

- Being a good parent, teacher, manager, or health-care provider all have something in common: an autonomy-supportive interpersonal style. [raw/books/psychology/Why we do what we do _ understanding self - motivation - Edward L_ Deci, Richard Flaste.pdf#p186]
- An autonomy-supportive interpersonal style begins with listening openly so you can understand the situation from the other's perspective. [raw/books/psychology/Why we do what we do _ understanding self - motivation - Edward L_ Deci, Richard Flaste.pdf#p186]
- Medical students whose instructors were autonomy supportive in their teaching approach became more autonomous in their reasons for studying interviewing and developed more positive attitudes about the biopsychosocial approach. [raw/books/psychology/Why we do what we do _ understanding self - motivation - Edward L_ Deci, Richard Flaste.pdf#p185]
- Medical students who became more autonomous in their own motivation and integrated the value of the psychosocial approach were more 'patient-centered' than those who had not. [raw/books/psychology/Why we do what we do _ understanding self - motivation - Edward L_ Deci, Richard Flaste.pdf#p186]

## Inferences

- Inference: The finding that an autonomy-supportive teaching style led students to adopt a more autonomy-supportive style of interacting with patients suggests that autonomy support can be transmitted through modeling and institutional culture.
- Inference: When health-care providers are autonomy supportive rather than controlling, patients are more likely to internalize health-related values and behave in healthier ways.
