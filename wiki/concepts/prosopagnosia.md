# Prosopagnosia

## Summary

Prosopagnosia is the inability to recognise faces, following right-hemisphere lesions. It reflects the right hemisphere's crucial ability to see the 'configurational' aspects of the whole face, as opposed to the left hemisphere's laborious process of trying to assemble the face from parts.

## Key facts

- An inability to recognise faces is called prosopagnosia, and follows right-hemisphere lesions. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p93]
- In prosopagnosia, the right hemisphere's capacity to see the 'configurational' aspects of the whole is lacking; in its absence the left hemisphere has to rely on the laborious process of trying to put the face together from the parts. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p94]
- A patient reported that faces seemed to him 'strangely flat, white with emphatic dark eyes, as if made from a flat surface, like white oval plates, all alike'. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p94]
- Prosopagnosia was linked to right-hemisphere lesions by Sergent and Villemure, who recognised an 'inability to combine the component features into a configurational facial representation that would uniquely define each face'. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p94]
- There is a specific defect for recognition of eyes in prosopagnosia; subjects with partial prosopagnosia following right-hemisphere lesion could glean evidence from the mouth area but were quite unable to use information from the eyes. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p95]
- Wigan achieved recognition of prosopagnosia in 1844, but it took another hundred years for it to be given a name; in 1947 Joachim Bodamer described the condition. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p94]
