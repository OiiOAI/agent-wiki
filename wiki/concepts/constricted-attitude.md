# Constricted Attitude

## Summary

A constricted (negative) attitude narrows a person's experience of the world to reduce uncertainty and control the unexpected. Originating in early childhood from lack of comfort and support, these attitudes create self-sabotaging dynamics where people unconsciously provoke the negative responses they fear. Greene identifies five common forms: Hostile, Anxious, Avoidant, Depressive, and Resentful.

## Key facts

- People with constricted attitudes unconsciously want to limit what they see and experience to gain more control, anticipating bad things and focusing on neutralizing them. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p269]
- The Hostile Attitude: people interpret early experiences as hostile and respond by becoming hostile themselves, provoking anger in others to justify their original attitude. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p270]
- The Anxious Attitude: people anticipate obstacles and criticism, limit their world to reduce unpredictability, and often disguise control needs as love or concern. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p272]
- The Avoidant Attitude: people avoid responsibility and challenges where self-esteem might be at stake, constantly seeking escape routes through illness, addiction, or lateness. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p273]
- The Depressive Attitude: people who felt unloved as children internalize negative judgment, feeling worthless and drawn to gloom, often sabotaging success because they don't feel they deserve it. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p274-275]
- The Resentful Attitude: people who never felt they received enough attention scan for disrespect, incubate hurts internally, and eventually seek revenge through passive aggression or sabotage. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p277]

## Inferences

- Inference: These attitudes often combine and reinforce each other—anger breeds suspicion, which deepens insecurity, creating viscous cycles that are difficult to break without conscious intervention.
