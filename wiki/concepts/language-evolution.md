# Language Evolution

## Summary

The study of how human language capabilities evolved, involving theories about gestural vs. vocal origins, the role of mirror neurons, genetic factors like FOXP2, and anatomical changes enabling speech production. Key questions include whether language evolved from manual gestures, vocalizations, or both, and what neural substrates enabled this uniquely human capacity.

## Key facts

- Robert Provine proposes that bipedalism made speech mechanically possible by breaking the link between breathing patterns and stride. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p52]
- Unique human anatomy - elongated necks, dropped tongues and pharynx - allows the huge variations in sound necessary for speech, despite increased choking risk. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p53]
- Rizzolatti and Arbib propose that the mirror neuron system was fundamental for developing speech and other forms of intentional communication before vocal language evolved. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p71]
- Vervet monkeys have different alarm calls for different predators (snakes, leopards, birds), demonstrating semantic communication in nonhuman animals. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p69]

## Inferences

- Inference: Nonhuman primates may recognize the behavioral effects of their calls but do not seem to make calls with the intention of informing others, suggesting a gap between communication and true linguistic intent

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The relative importance of gestural vs. vocal origins of language remains debated, with Corballis championing gestures and others arguing for vocal origins or combined systems
