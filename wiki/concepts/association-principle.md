# Association Principle

## Summary

The association principle states that people tend to dislike those connected to unpleasant information and like those connected to pleasant information, even when the person had no causal role in the events. This principle is extensively exploited by advertisers and compliance professionals who connect their products or themselves with positive stimuli.

## Key facts

- Research shows men who viewed car ads with attractive models rated the cars as faster, more appealing, more expensive-looking, and better designed than those viewing the same ad without the model. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p174]
- Consumers refused to believe the presence of the model influenced their judgments, demonstrating unconscious operation of the association principle. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p174]
- Gregory Razran's "luncheon technique" demonstrated that people become fonder of things experienced while eating, with subjects unable to remember which statements were seen during food service. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p176]
- The connection between a product and a celebrity need not be logical—only positive—to be effective. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p175]

## Inferences

- Inference: The association principle explains why advertisers use attractive models, why politicians surround themselves with valued symbols, and why weathermen are blamed for bad weather—mere association produces real liking or disliking.
