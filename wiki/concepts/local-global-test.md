# Local-Global Test

## Summary

The local-global test is an EEG-based method for detecting consciousness developed by Dehaene and colleagues. It presents repeated sequences of sounds (e.g., beep beep beep beep boop) until the brain habituates to the local deviant. A global deviant (five identical beeps breaking the learned pattern) then triggers a P3 wave indexing conscious access. The test distinguishes unconscious novelty detection (mismatch negativity) from conscious awareness, and has successfully detected residual consciousness in some vegetative-state patients.

## Key facts

- The local-global test presents sequences of sounds and measures whether the brain detects violations of learned patterns at a conscious level. [raw/books/neuroscience/Consciousness and the Brain_ Deciphering H - Stanislas Dehaene.pdf#p269]
- Early mismatch negativity (MMN) response to local deviant sounds is automatic and unconscious, persisting even in coma and deep sleep. [raw/books/neuroscience/Consciousness and the Brain_ Deciphering H - Stanislas Dehaene.pdf#p269]
- A late P3 wave to global deviants indexes conscious awareness because it disappears when subjects are distracted or asleep. [raw/books/neuroscience/Consciousness and the Brain_ Deciphering H - Stanislas Dehaene.pdf#p271]
- The test activates the global workspace network including bilateral prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate, parietal, and occipital areas. [raw/books/neuroscience/Consciousness and the Brain_ Deciphering H - Stanislas Dehaene.pdf#p271]
- In clinical trials, the test correctly identified 3 out of 4 minimally conscious patients as conscious, and found rare responsive vegetative patients. [raw/books/neuroscience/Consciousness and the Brain_ Deciphering H - Stanislas Dehaene.pdf#p272]

## Inferences

- Inference: The test's success confirms that consciousness requires sustained information maintenance and comparison across working memory—the core functions of the global workspace

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The test has false negatives—some patients who later recover consciousness initially fail the test, likely due to attention or language comprehension limitations
