# Keiretsu

## Summary

Keiretsu are Japanese corporate networks characterized by cross-shareholding, interlocking directorates, and long-term business relationships among member firms. They emerged from the dissolution of the prewar zaibatsu after World War II and represent a form of 'alliance capitalism' that differs from both arm's-length market transactions and hierarchical corporate structures.

## Key facts

- Keiretsu are horizontal corporate networks centered around major banks that coordinate activities among member firms. [raw/books/sociology/Trust - Francis Fukuyama.pdf#p422]
- The zaibatsu historically failed to seek or achieve monopoly positions, unlike their keiretsu successors which maintain competitive markets. [raw/books/sociology/Trust - Francis Fukuyama.pdf#p423]
- Inter-keiretsu 'beer wars' demonstrate competitive dynamics between different keiretsu groups. [raw/books/sociology/Trust - Francis Fukuyama.pdf#p424]
- Parent firms in keiretsu networks have used supplier pressure, including requests for fifteen percent price cuts over three years, to maintain control over their supply chains. [raw/books/sociology/Trust - Francis Fukuyama.pdf#p425]
- Cross-shareholding in Japanese keiretsu increased substantially in the 1960s only after the Japanese government agreed to liberalize rules concerning direct foreign investment, suggesting it was a defensive mechanism against foreign takeover rather than a necessity for maintaining network integrity or achieving scale economies. [raw/books/sociology/Trust - Francis Fukuyama.pdf#p454]

## Inferences

- Inference: Keiretsu represent a network form of organization that sits between pure markets and hierarchies, relying on trust-based relationships rather than solely on equity ownership
- Inference: The persistence of core keiretsu relations despite reductions in cross-shareholding during the 1990s recession suggests non-equity mechanisms (shared culture, long-term relationships) maintain network cohesion
- Inference: The emergence of cross-shareholding as a defensive mechanism rather than a network-organization tool suggests that keiretsu structures were partially shaped by geopolitical factors and international pressure rather than purely economic logic.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The exact mechanisms by which cross-shareholding helped prevent foreign takeover is not elaborated in the source.
