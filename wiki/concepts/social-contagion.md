# Social Contagion of Memory

## Summary

Social contagion describes how memory distortions spread through social networks like viruses. Roddy Roediger coined the term after demonstrating that planted collaborators could spread false memories to genuine participants. People are especially susceptible to memory errors from those who dominate conversations, speak first, or are most confident. Fake news exploits our preexisting beliefs and emotional responses, and the repeated exposure effect makes false information feel true over time. However, fact-checking after consuming misinformation can reduce endorsement of false memories.

## Key facts

- Roddy Roediger coined the term social contagion to describe how memory distortions spread like viruses through social networks. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p196]
- In Roediger's experiment, a planted collaborator who deliberately recalled objects never shown influenced true volunteers to also recall those false items. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p196]
- Even when warned that collaborators might have mistakenly recalled items, volunteers were still likely to remember the planted misinformation. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p196]
- People are especially susceptible to inheriting memory errors when they come from friends or trusted sources. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p196]
- In Elizabeth Loftus's fake news experiment, almost half of participants exposed to photoshopped images falsely claimed to remember having seen the fabricated article in the news. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p197]
- Up to a third of respondents inaccurately recalled fake news incidents as stories they had previously read, and many could not tell real stories from fabricated ones. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p197]
- Fake news is easier to digest when it aligns with preexisting beliefs, is emotionally arousing, includes photos, and comes from trusted sources. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p198]
- Fact-checking warnings after reading misinformation made people 25 percent less likely to endorse false headlines. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p199]
