# Simple, Complicated, and Complex Problems

## Summary

Zimmerman and Glouberman distinguished three types of problems: simple problems have recipes and can be repeated reliably; complicated problems require multiple experts and can be broken into components; complex problems are unique and require adaptation even with expertise. Checklists work best on simple problems and can help coordinate complex ones by ensuring communication.

## Key facts

- Simple problems like baking a cake from a mix have a recipe that, once mastered, brings high likelihood of success. [raw/books/strategy/The checklist manifesto _ how to get things right - Atul Gawande.pdf#p42]
- Complicated problems like sending a rocket to the moon require multiple people and specialized expertise but can be broken into components. [raw/books/strategy/The checklist manifesto _ how to get things right - Atul Gawande.pdf#p42]
- Complex problems like raising a child are unique and expertise is valuable but not sufficient; every case may require a different approach. [raw/books/strategy/The checklist manifesto _ how to get things right - Atul Gawande.pdf#p42]
- Central line infections, plane crashes, and drowning rescues are essentially simple problems despite many contributing factors—one key focus can prevent failure. [raw/books/strategy/The checklist manifesto _ how to get things right - Atul Gawande.pdf#p42]
- ICU care is complicated because it requires multiple practitioners orchestrating different combinations of tasks for different conditions. [raw/books/strategy/The checklist manifesto _ how to get things right - Atul Gawande.pdf#p43]

## Inferences

- Inference: Gawande's framework suggests different interventions suit different problem types: checklists for simple problems, protocols for complicated ones, and communication structures for complex ones.
