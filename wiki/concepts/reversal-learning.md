# Reversal Learning

## Summary

Reversal learning is a behavioral paradigm used to study flexible adjustment of reinforcement expectations. In these tasks, two stimuli and/or actions reverse their reinforcement contingencies, requiring subjects to learn new associations. Flexible adjustment during reversal learning can involve either a learning process (independent CS-US pair learning) or a cognitive control mechanism (using rule knowledge that contingencies switch simultaneously).

## Key facts

- Morrison et al. found that appetitive and aversive networks in the OFC and amygdala exhibit different learning rates during reversal learning: OFC positive cells completed learning faster than amygdala positive cells, while amygdala negative cells learned faster than OFC negative cells. [raw/books/neuroscience/Decision Neuroscience _ An Integrative Perspective - Jean - Claude Dreher; Léon Tremblay; Institut de sciences.pdf#p42]
- The faster learning response of the aversive network in the amygdala may reflect evolutionary preservation of a threat-avoidance system. [raw/books/neuroscience/Decision Neuroscience _ An Integrative Perspective - Jean - Claude Dreher; Léon Tremblay; Institut de sciences.pdf#p43]
- Saez et al. showed that monkeys can use cognitive strategies to infer new contingencies: after experiencing one CS switched, they instantly update expectations for the other CS based on rule knowledge. [raw/books/neuroscience/Decision Neuroscience _ An Integrative Perspective - Jean - Claude Dreher; Léon Tremblay; Institut de sciences.pdf#p43]

## Inferences

- Inference: The distinct learning rates across brain areas and valence systems suggest that appetitive and aversive networks use different neural mechanisms for updating value representations during environmental changes.
- Inference: The amygdala-OFC circuit dynamics during reversal learning are more complex than previously appreciated, with directional information flow evolving during learning.
