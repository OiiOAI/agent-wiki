# Japanese Robotics and Adaptability

## Summary

In the 1990s, some Japanese manufacturing companies dismantled their robotic systems because while robots could outperform human workers, they were too expensive to reprogram or redesign when plants needed to retool or change procedures. This illustrates the tradeoff between efficiency and adaptability.

## Key facts

- Japanese plants that relied almost entirely on robots for the 'lights out' factory model found reprogramming costs prohibitively expensive when models needed to change. [raw/books/strategy/The Power of Intuition - Gary Klein, Ph.D.pdf#p220]
- In one factory, workers were given only one day's notice to assemble new models of mobile phones, whereas engineers previously needed several months to reprogram robots for a model change. [raw/books/strategy/The Power of Intuition - Gary Klein, Ph.D.pdf#p220]
- A Toyota plant reduced its reliance on computers and found it could equip an assembly line for a quarter of the cost of a highly automated line, even though both had the same capacity. [raw/books/strategy/The Power of Intuition - Gary Klein, Ph.D.pdf#p221]

## Inferences

- Inference: This example demonstrates that maximum efficiency under stable conditions may come at the cost of flexibility needed when conditions change, supporting the Planning to Adapt philosophy.
