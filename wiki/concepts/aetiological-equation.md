# Aetiological Equation

## Summary

Freud's conceptual framework for understanding the causation of neuroses, distinguishing between preconditions (like heredity), specific causes (like sexual factors), concurrent causes, and precipitating causes. These factors combine in a quantitative equation where any factor can compensate for others in quantity but not quality.

## Key facts

- Preconditions are indispensable factors whose absence prevents the effect but which cannot alone produce it; specific causes are indispensable, found in every case, and sufficient in required quantity. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p136]
- Concurrent causes are not necessarily present and cannot alone produce the effect but operate alongside preconditions and specific causes. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p136]
- The specific cause determines the form of the neurosis; the total load on the nervous system determines whether illness occurs at all. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p123]
- Heredity acts like a multiplier introduced into an electric circuit, increasing the deviation but not determining its direction. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p123]

## Inferences

- Inference: The aetiological equation represents an early formulation of what would later become the concept of 'complemental series' in Freud's Introductory Lectures.
