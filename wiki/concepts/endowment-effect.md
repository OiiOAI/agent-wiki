# Endowment Effect

## Summary

The Endowment Effect is a behavioral economics theory stating that people place significantly higher value on things they own than on identical things they don't own. Michalowicz illustrates this with the example of a luxury car owner who would rather cancel insurance and stop driving than return the car, even when they can't afford it.

## Key facts

- Michalowicz defines the Endowment Effect as 'the theory that states that we place a much higher significance on something we possess than on an identical thing that we don't possess.'. [raw/books/business/Profit First - Mike Michalowicz.pdf#p188]
- The author uses the example of a Porsche owner who, when facing repossession, would rather cancel daughter's ballet, gym membership, and trip to the Cape than return the car, reasoning 'At least it is still mine.'. [raw/books/business/Profit First - Mike Michalowicz.pdf#p189]
- Capuchins exhibited a true endowment effect: when endowed with one of two equally preferred goods, they reliably preferred to keep the endowed good, even when compensated for transaction costs and time. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p91]
- Control conditions revealed the effect was not due to timing effects or transaction costs, as monkeys failed to trade even when compensated. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p91]
- These results provide the first evidence that a non-human species demonstrates a true endowment effect that cannot be explained by timing, inhibition, or transaction-related costs. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p91]
- Richard Thaler observed that Professor R, a wine collector, would not sell a bottle for under $100 but would not pay more than $35 for an identical bottle—a large gap inconsistent with standard economic theory which posits a single valuation. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p378]
- In a controlled experiment, mug owners set average selling prices of $7.12 while buyers set average buying prices of $2.87—roughly a 2.5:1 ratio, comparable to the loss-aversion coefficient in risky choices. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p382]
- The endowment effect does not apply to goods held 'for exchange': routine commercial transactions (selling shoes, exchanging money) show no loss aversion because both money and goods are treated as intermediaries. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p379]
- Experienced traders in baseball-card conventions and experienced market participants show reduced or absent endowment effects, as they have learned to evaluate items in terms of opportunity cost rather than acquisition context. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p384]
- The poor live below their reference point and treat all expenditures as loss reductions rather than gains, making them psychologically equivalent to sellers in the endowment effect paradigm. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p386]

## Inferences

- Inference: The endowment effect likely follows from loss aversion, and capuchins' exhibition of both phenomena supports this connection.
- Inference: The endowment effect may have evolutionary roots and may be present in species with social trading systems.
- Inference: Cultural differences in the endowment effect may be substantial; studies show the price gap between sellers and buyers is much larger in US student samples than in UK samples, suggesting cultural variation in the psychological relationship with possessions and money.
