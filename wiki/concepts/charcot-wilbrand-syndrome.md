# Charcot-Wilbrand Syndrome

## Summary

Charcot-Wilbrand Syndrome refers to a disorder characterized by the cessation or restriction of visual dream imagery, typically resulting from brain damage. The syndrome was first described by Jean-Martin Charcot and later expanded by Hermann Wilbrand. The condition involves loss of dream imagery while other aspects of dreaming may be preserved, representing a selective impairment of visual dream content.

## Key facts

- Charcot first described cases of sudden, isolated suppression of mental vision of signs and objects (forms and colors) in 1883. [raw/books/neuroscience/The_Neuropsychology_of_Dreams_A Clinico - Anatomical Study.pdf#p24]
- Wilbrand expanded the syndrome to include global cessation or reduction of dreaming. [raw/books/neuroscience/The_Neuropsychology_of_Dreams_A Clinico - Anatomical Study.pdf#p8]
- The book devotes multiple chapters to reconsidering Charcot-Wilbrand Syndrome, including patient descriptions, anatomical correlates, and clinical correlates. [raw/books/neuroscience/The_Neuropsychology_of_Dreams_A Clinico - Anatomical Study.pdf#p9]

## Inferences

- Inference: The syndrome represents a selective deficit in visual dream imagery, suggesting that visual imagery generation for dreams can be independently impaired from other dream and cognitive functions.
