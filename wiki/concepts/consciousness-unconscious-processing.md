# Consciousness and Unconscious Processing

## Summary

Much of mental processing occurs unconsciously. Consciousness has three essential features: subjectivity (private experience), unity (integrated experience), and intentionality (directed toward goals). Research using binocular rivalry, selective attention, and studies of phantom limbs and neglect syndromes reveals that neural activity can be dissociated from conscious perception.

## Key facts

- Helmholtz proposed that the brain requires considerable time to process sensory information before conscious perception of the stimulus's significance occurs, calling this unconscious inference. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p434]
- Libet found that awareness of the desire to move a finger follows rather than precedes the readiness potential by as much as one second, suggesting acts of free will have significant unconscious preparatory steps. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p435]
- Binocular rivalry demonstrates that sensory input alone does not give rise to consciousness; higher-level interpretation of input is required for conscious perception. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p440]
- Studies of anticorrelated images show that neurons in primary visual cortex compute depth from disparity even when the brain does not consciously perceive depth, demonstrating dissociation between neural activity and consciousness. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p440]

## Inferences

- Inference: The dissociation between neural activity in early visual cortex and conscious depth perception suggests that conscious experience depends on processing in higher cortical areas rather than being determined by initial sensory inputs.
