# Tests for Reality

## Summary

Gibson proposes that perfectly reliable and automatic tests for reality are involved in the working of a perceptual system, and these do not have to be intellectual. These tests include: whether a surface becomes clearer when fixated (an image does not), whether a surface can be scanned (an image cannot), whether crossed diplopia disappears with eye convergence (it does not for images), and whether new features can be discovered by scrutiny (imaginary entities cannot pass this test). The most decisive test is whether one can discover new features by the act of scrutiny—obtaining new stimulation and extracting new information.

## Key facts

- A surface is seen with more or less definition as the accommodation of the lens changes; an image is not. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p276]
- A surface becomes clearer when fixated; an image does not. A surface can be scanned; an image cannot. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p276]
- The most decisive test for reality is whether you can discover new features and details by the act of scrutiny. Can you obtain new stimulation and extract new information from it? Is the information inexhaustible? Is there more to be seen?. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p276]
- A criterion for real versus imaginary is what happens when you turn and move. Whatever goes out of sight as you move your head and comes into sight as you move back is a persisting surface. Whatever comes into sight when you move your head is a preexisting surface. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p276]

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Gibson acknowledges that dreams sometimes have the 'feeling' of reality and that hallucinations in psychosis might seem to prove images can be the same as percepts, but he remains dubious, suggesting alternative explanations for why dreamers, drug-takers, or hallucinating patients do not make ordinary tests for reality.
