# Metacognition

## Summary

Metacognition—the capacity to entertain thoughts about one's own thoughts—is the domain of competence that enables organisms to know the limits of their own knowledge and assign degrees of belief or confidence to their mental states. Dehaene presents evidence that monkeys, dolphins, rats, and pigeons possess the rudiments of metacognition, challenging the view that self-knowledge is uniquely human.

## Key facts

- Metacognition is the capacity to entertain thoughts about one's thoughts, including knowing the limits of one's own knowledge and assigning degrees of confidence to mental states. [raw/books/neuroscience/Consciousness and the Brain_ Deciphering H - Stanislas Dehaene.pdf#p306]
- Donald Rumsfeld's famous distinction among known knowns, known unknowns, and unknown unknowns neatly outlines the domain of metacognition. [raw/books/neuroscience/Consciousness and the Brain_ Deciphering H - Stanislas Dehaene.pdf#p306]
- Evidence suggests that monkeys, dolphins, rats, and pigeons possess the rudiments of metacognition. [raw/books/neuroscience/Consciousness and the Brain_ Deciphering H - Stanislas Dehaene.pdf#p306]

## Inferences

- Inference: The dolphin Natua experiment demonstrates that animals can monitor their own uncertainty: Natua spontaneously used an 'escape' response only on difficult trials, pressing a third paddle when the stimulus frequency was close to the 2,100 hertz reference—precisely where errors were likely [raw/books/neuroscience/Consciousness and the Brain_ Deciphering H - Stanislas Dehaene.pdf#p307-p308].
- Inference: Monkeys generalize metacognitive judgments beyond initial training contexts: once they learn to report 'I didn't perceive well' in a sensory task, they immediately use the opt-out key appropriately in a memory task, demonstrating 'I don't remember well' [raw/books/neuroscience/Consciousness and the Brain_ Deciphering H - Stanislas Dehaene.pdf#p308].

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Some researchers dispute the mentalist interpretation, arguing that the escape behavior can be described in simpler behaviorist terms as trained motor behavior maximizing reward [raw/books/neuroscience/Consciousness and the Brain_ Deciphering H - Stanislas Dehaene.pdf#p308].
