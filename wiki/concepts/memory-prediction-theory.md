# Memory-prediction theory

## Summary

Jeff Hawkins proposed the memory-prediction theory, suggesting the neocortex is primarily a memory system that constantly makes predictions rather than a computer performing calculations. The theory posits that the brain stores sequences of patterns, recalls them autoassociatively, stores them in invariant form, and uses feedback connections to compare predictions with incoming sensory information.

## Key facts

- Hawkins argues that the brain doesn't compute answers—it retrieves them from memory, with slow neurons being fast enough for this retrieval task. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p375]
- The neocortex stores patterns in four ways: as sequences, autoassociatively (recalling complete patterns from partial ones), in invariant form (handling variations automatically), and in hierarchy. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p375]
- Prediction is proposed as the primary function of the neocortex and the foundation of intelligence. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p376]
- The brain sends information flowing both up (what is happening) and down (what is expected to happen) the cortical hierarchy. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p376]
- Humans have unique intelligence because their neocortex can make predictions about more abstract patterns and longer temporal sequences. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p377]

## Inferences

- Inference: Hawkins believes that to behave as a human, one needs to experience life as a human biological entity, suggesting that human-level intelligence cannot simply be programmed into a robot.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The memory-prediction theory remains a theoretical framework and has not been universally accepted by the neuroscience community.
