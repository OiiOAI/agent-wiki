# Interrogative Suggestibility

## Summary

Interrogative suggestibility, as defined by Gisli Gudjonsson, is the tendency to change claims about the past in response to misleading information and suggestive questions. High levels of interrogative suggestibility correlate with vulnerability to false confessions and false memories. Individuals who score high on suggestibility scales are more likely to develop false beliefs about past events when exposed to suggestive procedures.

## Key facts

- Gisli Gudjonsson developed a scale for measuring interrogative suggestibility—the tendency to change claims about the past in response to misleading information and suggestive questions. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p164]
- People who had made criminal confessions they later retracted were more influenced by suggestive questions than 'deniers' who steadfastly refused to acknowledge involvement despite forensic evidence against them. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p165]
- Memory performance of confessors and deniers on standard clinical tests did not differ, indicating interrogative suggestibility is a distinct trait. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p165]
- Individuals who came up with earliest memories from prior to twenty-four months were more suggestible on Gudjonsson's interrogative suggestibility scale than those who did not. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p169]
- People with especially vivid imagery and those who score high on interrogative suggestibility scales appear to be at risk for creating some types of false memories. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p171]
