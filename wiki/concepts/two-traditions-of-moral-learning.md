# Two Traditions of Moral Learning

## Summary

Rawls distinguishes two main traditions of moral learning. The empiricist tradition (Hume, Sidgwick, Freud) holds that moral training supplies missing motives through rewards, punishments, and early conditioning, often before the child understands reasons for moral norms. The rationalist tradition (Rousseau, Kant, Mill, Piaget) holds that moral development comes from the natural free development of intellectual and emotional capacities once persons recognize their social relations.

## Key facts

- The empiricist tradition holds that moral training supplies missing motives: the desire to do right and aversion to doing wrong, achieved through approbation and punishment. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p407]
- In the empiricist tradition, moral sentiments are acquired early before adequate understanding of reasons for norms, often bearing scars of early training. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p407]
- The rationalist tradition holds that moral learning is the free development of innate capacities according to natural bent; once persons understand their place in society, they appreciate mutual benefits of fair cooperation. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p408]
- Rationalist tradition regards moral feelings as a natural outgrowth of full appreciation of our social nature rather than added motives. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p408]
- Rawls aims to combine both traditions in sketching moral development in a well-ordered society realizing justice as fairness. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p409]

## Inferences

- Inference: Rawls appears to favor elements of the rationalist tradition since it presents a 'happier picture' where principles of right spring from our nature and are not at odds with our good.
