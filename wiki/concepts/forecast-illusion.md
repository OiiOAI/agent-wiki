# Forecast Illusion

## Summary

The forecast illusion is the tendency to overestimate the reliability of expert predictions. Philip Tetlock's 10-year study of 284 experts found their accuracy only marginally better than random chance. Experts face a win-win scenario: correct predictions bring publicity, wrong predictions carry no penalties.

## Key facts

- Philip Tetlock evaluated 28,361 predictions from 284 self-appointed professionals over ten years; in terms of accuracy, experts fared only marginally better than a random forecast generator. [raw/books/strategy/The Art of Thinking Clearly _ Better thinking, Better - Rolf Dobelli; Nicky Griffin.pdf#p97]
- The media darlings were among the poorest performers; the worst were prophets of doom and disintegration whose far-fetched forecasts included the collapse of Canada, Nigeria, China, India, Indonesia, South Africa, Belgium and the EU. [raw/books/strategy/The Art of Thinking Clearly _ Better thinking, Better - Rolf Dobelli; Nicky Griffin.pdf#p97]
- John Kenneth Galbraith wrote: 'There are two kinds of forecasters: those who don't know, and those who don't know they don't know.'. [raw/books/strategy/The Art of Thinking Clearly _ Better thinking, Better - Rolf Dobelli; Nicky Griffin.pdf#p97]
- Peter Lynch noted that 60,000 economists in the U.S. employed full-time trying to forecast recessions and interest rates would all be millionaires by now if they could do it successfully twice in a row. [raw/books/strategy/The Art of Thinking Clearly _ Better thinking, Better - Rolf Dobelli; Nicky Griffin.pdf#p97]

## Inferences

- Inference: Experts have incentives to make sensational predictions because correct forecasts bring publicity and publication deals while wrong forecasts face no penalties.
