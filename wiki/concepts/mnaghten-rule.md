# M'Naghten Rule

## Summary

The legal standard for determining insanity, established after the 1843 M'Naghten case, stating that a person is not criminally responsible if, at the time of the crime, they were so 'laboring under such a defect of reason from disease of the mind' that they could not distinguish right from wrong. This codified the concept that severe mental illness can negate criminal responsibility.

## Key facts

- Daniel M'Naghten attempted to assassinate the British Prime Minister in 1843 but killed his secretary instead, believing himself persecuted by powerful forces including the Prime Minister. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p602]
- The M'Naghten rule states that a person is not guilty by reason of insanity if, at the time of the crime, they could not distinguish right from wrong due to disease of the mind. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p603]
- M'Naghten spent the rest of his life in insane asylums, reasonably well treated by the standards of the time, after being found not guilty by reason of insanity. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p603]

## Inferences

- Inference: The M'Naghten rule represents an early attempt to integrate psychological understanding into legal frameworks, though it focuses on a narrow criterion (right/wrong distinction) rather than broader questions of volition and biological constraint.
