# Meme

## Summary

A meme is a unit of cultural transmission or imitation, analogous to the gene. Dawkins proposed that memes propagate themselves in the 'meme pool' by leaping from brain to brain via imitation. Examples include tunes, ideas, catch-phrases, clothes fashions, and ways of making pots. The meme concept suggests that cultural evolution follows principles similar to genetic evolution, with successful memes spreading through populations.

## Key facts

- The word 'meme' comes from Greek 'mimeme' (to imitate), abbreviated to sound like 'gene' and pronounced to rhyme with 'cream'. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p230]
- Memes propagate themselves in the meme pool by leaping from brain to brain via a process which can be called imitation. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p230]
- When you plant a fertile meme in someone's mind you literally parasitize their brain, turning it into a vehicle for the meme's propagation. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p230]
- Successful memes share three qualities with successful genes: longevity, fecundity, and copying-fidelity. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p231]
- The meme for 'belief in life after death' exists millions of times over as a structure in the nervous systems of individual humans. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p230]
- Memes should be regarded as living structures technically, not just metaphorically: they are self-replicating brain structures, actual patterns of neuronal wiring-up that reconstitute themselves in one brain after another. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p374]
- Juan Delius of the University of Konstanz published a detailed picture of what the neuronal hardware of a meme might look like, exploring analogies between memes and parasites. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p374]
- 'Auld Lang Syne' is universally rendered with an error: 'For the sake of auld lang syne' instead of Burns' original 'For auld lang syne'; the mutation spread because the sibilant 's' and 'k' sounds are more conspicuous, making it easier for uncertain singers to learn the mutant form. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p375]
- Hamilton's 1964 papers on kin selection showed dormancy followed by exponential spread: citations were negligible until 1973-1974, then increased explosively, consistent with an epidemic-like memic spread. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p377]
- The mutant citation 'The genetical theory of social behaviour' (instead of 'The genetical evolution of social behaviour') arose independently in both Wilson's Sociobiology and Dawkins' The Selfish Gene, likely because Fisher's famous book 'The Genetical Theory of Natural Selection' primed both authors to add the third word. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p379]
- Computer viruses are memes that have found a new habitat in electronic computers, spreading through networks and floppy discs, analogous to biological parasites. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p380]
- Faith is defined as belief in something without supporting evidence; Dawkins characterizes it as a kind of mental illness capable of immunizing people against appeals to pity or decent human feelings. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p381]

## Inferences

- Inference: Viruses may be genes that have broken loose from 'colonies' like ourselves and travel directly between bodies rather than via sperm and eggs
- Inference: Co-adapted meme-complexes may evolve, similar to co-adapted gene complexes, with religions forming stable sets of mutually-assisting memes
- Inference: The spread of scientific ideas follows memic patterns: Hamilton's papers were 'in the air' before 1975 books popularized them, suggesting a longer-term exponential bandwagon rather than book-triggered spread.
- Inference: Keith Henson's term 'memeoids' describes victims taken over by memes to the extent their own survival becomes inconsequential; examples include religious fanatics in Belfast or Beirut.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The concept of memes remains speculative and its scientific status is debated
- Uncertain: Whether the milieu of human culture really has what it takes to get a form of Darwinism going remains uncertain.
- Uncertain: Whether coadapted meme complexes, selected for mutual compatibility, form stable cultural units requires further investigation.
