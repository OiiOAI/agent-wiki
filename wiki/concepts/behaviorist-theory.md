# Behaviorist Theory of Language

## Summary

The behaviorist theory of language, associated with B.F. Skinner, proposes that language is learned through the same associative mechanisms as other behaviors—through reinforcement of sound-meaning pairings. Deacon discusses this theory as one of several paradigms for understanding language, noting that while it explains how arbitrary associations can be learned, it fails to account for the systematic combinatorial structure of grammar and the symbolic nature of word meaning.

## Key facts

- Skinner's behaviorist theory views language learning as conditioning—pairing sounds with objects and reinforcing correct associations. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p25]
- Deacon cites Herrnstein's pigeon experiment as demonstrating that complex interlocking associations can be learned, but argues this doesn't constitute symbolic reference. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p68]
- The behaviorist approach cannot explain how learned associations gain systematic combinatorial properties or how they transcend the correlation-dependence of indexical reference. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p68]
