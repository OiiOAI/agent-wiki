# Teamwork Failures in Surgery

## Summary

The disconnect between surgical team members' awareness of patient risks and operative plans, documented through multiple surveys and studies.

## Key facts

- Researchers observed that team members are commonly not all aware of a given patient's risks, the problems they need to be ready for, or why the surgeon is doing the operation. [raw/books/strategy/The checklist manifesto _ how to get things right - Atul Gawande.pdf#p78]
- In a survey of 300 staff members exiting the operating room after a case, one out of eight reported they were not even sure about where the incision would be until the operation started. [raw/books/strategy/The checklist manifesto _ how to get things right - Atul Gawande.pdf#p78]
- Brian Sexton found that 64% of surgeons rated their operations as having high levels of teamwork, while only 39% of anesthesiologists, 28% of nurses, and 10% of anesthesia residents agreed. [raw/books/strategy/The checklist manifesto _ how to get things right - Atul Gawande.pdf#p78]
- One in four surgeons believed that junior team members should not question the decisions of a senior practitioner. [raw/books/strategy/The checklist manifesto _ how to get things right - Atul Gawande.pdf#p78]
- The most common obstacle to effective teams is a kind of silent disengagement where specialized technicians stick narrowly to their domains, exemplified by 'That's not my problem' thinking. [raw/books/strategy/The checklist manifesto _ how to get things right - Atul Gawande.pdf#p78]

## Inferences

- Inference: The substantial gap between surgeon self-assessment and other team members' perceptions suggests that surgeons may be the least accurate judges of their own teamwork quality.
