# Surface Layout

## Summary

Surface layout is Gibson's ecological replacement for the concept of geometrical space. Rather than objects in space, Gibson describes the environment as consisting of substances and surfaces whose layouts constitute what is perceived. Surfaces are substantial, textured, and have a facing orientation—unlike abstract geometrical planes which are ghosts. The layout of surfaces persists in some respects and changes in others.

## Key facts

- The environment consists of medium, substances, and the surfaces that separate them—not of bodies in space. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p43]
- A surface has only one side; a plane has two. A geometrical plane must be conceived as a very thin sheet in space, not as an interface between medium and substance. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p61]
- A surface has the property of facing a source of illumination or a point of observation; a plane does not have this property. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p61]
- Surfaces are denumerable (objects can be counted) whereas substances are not denumerable. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p60]
- The ecological laws of surfaces include: all persisting substances have surfaces with layout; resistance to deformation depends on viscosity; resistance to disintegration depends on cohesion; surfaces have characteristic texture, shape, reflectance, and spectral reflectance. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p50]

## Inferences

- Inference: Surface layout replaces the need for Kantian space—animals perceive surfaces and their layouts directly, not abstract geometrical space.
