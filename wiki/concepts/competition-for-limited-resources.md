# Competition for Limited Resources

## Summary

Competition for limited resources intensifies desire beyond what scarcity alone produces. Research demonstrates that items made scarce through social demand are rated more desirable than items made scarce by mistake. This principle is exploited in sales tactics, romantic relationships, and auction settings.

## Key facts

- Cookies made less available through social demand were rated the most desirable of any in the study, significantly higher than cookies made scarce by mistake. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p235]
- Advertisers exploit competition by showing 'popular demand' messages, crowds pressing store doors, or empty shelves being rapidly depleted. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p235]
- Romantic partners sometimes reveal or invent the attentions of a new admirer to increase desire through competition. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p235]
- Sales tactics called 'goosing 'em off the fence' involve creating a fictitious competing buyer to pressure indecisive prospects. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p235]
- In the 1973 Poseidon Auction, Barry Diller paid $3.3 million for The Poseidon Adventure—ABC expected to lose $1 million on the deal. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p236]
- CBS president Robert Wood described the bidding as entering a 'fever' where he 'kept bidding like a guy who had lost his mind.'. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p237]

## Inferences

- Inference: The Poseidon Auction case demonstrates that auction formats create a competitive dynamic that overrides rational cost-benefit analysis, as evidenced by both Diller's $1 million expected loss and Wood's admission of irrational bidding behavior.
