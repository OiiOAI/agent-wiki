# Tower Tasks

## Summary

Classic neuropsychological paradigms for studying planning ability. Tasks involve moving disks across pegs according to rules to achieve a goal state. Difficulty increases with the number of required moves. Patients with prefrontal cortex lesions are impaired on these tasks, particularly when making counter-intuitive moves.

## Key facts

- Planning difficulty increases with the number of competing alternative choices at each step, with increased pre-move preparation time related to the number of competing options. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroscience of Decision Making - Mandel, David R.,Vartanian, Oshin.pdf#p391]
- When performance on easy Tower of London problems was subtracted from harder problems, only right Brodmann area 10 and left Brodmann area 9 along with premotor cortex remained significantly activated. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroscience of Decision Making - Mandel, David R.,Vartanian, Oshin.pdf#p392]
- Patients with PFC lesions are impaired on Tower tasks when they must overcome a prepotent strategy and make a counter-intuitive move, regardless of problem difficulty. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroscience of Decision Making - Mandel, David R.,Vartanian, Oshin.pdf#p393]
- fMRI studies identify two distinct profiles during Tower tasks: one varying parametrically with goal-processing operations (right DLPFC, bilateral parietal cortex, premotor cortex), and one pronounced only during goal-intensive trials (left inferior frontal gyrus). [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroscience of Decision Making - Mandel, David R.,Vartanian, Oshin.pdf#p392]

## Inferences

- Inference: Tower tasks reveal that planning involves both working memory units and plan units that generate novel activation patterns for plan construction.
- Inference: The difference in neural activation between easy and hard problems helps isolate planning-specific processes from more general task demands.
