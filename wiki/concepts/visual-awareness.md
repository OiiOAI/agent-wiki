# Visual Awareness

## Summary

Visual awareness refers to what we consciously see. The search for its neural correlate asks which neurons' activity corresponds to conscious visual perception. Koch and Crick hypothesized that consciousness corresponds to a particular type of activity in a transient set of neurons distributed across the cortex, likely requiring some form of attention, very short-term memory, and the binding together of different visual features.

## Key facts

- There appears to be no single cortical area whose activity corresponds to the global content of our visual awareness. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p204]
- Koch and Crick hypothesized that consciousness corresponds to a particular type of activity in a transient set of neurons that are a fraction of a much larger set of potential candidates. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p207]
- The questions at the neural level are: Where are these neurons in the brain? Are they of any particular neuronal type? What is special about their connections? What is special about the way they are firing?. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p207]
- Awareness is likely to involve some form of attention, so the brain's attention mechanism should be studied. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p208]
- Awareness is likely to involve some form of very short-term memory, so the neural behavior when storing and using such memories should be examined. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p208]
- There may be several forms of visual awareness and, by extension, even more forms of consciousness in general. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p262]
- Three possible stages of visual processing correspond to different levels of visual awareness: a very transient one (primal sketch), a more lasting and vivid one (2½D sketch), and an object-centered one (3D model). [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p262]
- The 2½D sketch and the 3D model are both inferences, with both interpretations of the visual input potentially being mistaken. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p262]
- Customary usages of words like 'see' may not accurately describe the actual activities in our brains, since the word is used in multiple senses. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p262]

## Inferences

- Inference: Johnson-Laird's operating system concept reminds one somewhat of the thalamus, though neurons there may be too few to represent all contents of visual awareness.
- Inference: The minimum message for understanding consciousness is that if one is interested in visual awareness, the thalamus should not be neglected, as it coordinates activities of associated cortical areas.
- Inference: Awareness requires the activity of various cortical areas as well as the thalamus, analogous to how a conductor needs the orchestra to produce music.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Whether the right hemisphere is more than an automaton, lacking well-developed language, remains to be answered.
- Uncertain: Whether the subdivisions of the pulvinar correspond to Marr's stages or to the more numerous sub-subdivisions in the Van Essen visual hierarchy is unknown.
