# Looming

## Summary

Looming is the accelerated magnification of the visual solid angle of an approaching object, which specifies the imminence of collision. Schiff, Caviness, and Gibson (1962) demonstrated that looming elicits blink reflexes in humans and avoidance behavior in animals (monkeys, kittens, chicks, fiddler crabs), even though no mechanical contact occurs. The rate of magnification is proportional to the imminence of collision.

## Key facts

- Progressive magnification of a shadow on a screen creates the perception of an object approaching and coming up to the face; observers blink and may duck or dodge involuntarily. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p199]
- The rate of magnification of looming is proportional to the imminence of collision—this is the visual solid angle approaching a hemisphere of the ambient array (180 degrees). [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p199]
- Fiddler crabs, monkeys, kittens, and chicks all show avoidance behavior to looming stimuli but not to miniature (retreating) stimuli, demonstrating that looming specifies threat of collision across species. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p199]
- When looming is skewed (asymmetric), animals (crabs) dodge appropriately left or right, suggesting the optical information specifies the full trajectory of the approaching object. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p199]

## Inferences

- Inference: Looming should be considered a 'stimulus' for the blink reflex in the same sense that a puff of air to the cornea is a stimulus, rather than merely an arbitrary visual cue.
