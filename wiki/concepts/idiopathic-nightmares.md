# Idiopathic Nightmares

## Summary

Idiopathic nightmares are disturbing dreams with no obvious trauma-related cause, affecting about 4 percent of the general adult population. They are more common in women and are associated with conditions including insomnia, depression, and suicidal ideation. The authors discuss multiple contributing factors including stress, genetics, personality characteristics (thin psychological boundaries), and early childhood adversity.

## Key facts

- Clinically significant nightmares, occurring at least once a week, are reported by about 4 percent of the general adult population. [raw/books/neuroscience/When Brains Dream - Antonio Zadra.pdf#p213]
- Idiopathic nightmares are more common in women than men and are usually accompanied by significant daytime worries about the dreams. [raw/books/neuroscience/When Brains Dream - Antonio Zadra.pdf#p213]
- Ernest Hartmann proposed that frequent nightmare sufferers have 'thin psychological boundaries'—being unusually open, emotionally sensitive, and creative. [raw/books/neuroscience/When Brains Dream - Antonio Zadra.pdf#p214]
- People with thin psychological boundaries tend to remember more dreams, experience more nightmares, and report more intense and bizarre dreams. [raw/books/neuroscience/When Brains Dream - Antonio Zadra.pdf#p214]
- A large-scale twin study found gene variants associated with both childhood and adult nightmares. [raw/books/neuroscience/When Brains Dream - Antonio Zadra.pdf#p215]

## Inferences

- Inference: The heterogeneity of idiopathic nightmares—varying from acute to chronic, with different contents and emotions—suggests multiple underlying mechanisms rather than a single cause.
