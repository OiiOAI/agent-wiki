# Active Sensing

## Summary

Active sensing refers to brain-initiated search for sensory information as opposed to passive response to expected events. The sensitivity of sensors depends on the effectors that can move them and maximize their efficacy. Active sensing occurs in two forms: the entire body can translocate (fly's solution) or sensors can be moved locally (spider's solution). The physiological foundation is corollary discharge, informing sensory systems about upcoming sensory input changes due to self-generated movements. Examples include echolocation in bats, electrolocation in electric fish, sniffing in olfaction, and saccadic eye movements in vision.

- Active sensing refers to a brain-initiated search as opposed to response to an expected event, where stimulus sensitivity depends on effectors that can move sensors and maximize their efficacy. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain from Inside Out - György Buzsáki MD, PhD.pdf#p89]
- Patient AI, unable to move her eyes since birth due to fibrosis, compensated by making head saccades (200 ms average duration) to read and scan pictures, demonstrating that active head movement enhances visual sensing ability. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain from Inside Out - György Buzsáki MD, PhD.pdf#p89]
- Weakly electric fish (Eigenmannia) actively probe their environment with electricity, using corollary discharge comparator circuits analogous to those in crickets to distinguish self-generated from external electric fields. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain from Inside Out - György Buzsáki MD, PhD.pdf#p90]
- In olfaction, sniffing (4-10 sniffs per second) generates turbulence so odorants come in direct contact with receptors, and the entire olfactory system is informed ahead of time about expected odorant arrival by corollary discharge. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain from Inside Out - György Buzsáki MD, PhD.pdf#p92]
- Microsaccades during eye fixation increase visual performance by enhancing spiking activity of neurons in the visual cortex, with saccades functioning as 'visual sniffs' that provide sensory gain. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain from Inside Out - György Buzsáki MD, PhD.pdf#p74]
