# Negotiation and Loss Aversion

## Summary

Loss aversion profoundly shapes negotiation dynamics. The existing contract or status quo serves as the reference point, making any proposed change from that baseline feel like a loss to the party being asked to concede. This asymmetry—where your sacrifice feels more painful than the benefit it delivers to me—makes mutually beneficial deals harder to reach, especially when dividing a shrinking resource (a 'shrinking pie') rather than an expanding one.

## Key facts

- In labor negotiations, both parties understand that the existing contract is the reference point, and proposals for change in any dimension are perceived as one party giving in to the other. Loss aversion makes the giver feel more pain than the receiver feels benefit, creating systematic resistance to concessions. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p393]
- Negotiators often pretend to be highly committed to a concession (or a missile system in arms control negotiations) even when they view it as a bargaining chip they intend to trade away, because the apparent sacrifice is supposed to obligate the other party to make a comparable sacrifice. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p393]
- Dividing a shrinking pie is psychologically harder than dividing an expanding one: when both parties must give something up, the loss aversion of each party amplifies resistance, whereas when the total is growing, each party can receive more without the other party experiencing a direct loss. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p393]
- In real estate during market downturns, homeowners with high purchase prices (high reference points) set higher asking prices, stay on the market longer, and ultimately accept higher sale prices—a form of loss aversion-driven behavior that actually works against quick sales. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p384]

## Inferences

- Inference: Loss aversion in negotiation can be exploited by changing the reference point: framing a concession as 'returning to the original agreement' rather than 'giving up a benefit' may reduce the perceived loss, or framing the entire negotiation as building on a growing resource rather than dividing a fixed one can shift dynamics from adversarial to collaborative.
