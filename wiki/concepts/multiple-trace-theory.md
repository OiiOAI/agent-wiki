# Multiple Trace Theory of Memory Consolidation

## Summary

The Multiple Trace Theory, proposed by Lynn Nadel and Morris Moscovitch, posits that retrieval of autobiographical episodes always requires hippocampal engagement. Unlike the Standard Model of Consolidation, this theory distinguishes between semantic memories (which become cortex-independent) and episodic autobiographical memories (which remain hippocampal-dependent indefinitely).

## Key facts

- According to the Multiple Trace Theory, semantic memories can become independent of the hippocampal system because they do not require contextual details. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p223]
- Episodic autobiographical memories always require hippocampal engagement because they include specific details of when, where, and what happened. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p223]
- Henry could remember semantic facts from his past (e.g., father from the South, general Christmas practices) but could not retrieve specific autobiographical episodes. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p218]
- Henry retained only two autobiographical memories from his preoperative life: his first cigarette and a plane ride at age thirteen. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p226]

## Inferences

- Inference: Henry's case supports the Multiple Trace Theory over the Standard Model of Consolidation because he could retrieve semantic information but not episodic autobiographical events from the same time periods.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Larry Squire suggested Henry's autobiographical memory deficit might have resulted from age-related brain disease observed in later scans, though the autobiographical vacuum was evident in 1992 interviews.
