# Sense-making

## Summary

Sense-making is the process by which an autopoietic system constitutes its environment as a domain of significance and valence. According to Varela, living is sense-making: an organism brings forth a world of meaning based on its autopoietically established identity. The environment (Umwelt) emerges from the physicochemical world through the being of the organism, which singles out features as relevant to its continued existence. Sense-making involves the surplus of significance that living systems inject into an otherwise indifferent physicochemical world, transforming it into a meaningful domain of attraction and repulsion, approach and escape.

## Key facts

- Varela reformulated Maturana's proposition 'living is a process of cognition' as 'living is sense-making.'. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p157]
- The organism's environment is the sense it makes of the world—a place of significance and valence resulting from the global action of the organism. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p157]
- Varela describes the organism's environment as having a 'surplus of significance' compared to the physicochemical world, thanks to the organism's autonomy. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p157]
- Di Paolo argues that although autopoiesis is necessary for sense-making, it is not sufficient; adaptivity must be added for sense-making to occur. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p148]

## Inferences

- Inference: Sense-making is none other than intentionality in its minimal and original biological form.
- Inference: Sense-making requires more than minimal autopoiesis; it requires the capacity for adaptivity or assimilation and accommodation.
