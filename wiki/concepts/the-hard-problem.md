# The Hard Problem

## Summary

The Hard Problem, coined by David Chalmers in the early 1990s, asks why physical processes give rise to rich inner life at all—why there is something it is like to have conscious experiences. It contrasts with 'easy problems' that explain functional and behavioural properties. The hard problem questions why experience accompanies physical processing, even after all functional explanations are complete.

## Key facts

- The term 'hard problem' was coined by Australian philosopher David Chalmers in the early 1990s. [raw/books/neuroscience/Being You _ A New Science of Consciousness - Seth, Anil.pdf#p23]
- The hard problem asks why physical processing gives rise to a rich inner life at all. [raw/books/neuroscience/Being You _ A New Science of Consciousness - Seth, Anil.pdf#p23]
- Chalmers contrasts the hard problem with 'easy problems' concerning functional and behavioural properties like sensory processing, action selection, and attention. [raw/books/neuroscience/Being You _ A New Science of Consciousness - Seth, Anil.pdf#p24]
- Even after explaining all functional properties, a further unanswered question remains: why is the performance of these functions accompanied by experience?. [raw/books/neuroscience/Being You _ A New Science of Consciousness - Seth, Anil.pdf#p24]
- The hard problem of consciousness is described as 'the biggest unsolved puzzle of contemporary neuroscience, if not all science', and involves explaining how the physical brain gives rise to subjective experience. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p13]
- Solms states that the book proposes a solution to the hard problem that is a radical departure from conventional approaches. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p13]
- The book argues that the hard problem has been made unnecessarily hard by the universal assumption that consciousness is a cortical function. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p13]
- Solms argues that the three departures in the book — consciousness is more primitive than cortex, feelings are primary over cognition, and consciousness is engineerable — make the hard problem less hard. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p14]
- Many philosophers and scientists still believe that sentience serves no physical purpose, but Solms argues that consciousness exists to help organisms do better — feeling is about how well or badly you are doing in life. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p13]

## Inferences

- Inference: The hard problem's persistence may stem from interpreting failures of imagination as insights into necessity, similar to how vitalism failed.
- Inference: By relocating consciousness from the cortex to the brainstem, Solms proposes that the hard problem becomes more tractable because the brainstem's functions can be understood in terms of well-established physical and biological principles, including Friston's free energy principle.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Whether Solms's proposed solution will be accepted as successfully resolving the hard problem is uncertain — the book presents it as a new perspective rather than a definitively proven theory.
