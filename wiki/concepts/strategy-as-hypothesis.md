# Strategy as Hypothesis

## Summary

Rumelt argues that a good strategy is like a scientific hypothesis—an educated prediction about what will work. Just as scientists form conjectures at the edge of knowledge and test them against known laws and empirical data, business leaders must test strategic insights against established principles and accumulated knowledge. A new strategy presents the opportunity to generate proprietary functional knowledge, and its implementation is an experiment. As results appear, good leaders learn and adjust their strategies accordingly.

## Key facts

- A good business strategy deals with the edge between the known and the unknown, and the uneasy sense of ambiguity is the scent of opportunity. [raw/books/strategy/Good strategy, bad strategy _ the difference and why it - Richard P_ Rumelt.pdf#p89]
- In science, a new theory cannot have been deduced from existing knowledge—it arises as an insight or creative judgment, and its worth is determined by empirical data. [raw/books/strategy/Good strategy, bad strategy _ the difference and why it - Richard P_ Rumelt.pdf#p90]
- Howard Schultz formed the strategic hypothesis that the Italian espresso experience could be recreated in America, but he tested and modified this hypothesis through hundreds of iterations as he observed customer responses. [raw/books/strategy/Good strategy, bad strategy _ the difference and why it - Richard P_ Rumelt.pdf#p91]
- The process of learning—hypothesis, data, anomaly, new hypothesis, data—is called scientific induction and is a critical element of every successful business. [raw/books/strategy/Good strategy, bad strategy _ the difference and why it - Richard P_ Rumelt.pdf#p92]
- A strategy is an educated prediction of how the world works, and its ultimate worth is determined by its success, not its acceptability to authorities. [raw/books/strategy/Good strategy, bad strategy _ the difference and why it - Richard P_ Rumelt.pdf#p90]

## Inferences
