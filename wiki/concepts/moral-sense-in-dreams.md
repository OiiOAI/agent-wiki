# Moral Sense in Dreams

## Summary

A debated question in dream psychology concerning whether moral dispositions persist into dream life, with authorities divided between those who claim morality disappears in dreams and those who maintain the moral personality continues.

## Key facts

- Jessen wrote that we do not become better or more virtuous in sleep; conscience seems to be silent in dreams, and we may commit the worst crimes with complete indifference. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p1342]
- Hildebrandt argued that Kant's categorical imperative is a companion who follows so close at our heels that we cannot be free of it even in sleep. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p1343]
- Maury observed that in dreams his impulses speak and make him act without conscience holding him back, and he succumbs to impulses he resists while awake. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p1348]
- Hildebrandt concluded that any immoral dream impulse possesses a degree of power even in waking life, though inhibited and unable to force its way into action. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p1347]

## Inferences

- Inference: The conflict between waking morality and dream immorality suggests that what is fundamental in man's nature, his moral being, is too firmly fixed to be affected by the kaleidoscopic shuffling of imagination in dreams.
- Inference: Dreams may reveal hidden impulses and aspects of character that waking consciousness normally suppresses or denies.
