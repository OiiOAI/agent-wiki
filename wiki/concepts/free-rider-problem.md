# Free Rider Problem

## Summary

The central challenge to cooperation: individuals can benefit from group resources while contributing little or nothing to the group's efforts. Darwin identified this as the main objection to group selection—if altruistic individuals sacrifice for the group while selfish individuals free-ride, then selfishness should win within every group. Solutions to the free rider problem include monitoring, punishment, gossip, shared moral matrices, and sacralization of norms. Successful groups find ways to suppress selfishness enough that group-level selection can operate, enabling superorganism-level traits to evolve.

## Key facts

- Darwin raised the free rider problem: 'He who was ready to sacrifice his life...rather than betray his comrades, would often leave no offspring to inherit his noble nature.'. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p213]
- The Fehr and Gächter public goods experiment showed that 84% of subjects paid to punish free riders even when there was no chance of reciprocity, suggesting evolved punishment instincts. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p200]
- Punishing bad behavior promoted virtue and benefits the group; when punishment was removed, people behaved selfishly (replicating Glaucon's ring of Gyges argument). [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p200]
- Richard Sosis found that religious communes survived longer because sacralization made norms more binding, preventing cost-benefit analysis that would undermine irrational but group-beneficial practices. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p279]

## Inferences

- Inference: The universality of punishment behaviors (even costly, non-reciprocal punishment) suggests that mechanisms for solving the free rider problem were essential to human evolution.
