# Reward and Punishment Super Response Tendency

## Summary

The tendency to respond extremely strongly to incentives and disincentives. Munger places this first in his discussion because, despite its apparent obviousness, he consistently underestimated its power. The tendency includes incentive-caused bias, where people rationally justify bad behavior driven by rewards. Federal Express and Xerox are cited as case studies.

## Key facts

- Federal Express had extreme difficulty getting the night shift to perform until they changed from hourly pay to per-shift pay, letting employees go home when all planes were loaded. [raw/books/strategy/Poor Charlie's Almanack_ The Wit and Wisdo - Charles T. Munger, Peter D. Kaufman, Ed We.pdf#p473]
- Xerox discovered its sales commission structure gave perverse incentives to push the inferior machine, explaining poor sales of its superior new machine. [raw/books/strategy/Poor Charlie's Almanack_ The Wit and Wisdo - Charles T. Munger, Peter D. Kaufman, Ed We.pdf#p474]
- Munger states: 'Perhaps the most important rule in management is Get the incentives right.'. [raw/books/strategy/Poor Charlie's Almanack_ The Wit and Wisdo - Charles T. Munger, Peter D. Kaufman, Ed We.pdf#p476]
- Ben Franklin's maxim states: 'If you would persuade, appeal to interest and not to reason.'. [raw/books/strategy/Poor Charlie's Almanack_ The Wit and Wisdo - Charles T. Munger, Peter D. Kaufman, Ed We.pdf#p475]
- Soviet communism failed because workers pretended to work while the state pretended to pay them, illustrating extreme incentive misalignment. [raw/books/strategy/Poor Charlie's Almanack_ The Wit and Wisdo - Charles T. Munger, Peter D. Kaufman, Ed We.pdf#p475]

## Inferences

- Inference: Munger distinguishes between 'Planck knowledge' (genuine expertise from actual mastery) and 'chauffeur knowledge' (superficial talk without deep understanding), warning that incentive structures can make people appear competent when they merely possess chauffeur knowledge.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Munger acknowledges Skinner's overclaiming about incentive superpower but credits him with demonstrating prompt rewards work better than delayed rewards, and that random distribution of rewards maintains conditioned behavior longest.
