# Neuroeconomics of Honesty

## Summary

The neuroscience of truth-telling and deception, involving the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) for both resisting lying and executing it effectively, the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) for monitoring cognitive conflict between reality and false reports, and the finding that compulsive liars show both increased white matter and decreased gray matter in frontal regions.

## Key facts

- Transcranial direct-current stimulation that inactivates the dlPFC makes subjects slower and less successful in lying, demonstrating a causal role for the dlPFC in deceptive behavior. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p529]
- People with dlPFC damage are less likely to take honesty into account when honesty and self-interest are pitted against each other in economic games. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p529]
- Compulsive liars have increased white matter but decreased gray matter in the frontal cortex, reflecting both enhanced connectivity for strategic deception and reduced regulatory capacity. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p527]
- In instructed lying, the dlPFC handles the difficult task of lying effectively while the ACC monitors cognitive conflict between reality and instructed report. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p528]

## Inferences

- Inference: The finding that dlPFC damage increases honest behavior while dlPFC inactivation impairs lying suggests this brain region serves paradoxical functions in both enabling and constraining deception.
