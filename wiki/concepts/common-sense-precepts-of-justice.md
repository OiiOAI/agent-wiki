# Common Sense Precepts of Justice

## Summary

Common sense precepts of justice (such as 'to each according to his effort' or 'to each according to his contribution') arise naturally in a competitive economy but have no independent force—they must be understood within the framework of the principles of justice regulating background institutions. Different conceptions of justice generate different weights for these precepts without necessarily changing which precepts are recognized. The precepts are at the wrong level of generality to serve as first principles.

## Key facts

- Common sense precepts of justice arise in a competitive economy by identifying features of jobs significant on the demand or supply side of the market—marginal productivity, training, effort, and risk all earn premiums. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p279]
- Different conceptions of justice are likely to generate much the same common sense precepts but assign them different weights—the contrast shows up not at the level of norms but in their relative emphasis over time. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p280]
- None of the common sense precepts can be plausibly raised to a first principle—each has arisen in answer to a relevant feature connected with particular institutions. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p281]
- The precept of contribution depends upon supply and demand conditions reflecting underlying opportunities—there is no presumption that following it leads to a just outcome unless the basic structure is appropriately regulated. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p281]
- The precept of need is left to the transfer branch and does not serve as a precept of wages—to assess the justice of distributive shares, one must note the total working of the background arrangements. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p282]
