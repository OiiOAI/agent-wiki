# Organizational Dysfunction

## Summary

Organizational dysfunction arises when problems come from the organization itself rather than competition or technology. The most common problems come from history of specialization and success—what worked in an era of growth becomes 'the way things are done.' GM's dysfunction included spin doctoring replacing honest analysis, rampant careerism, insufferable bureaucracy, and lack of trust. Nokia's decline from over 50% market share in 2007 to under 5% by 2012 stemmed from clinging to Symbian, but deeper causes were: the industry shifted from hardware to software excellence; leadership had little knowledge of software; the matrix diffused responsibility so no one could create a new phone; and top leadership was aggressive and didn't want to hear bad news. Mass has inertia; greater mass requires more force to change direction. Successful organizations have found ways of managing size costs, but these difficulties are never solved, only managed.

## Key facts

- Nokia shipped over half of the industry's mobile phones in 2007, and five years later its share had collapsed to under 5 percent. [raw/books/strategy/The Crux - Richard Rumelt.pdf#p207]
- Nokia's matrix structure created problems: conflicts resolved by slow committee meetings, and top management became insulated from truth on the ground. [raw/books/strategy/The Crux - Richard Rumelt.pdf#p208]
- Nokia engineers knew about Apple's finger-touch design plans in late 2005, and CEO Ollila made it a priority, yet no finger-touch phone appeared until 2009. [raw/books/strategy/The Crux - Richard Rumelt.pdf#p209]
- Researchers found that Nokia middle managers and engineers felt bullied into making optimistic promises they knew were false, and top managers gave direction but didn't want to hear bad news. [raw/books/strategy/The Crux - Richard Rumelt.pdf#p209]
- Complex organizations must be simplified before real change can be engineered, through weeding out activities, removing superfluous layers, and breaking large units into smaller ones. [raw/books/strategy/The Crux - Richard Rumelt.pdf#p210]

## Inferences

- Inference: Nokia's fourfold answer to why no one acted: industry shift to software, leadership lacked software knowledge, matrix diffused responsibility, and aggressive leadership style prevented bad news from reaching the top.
