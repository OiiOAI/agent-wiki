# The Hard Problem of Consciousness

## Summary

The hard problem, as articulated by David Chalmers, asks why and how physical brain processes give rise to subjective experience. Solms argues that the hard problem arises from conflating two types of explanatory gaps: (1) between different experiential perspectives, and (2) between experience and its functional mechanisms. He proposes that focusing on the function of feeling rather than cognition may dissolve the hard problem.

## Key facts

- The hard problem asks why physical processing gives rise to a rich inner life and why it seems objectively unreasonable yet does so. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p221]
- Solms argues that the hard problem arises partly from conflating two explanatory gaps: between experiential perspectives and between experience and mechanism. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p231-232]
- The primary function of consciousness is not perceiving or remembering but feeling. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p242]

## Inferences

- Inference: Unlike cognitive processes, feeling cannot occur 'in the dark'—if we explain the function of feeling adequately, this should automatically explain why it feels like something.
