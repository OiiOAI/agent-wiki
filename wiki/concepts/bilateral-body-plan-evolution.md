# Bilateral Body Plan Evolution

## Summary

The evolutionary emergence of bilateral symmetry around 630 million years ago, representing a major transition from radial body plans to organisms with anterior-posterior and dorsal-ventral axes, a head region, and centralized nervous systems.

## Key facts

- Around 630 million years ago, a new body style—bilateral symmetry—appeared, representing organisms with two major axes (front-to-back and top-to-bottom). [raw/books/neuroscience/The Deep History of Ourselves_ 4 - Billion - Year Story of How - Joseph E LeDoux; Caio Da Silva Sorrentino.pdf#p143]
- Bilaterals were the first organisms to possess a head, with sensory organs and brain located at the anterior end, and the direction the head faces is forward—typically the direction of locomotion. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Deep History of Ourselves_ 4 - Billion - Year Story of How - Joseph E LeDoux; Caio Da Silva Sorrentino.pdf#p145]
- Hox genes are major players in Bauplan construction, directing construction along the anterior-posterior axis and guiding development of body parts—a process called anterior-posterior axial patterning. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Deep History of Ourselves_ 4 - Billion - Year Story of How - Joseph E LeDoux; Caio Da Silva Sorrentino.pdf#p146]
- The LCBA is hypothesized to have been a primitive aquatic worm with bilateral body plan that emerged about 630 million years ago, with some later features (nervous system, gut, coelom) acquired by descendants. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Deep History of Ourselves_ 4 - Billion - Year Story of How - Joseph E LeDoux; Caio Da Silva Sorrentino.pdf#p150]
- Free-swimming larvae of cnidarians exhibit some bilateral symmetry and have genes used to direct body development in bilaterals, suggesting the LCBA emerged as a modification of cnidarian larval bodies. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Deep History of Ourselves_ 4 - Billion - Year Story of How - Joseph E LeDoux; Caio Da Silva Sorrentino.pdf#p150]

## Inferences

- Inference: The emergence of the bilateral body plan enabled directed locomotion and predator-prey interactions, which may have driven the Cambrian Explosion of body plan diversity.
