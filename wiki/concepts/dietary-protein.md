# Dietary Protein

## Summary

Protein is the most important macronutrient for healthspan, serving as the essential building block for muscle mass, enzymes, hormones, and immune function. The US RDA of 0.8 g/kg/day is insufficient for thriving; optimal intake is 1.6-2.2 g/kg/day, with elderly individuals potentially requiring more due to anabolic resistance. Protein should be consumed in four servings per day to optimize muscle protein synthesis.

## Key facts

- The US recommended dietary allowance (RDA) for protein is 0.8 g/kg of body weight, which may reflect survival needs but is inadequate for thriving. [raw/books/health/Outlive.pdf#p338]
- Studies show that elderly people consuming the RDA of protein lose muscle mass even in as short a period as two weeks. [raw/books/health/Outlive.pdf#p338]
- The author sets 1.6 g/kg/day as the minimum protein intake for patients, twice the RDA. [raw/books/health/Outlive.pdf#p339]
- For active people with normal kidney function, 1 gram per pound of body weight per day (2.2 g/kg/day) is recommended. [raw/books/health/Outlive.pdf#p339]
- Protein should be consumed in four servings per day, each at approximately 0.25 g/lb of body weight, to avoid losing amino acids to oxidation. [raw/books/health/Outlive.pdf#p339]
- The safe upper limit of protein consumption is 3.7 g/kg/day (1.7 g/lb), defined by kidney stress concerns. [raw/books/health/Outlive.pdf#p339]
- Plant protein has lower bioavailability (60-70%) compared to animal protein due to being tied up in indigestible fiber. [raw/books/health/Outlive.pdf#p340]
- The Healthy Aging and Body Composition Study found that elderly subjects eating the most protein (18% of calories) kept 40% more lean body mass over three years than those in the lowest quintile. [raw/books/health/Outlive.pdf#p340]
- Leucine is particularly important for muscle protein synthesis, with recommendations of 2-3 grams per serving, four times per day for those trying to increase lean mass. [raw/books/health/Outlive.pdf#p340]

## Inferences

- Inference: Low-protein diets shown to extend lifespan in mice may not apply to humans, as mice and humans respond differently to protein restriction.
