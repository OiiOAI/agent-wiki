# Morality of Principles

## Summary

The third and final stage of moral development, in which someone understands the principles of justice and develops a desire to apply and act upon them. The conception of acting justly comes to have an attraction analogous to that possessed before by subordinate ideals.

## Key facts

- The third psychological law states that once the attitudes of love and trust, and of friendly feelings and mutual confidence, have been generated, then the recognition that we and those for whom we care are the beneficiaries of an established and enduring just institution tends to engender in us the corresponding sense of justice. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p420]
- A sense of justice leads us to accept the just institutions that apply to us and from which we and our associates have benefited, and gives rise to a willingness to work for the setting up of just institutions and for the reform of existing ones. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p420]
- The morality of principles takes two forms: one corresponding to the sense of right and justice (not supererogatory), and the other to the love of mankind and to self-command (which is supererogatory). [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p424]

## Inferences

- Inference: Once a morality of principles is accepted, moral attitudes are no longer connected solely with the well-being and approval of particular individuals and groups, but are shaped by a conception of right chosen irrespective of these contingencies.
