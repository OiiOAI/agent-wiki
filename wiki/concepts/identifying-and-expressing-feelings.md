# Identifying and Expressing Feelings

## Summary

The second component of NVC involves expressing how we are feeling. Many people have limited emotional vocabularies and confuse feelings with thoughts. NVC distinguishes between actual feelings and statements that describe thoughts, assessments, or interpretations of others' behavior. Building a robust feelings vocabulary—distinguishing what we feel from what we think—enables clearer communication and stronger connections.

## Key facts

- Psychoanalyst Rollo May suggests that the mature person becomes able to differentiate feelings into as many nuances as in the different passages of music in a symphony. [raw/books/psychology/Nonviolent Communication_ A Language of Li - Marshall B. Rosenberg.pdf#p64]
- Feelings are not clearly expressed when 'feel' is followed by 'that,' 'like,' 'as if,' the pronouns I/you/he/she/they/it, or names referring to people. [raw/books/psychology/Nonviolent Communication_ A Language of Li - Marshall B. Rosenberg.pdf#p69]
- Words like 'unimportant,' 'misunderstood,' and 'ignored' express how we interpret others rather than how we actually feel. [raw/books/psychology/Nonviolent Communication_ A Language of Li - Marshall B. Rosenberg.pdf#p70-71]
- Expressing vulnerability—admitting feelings such as fear—can help resolve conflicts even in professional settings. [raw/books/psychology/Nonviolent Communication_ A Language of Li - Marshall B. Rosenberg.pdf#p67]
- The book provides extensive vocabulary lists for how we feel when needs are being met (e.g., absorbed, buoyant, grateful, peaceful) and when needs are not being met (e.g., afraid, angry, anxious, confused). [raw/books/psychology/Nonviolent Communication_ A Language of Li - Marshall B. Rosenberg.pdf#p73-81]

## Inferences

- Inference: Expressing feelings vulnerably can create dramatic positive outcomes, as demonstrated when an administrator's admission of fear led physicians to reverse their previous vote.
