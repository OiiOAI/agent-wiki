# Birth Dreams

## Summary

Birth dreams are dreams whose manifest content represents phantasies of intrauterine life, existence in the womb, and the act of birth. Freud identifies landscapes or localities emphasized with déjà vu feelings as representing the mother's genitals. Water dreams, dreams of diving, and rescue dreams often serve as birth symbolism. Birth dreams are interpreted by reversing the event reported in the manifest content—for instance, 'diving into water' becomes 'coming out of the water' (being born).

## Key facts

- Dreams of landscapes or localities with a convinced feeling of having been there once before invariably represent the genitals of the dreamer's mother. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p358]
- Many dreams involving narrow spaces or being in water are based upon phantasies of intrauterine life. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p358]
- The interpretation of birth dreams is reached by reversing the event reported in the manifest dream; 'diving into water' means 'coming out of the water,' i.e., being born. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p359]
- Rescue dreams are connected with birth dreams; in women's dreams, to rescue, especially from water, has the same significance as giving birth. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p361]
- Birth phantasies contain an explanation for the remarkable dread that many people have of being buried alive. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p360]

## Inferences

- Inference: The anxiety associated with birth dreams connects to the broader Freudian concept that the act of birth is the first experience of anxiety, serving as the prototype for later anxiety responses.
