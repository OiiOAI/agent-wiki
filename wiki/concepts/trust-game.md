# Trust Game

## Summary

The Trust Game, introduced by Berg, Dickhaut, and McCabe (1995), measures trust and trustworthiness in economic exchange. An investor decides how much to send to a trustee; money is multiplied and the trustee decides how much to return.

## Key facts

- Berg et al. (1995) reported that investors send about 50% of the endowment on average, and trustees generally return the amount sent. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p56]
- Berg et al. (1995) reported that fully 50% of trustees returned $1 or less. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p56]
- McCabe et al. (2001) found medial prefrontal regions more active when subjects played with human counterparts compared to computer counterparts. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p58]
- fMRI studies show that the trustee's caudate activation tracks how much reciprocity the investor showed on previous trials. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p72]
- In the trust game, trustors' minimum acceptance probabilities (MAPs) are substantially higher than in a risky dictator game, indicating more than just risk aversion when a human trustee determines outcomes. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p211]
- Oxytocin-infused subjects are more trusting than placebo controls, though their beliefs about repayment chances are not higher and oxytocin does not affect risk-taking in pure risk conditions. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p218]
- Oxytocin affects trusting behavior only in situations involving social betrayal; it dampens amygdala activity and may reduce fear of betrayal in social interactions. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p218]
- Oxytocin causes specific activity reduction in caudate nucleus, which may explain why OT-treated subjects fail to reduce trusting behavior after learning about trustee betrayals. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p229]

## Inferences

- Inference: Trust behavior activates brain regions associated with theory-of-mind processing, suggesting trust involves inferring others' intentions.
- Inference: Oxytocin appears to limit fear of betrayal in social interactions by dampening amygdala activity, suggesting a specific neuropeptide mechanism for social trust
- Inference: The trust game reveals that trusting involves more than risk assessment; social betrayal aversion is a distinct psychological phenomenon from monetary risk aversion
