# Groupthink

## Summary

Groupthink occurs when a group of smart people makes reckless decisions because everyone aligns their opinions with the supposed consensus. Individual members suppress dissenting views to maintain group harmony, leading to flawed decisions that each person would have rejected individually.

## Key facts

- Groupthink is where a group of smart people makes reckless decisions because everyone aligns their opinions with the supposed consensus, passing motions each individual would have rejected without peer pressure. [raw/books/strategy/The Art of Thinking Clearly _ Better thinking, Better - Rolf Dobelli; Nicky Griffin.pdf#p65]
- The Bay of Pigs invasion was a classic groupthink failure where Kennedy's team completely underestimated Cuba's air force and expected a refuge 100 miles away despite an insurmountable swamp. [raw/books/strategy/The Art of Thinking Clearly _ Better thinking, Better - Rolf Dobelli; Nicky Griffin.pdf#p65]
- To counter groupthink, one should appoint someone as devil's advocate who will question tacit assumptions even if risking expulsion from the group. [raw/books/strategy/The Art of Thinking Clearly _ Better thinking, Better - Rolf Dobelli; Nicky Griffin.pdf#p66]
- Irving Janis introduced the concept of groupthink, examining high-level policy-making cases where choices were made without systematic data gathering or analysis. [raw/books/strategy/The Crux - Richard Rumelt.pdf#p263]
- Group members work to preserve optimism rather than realism and minimize controversy, with important information not examined and alternative courses of action pushed aside. [raw/books/strategy/The Crux - Richard Rumelt.pdf#p263]
- Janis's classic example was President Kennedy's 1961 Bay of Pigs fiasco, where the advisory group did not examine many alternatives. [raw/books/strategy/The Crux - Richard Rumelt.pdf#p264]
- Rumelt argues that it is not group process per se that causes too early convergence on action, but the habits of viewing strategy as setting overall goals or as decision making among predetermined alternative actions. [raw/books/strategy/The Crux - Richard Rumelt.pdf#p264]
- Federal courts data provides 'strong evidence that like-minded judges also go to extremes: the probability that a judge will vote in one or another direction is greatly increased by the presence of judges appointed by the president of the same political party.'. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking_in_Bets.epub#L1375-L1380]
- Supreme Court justices have created their own echo chambers by ceasing to hire clerks with diverse ideological backgrounds, with Justice Thomas hiring 84-for-84 clerks from Republican-appointed judges. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking_in_Bets.epub#L1375-L1380]
- Justice Thomas once said, 'I won't hire clerks who have profound disagreements with me. It's like trying to train a pig. It wastes your time, and it aggravates the pig.'. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking_in_Bets.epub#L1375-L1380]

## Inferences

- Inference: Rumelt extends Janis's analysis by identifying that predetermined outcomes attached to specific actions—rather than poor group dynamics—prevent genuine collective reflection.
