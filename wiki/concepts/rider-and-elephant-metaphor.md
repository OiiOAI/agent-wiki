# Rider and Elephant Metaphor

## Summary

The rider and elephant is a central metaphor for the divided nature of the human mind. The rider represents controlled, conscious processes including language-based reasoning and deliberate decision-making. The elephant represents automatic processes including emotions, intuitions, and the vast majority of mental processing that occurs outside awareness. Haidt argues that the rider evolved to serve the elephant, not to rule it—its function is to help the elephant navigate the social world, manage reputation, and persuade others. The metaphor illustrates why moral arguments are frustrating: you cannot change minds by refuting arguments because the arguments are post hoc rationalizations of intuitions.

## Key facts

- The rider represents conscious, controlled reasoning—the stream of words and images of which we are fully aware—while the elephant represents the other 99 percent of mental processes that occur outside awareness but govern most behavior. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p12]
- The rider's jobs include seeing further into the future, learning new skills, and acting as the elephant's 'full-time public relations firm' to manage reputation in social groups. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p61]
- Haidt first used the metaphor in The Happiness Hypothesis (2006) to describe how rider and elephant work together as humans search for meaning and connection. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p12]
- The metaphor captures Jefferson's model of mind (head and heart as co-rulers) modified to reflect that reason is more like a servant than an equal partner. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p44-45]

## Inferences

- Inference: The metaphor explains why moral arguments rarely convince opponents: you must speak to the elephant through emotional appeals and social influence, not through logical refutation of the rider's arguments.
