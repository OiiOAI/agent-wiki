# Diminishing Sensitivity

## Summary

Diminishing sensitivity is the principle that the psychological difference between two outcomes shrinks as the magnitude of both outcomes increases. It applies to both sensory dimensions and evaluations of monetary changes. For example, the subjective difference between gaining $900 and $1,000 is far smaller than the difference between $100 and $200. In Prospect Theory's value function, this is reflected in the concave shape of the curve for gains and the convex shape for losses.

## Key facts

- Diminishing sensitivity causes a lit bright lamp to have little effect in an already bright room, and similarly the subjective difference between $900 and $1,000 is far smaller than the difference between $100 and $200. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p364]
- Diminishing sensitivity contributes to risk-seeking in the domain of losses: a certain loss of $900 feels more than 90% as bad as a possible loss of $1,000, making the gamble relatively more attractive. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p368]
- The value function in Prospect Theory is concave above the reference point (diminishing sensitivity for gains) and convex below it (diminishing sensitivity for losses), producing the characteristic S-shape. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p365]
