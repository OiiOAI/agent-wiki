# Group Selection and Language Evolution

## Summary

McGilchrist argues that referential language, like music, would have had to be a product of group selection rather than individual selection. The advantages of spoken language would have benefited all individuals in the group, since products of activities like hunting would have been shared. This contrasts with classical natural selection which would require a skill to be hidden or kept secret - but language by its nature is concerned with sharing information.

## Key facts

- Referential language would have been a product of group selection - either everyone in the group develops it and the group benefits, or it doesn't develop. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p180]
- The advantages of spoken language such as more efficient hunting would have benefited all individuals in the group, even if there was a range of development in linguistic skills, since the products would have been shared. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p180]
- Classical natural selection would demand that a skill be positively hidden or kept secret from others - a characteristic incompatible with communication. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p180]
- Human singing is unique: no other creature begins to synchronise the rhythm or blend the pitch of its utterances with that of its fellows. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p180]

## Inferences

- Inference: Language may have started not as the product of ruthless competition, but as another area in which humanity has done better by co-operation and collaboration.
