# Visual Processing

## Summary

Visual processing is the hierarchical neural computation that extracts meaningful information from patterns of light falling on the retina. Beginning with photoreceptors detecting intensity and wavelength, the retina performs contrast enhancement through ON and OFF bipolar cells and retinal ganglion cells. Visual information then proceeds through a hierarchy of cortical areas (V1, V2, V3, V4) that extract increasingly abstract features, ultimately enabling object recognition and spatial mapping.

## Key facts

- The retina extracts ecologically meaningful information from light, with rods sensitive across wavelengths and three types of cones selective for red, green, and blue wavelengths. [raw/books/neuroscience/Free Agents _ How Evolution Gave Us Free Will - Kevin J_ Mitchell.pdf#p124]
- Retinal ganglion cells act as contrast detectors, most strongly activated when light comes from a particular spot while less light comes from its immediate surround. [raw/books/neuroscience/Free Agents _ How Evolution Gave Us Free Will - Kevin J_ Mitchell.pdf#p124]
- V1 contains multiple maps of the visual world maintaining nearest-neighbor relationships from retina through thalamus to cortex. [raw/books/neuroscience/Free Agents _ How Evolution Gave Us Free Will - Kevin J_ Mitchell.pdf#p130]
- Many V1 cells selectively respond only to lines of specific orientation, making them excellent line detectors corresponding to edges of objects. [raw/books/neuroscience/Free Agents _ How Evolution Gave Us Free Will - Kevin J_ Mitchell.pdf#p130]
- Perception involves an updating of internal models based on comparison of incoming sensory information with top-down expectations. [raw/books/neuroscience/Free Agents _ How Evolution Gave Us Free Will - Kevin J_ Mitchell.pdf#p134]
- Vision creates a three-dimensional representation of the world from two-dimensional retinal images. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p608]
- The brain parses scenes into distinct components, separating foreground from background using previously learned rules about the structure of the world. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p608]
- The visual system processes sensory information according to computational rules that derive in part from experience and in part from built-in neural wiring. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p609]
- Gestalt organizational rules include similarity, proximity, and good continuation. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p609]
- A ventral pathway into the temporal lobe carries information about what the stimulus is. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p611]
- A dorsal pathway into the parietal lobe carries information about where the stimulus is, information critical for guiding movement. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p611]

## Inferences

- Inference: The visual system essentially solves an inverse problem by making inferences about what is causing detected light patterns.
- Inference: The constructive nature of visual perception, where the brain actively organizes visual elements using Gestalt principles and prior knowledge, explains phenomena like visual illusions and context-dependent perception.
