# Spatial Memory

## Summary

Spatial memory is the form of explicit memory that allows animals and humans to navigate and remember locations in their environment. It critically depends on the hippocampus and the spatial maps formed by place cells. The hippocampus is activated during imagined travel, and studies of London taxi drivers show enlarged hippocampi after years of spatial navigation training.

## Key facts

- Birds in which spatial memory is particularly important—those that store food at a large number of sites—have a larger hippocampus than other birds. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p315]
- London taxi drivers must demonstrate knowledge of every street name in London and the most efficient routes, and fMRI revealed after two years of this training they have a larger hippocampus than other persons the same age. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p316]
- The size of the London taxi drivers' hippocampus continues to increase with time on the job. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p316]
- Brain-imaging studies show the hippocampus is activated during imagined travel when a taxi driver is asked to recall how to get to a particular destination. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p316]
- Richard Morris showed that by blocking the NMDA receptor pharmacologically, one could block long-term potentiation and interfere with spatial memory. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p300]
- Kandel and Grant found that knocking out a particular gene encoding a protein important for LTP compromised synaptic strengthening in the Schaffer collateral pathway and correlated with a defect in the mouse's spatial memory. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p301]
