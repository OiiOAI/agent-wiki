# Regret and Moral Responsibility

## Summary

Loss aversion and anticipated regret create powerful resistance to trades that involve exchanging safety or health for money, even when the exchange is economically rational. These 'taboo trade-offs' (such as accepting a small increase in health risk for additional income) are viewed as morally problematic because they violate the sacred status of health and safety, triggering anticipated regret and social blame.

## Key facts

- Participants required a median payment of 50 times the amount they would pay for a vaccine preventing the same 1-in-1,000 health risk—demonstrating that the act of selling health (rather than buying protection) triggers a qualitatively different and far stronger aversion driven by anticipated regret and sense of responsibility. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p452]
- Over two-thirds of parents stated they would not switch to a cheaper, slightly riskier pesticide at any price, treating any increase in child safety risk as a non-negotiable loss even when the cost savings could be used to purchase other safety improvements. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p452]
- The precautionary principle in European and international law embeds this asymmetry: it places the burden of proof on those taking actions that could cause harm, and has been estimated by Cass Sunstein to have blocked innovations including aircraft, air conditioning, antibiotics, automobiles, chlorine, heart surgery, radio, X-rays, and smallpox vaccine if applied in its strongest form. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p453]

## Inferences

- Inference: The taboo trade-off phenomenon reveals that moral intuitions about sanctity and reference points can override economic rationality, and that the conflict between deep loss aversion and efficient risk management has no simple resolution in policy.
