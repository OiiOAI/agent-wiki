# Theory of Constructed Emotion

## Summary

Barrett's theory of constructed emotion proposes that emotions are not triggered but are actively made by the brain. In every waking moment, the brain uses past experience organized as concepts to guide actions and give sensations meaning. When the concepts involved are emotion concepts, the brain constructs instances of emotion. Emotions vary from culture to culture, emerge from combinations of bodily properties, a flexible brain, and cultural upbringing, and are real as social reality rather than objective physical phenomena like molecules.

## Inferences

- Inference: Because emotions are constructed rather than triggered, interventions that target prediction and concept formation may be more effective than those targeting emotion detection.
- Inference: The theory explains why emotion recognition across cultures is unreliable—because there is no universal biological fingerprint for any emotion, only culturally learned concepts.
- Inference: The theory challenges the basic emotion theory's assumption that emotions have universal biological signatures.
- Inference: The theory emphasizes the role of prediction and interoception in constructing emotional experiences.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The exact neural mechanisms of how prediction and categorization produce the subjective feeling of emotion require further elucidation.
