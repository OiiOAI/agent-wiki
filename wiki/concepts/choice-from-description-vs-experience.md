# Choice from Description vs. Choice from Experience

## Summary

Choices made from description involve selecting between explicitly described prospects (as in most textbook decision problems), while choices from experience involve learning about prospects through repeated sampling. These two modes follow different psychological rules: the possibility effect (overweighting small probabilities) is robust in choice from description but absent or reversed in choice from experience, where rare events may be ignored if never experienced. This distinction explains why people underrespond to rare risks like environmental threats or financial crises.

## Key facts

- In choice from description, participants were shown explicit probability descriptions (e.g., '5% chance of $12') and showed the possibility effect; in choice from experience, participants observed outcomes from repeated button presses and did not overweight small probabilities—they showed no possibility effect. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p427]
- Rare events receive less than appropriate weighting in choice from experience when people have not personally experienced them: most California residents have never experienced a major earthquake, and no banker in 2007 had personally experienced a catastrophic financial crisis, leading to systematic underweighting of these risks. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p429]
- When a rare event is highly salient (explicitly described, emotionally vivid, or concretely represented as a frequency), it receives excessive decision weight even in real-world decisions, as seen in the disproportionate fear of terrorism relative to statistically greater risks like car accidents. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p430]
- Hertwig and Erev noted that the underweighting of rare events in choice from experience contributes to the weak public response to long-term environmental threats: the events are rare enough that most people have no personal experience of them. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p429]

## Inferences

- Inference: The description-experience gap has important policy implications: public health campaigns may be more effective when they describe risks explicitly (leveraging the possibility effect) than when they rely on people to learn from environmental statistics, which people may never personally experience.
