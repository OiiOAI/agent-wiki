# How to Run a Panel Discussion

## Summary

Panel discussions usually fail unnecessarily due to boring opening statements and lack of moderator preparation. To succeed, seat panelists at tables in an open-U configuration so the audience feels like they are witnessing a conversation. The moderator should introduce each panelist briefly, forbid opening statements, prepare controversial questions, remove lecterns and soft seating, limit discussions to one hour, and offer a summary at the end.

## Key facts

- Panel discussions usually fail unnecessarily due to boring opening statements, long-winded introductions, and lack of moderator preparation. [raw/books/strategy/Make it Clear - Patrick Henry Winston.pdf#p355]
- Seat panelists at three tables in an open-U configuration so conversations feel natural among four to six people. [raw/books/strategy/Make it Clear - Patrick Henry Winston.pdf#p355]
- Introduce each panelist yourself in a sentence or two explaining why their opinions matter; if you say more, the audience will tune out. [raw/books/strategy/Make it Clear - Patrick Henry Winston.pdf#p356]
- Forbid opening statements because panelists will agonize over stump speeches, use too many slides with too many words, and everything becomes tense. [raw/books/strategy/Make it Clear - Patrick Henry Winston.pdf#p356]
- Prepare questions that stimulate debate and controversy—ask why work is going slowly, why someone is wrong, or about dangers in what is being done. [raw/books/strategy/Make it Clear - Patrick Henry Winston.pdf#p358]
- Remove lecterns and avoid soft chairs; people sink into chairs wondering how they look, which kills confidence. [raw/books/strategy/Make it Clear - Patrick Henry Winston.pdf#p357]
- Limit panel discussions to one hour; ninety minutes is generally too much. [raw/books/strategy/Make it Clear - Patrick Henry Winston.pdf#p358]
- Offer a summary when the allotted time is up, telling people what they learned. [raw/books/strategy/Make it Clear - Patrick Henry Winston.pdf#p358]
