# Neural Regeneration in the CNS

## Summary

Central and peripheral nerves differ substantially in their ability to regenerate after injury. Peripheral nerves can often be repaired, with axons sprouting from the proximal stump and growing along the distal stump toward targets. In contrast, central nervous system regeneration is poor—proximal stumps form retraction bulbs that fail to progress. This failure results from multiple factors including insufficient growth-promoting factors, growth-inhibitory factors in myelin, impenetrable glial scars, and intrinsic reluctance of adult central axons to grow.

## Key facts

- Peripheral nerves can often be repaired following injury because Schwann cells promote axonal growth by producing trophic and attractant factors and expressing adhesive proteins. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p1339]
- In the central nervous system, the proximal stumps of damaged axons form short sprouts that stall and form swollen endings called retraction bulbs that fail to progress. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p1340]
- Key barriers to CNS regeneration include insufficient growth-promoting factors, growth-inhibitory factors in myelin, impenetrable glial scars, and intrinsic reluctance of adult central axons to grow. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p1354]

## Inferences

- Inference: The limited regenerative capacity of mature mammals may be a tradeoff for the precise wiring diagrams required for superior intellectual functions, since the brain must remodel its connections during critical periods in early postnatal life
