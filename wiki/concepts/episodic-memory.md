# Episodic Memory

## Summary

Episodic memory is a uniquely human memory system that allows mental time travel through subjective time, enabling recollection of one's own previous experiences and imagination of possible future experiences, mediated by autonoetic awareness.

## Key facts

- Episodic memory is oriented to the past and allows mental time travel through subjective time to the past, present, and future. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p311]
- Episodic memory requires autonoetic awareness, the ability to focus attention directly on one's own subjective experiences. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p312]
- Episodic memory always includes the self as the agent or recipient of an action. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p312]
- Children develop reliable episodic memory abilities by ages three to four, explaining the lack of autobiographical memory from early years. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p313]
- Henry could talk about drives along the Mohawk Trail with his parents in general terms but could not provide details of any specific trip. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p220]
- When asked about his favorite memory of his mother, Henry could only say 'Well, I—that she's my mother' without any specific episode. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p218]
- Henry recalled only two autobiographical episodes from his preoperative life with rich experiential detail: his first cigarette and a plane ride at age thirteen. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p226]
- His two retained memories involved powerful emotions that strongly activated the hippocampus, prefrontal cortex, and amygdala during encoding. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p228]
- Endel Tulving coined the term episodic memory in 1972 to describe remembering that allows us to call back and reexperience events from the past. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p37]
- Tulving proposed that to remember an event (episodic memory), we need to mentally return to a specific place and time. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p37]
- Episodic memory is not designed to capture common elements of experiences; it stores and indexes every event differently to prevent mixing up memories. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p40]
- Research on patients with developmental amnesia (hippocampal damage) showed they could acquire semantic knowledge but had severe episodic memory deficits. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p42]
- The reminiscence bump refers to the predominance of memories from ages 10 to 30 in autobiographical recall. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p52]

## Inferences

- Inference: Semantic memory appears earlier in development than episodic memory, with the latter requiring a more mature brain.
- Inference: The hippocampus is necessary for re-experiencing unique moments in one's past, regardless of how long ago the events occurred.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The exact developmental timeline of episodic memory in individual children varies.
