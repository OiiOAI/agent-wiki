# Ambiguity Aversion

## Summary

Ambiguity aversion refers to the preference for known risks over unknown risks (ambiguity), as demonstrated in Ellsberg's paradox. Decision theory models like α-maxmin utility capture this by weighting best and worst-case scenarios. Neuroimaging studies show different neural responses to ambiguity versus pure risk, with the amygdala and lateral orbitofrontal cortex showing differential activation in ambiguous situations.

## Key facts

- Ambiguity aversion is a stylized fact in decision experiments where many individuals prefer known probabilities over unknown ones (Ellsberg, 1961). [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p360]
- The α-maxmin utility model generalizes maxmin expected utility by allowing a mixture of worst-case and best-case scenarios, with α > 0.5 indicating ambiguity aversion. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p361]
- Hsu et al. (2005) found the striatum to be differentially activated under risk relative to ambiguity, whereas amygdala and lateral orbitofrontal cortex showed the reverse pattern. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p362]
- The Ellsberg Paradox: most people choose Box A (known distribution) for both red and black draws, yet if they assumed Box B had fewer red balls, they should choose it for black — logical inconsistency. [raw/books/strategy/The Art of Thinking Clearly _ Better thinking, Better - Rolf Dobelli; Nicky Griffin.pdf#p177]
- Risk means probabilities are known; uncertainty means probabilities are unknown. 'There is a 30% risk you will die of cancer' makes sense; '30% chance the euro will collapse' does not. [raw/books/strategy/The Art of Thinking Clearly _ Better thinking, Better - Rolf Dobelli; Nicky Griffin.pdf#p178]
- The amygdala's build determines how easily you tolerate uncertainty; the more averse you are, the more conservatively you vote. [raw/books/strategy/The Art of Thinking Clearly _ Better thinking, Better - Rolf Dobelli; Nicky Griffin.pdf#p178]
