# Rejection-then-Retreat Technique

## Summary

The rejection-then-retreat technique (also called the foot-in-the-door technique variant) exploits reciprocity by presenting a large request that will be refused, then retreating to a smaller request that appears as a concession. The technique engages both reciprocity and contrast principles simultaneously, producing compliance through the target's felt obligation to reciprocate the apparent concession while perceiving the second request as smaller than it actually is.

## Key facts

- The rejection-then-retreat technique structures requests as a larger one followed by a smaller one, making the second request appear as a concession to be reciprocated. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p41]
- Cialdini's experiment with the Boy Scout showed that after refusing $5 tickets and being offered $1 chocolate bars instead, he purchased the candy even though he disliked chocolate and liked dollars. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p39]
- The County Youth Counseling experiment showed 83% refused a zoo chaperone request, but asking for two years of counseling first (which all refused) tripled compliance to 50% when followed by the smaller zoo request. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p42]
- The technique is effective only up to a point—Bar-Ilan University research showed that if the first request is too extreme and seen as unreasonable, the tactic backfires. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p42]
- Television producers Grant Tinker and Gary Marshall admitted to deliberately inserting lines they knew censors would cut, then retreating to lines they actually wanted. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p43]
- G. Gordon Liddy's three proposals for Watergate ($1 million, $500,000, $250,000) represent a documented case of the rejection-then-retreat technique, with the $250,000 plan approved despite being still ill-conceived. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p46]
- The technique cannot fail—requesting $10 first means getting $10 if accepted, or successfully retreating to $5 if refused, engaging both reciprocity and contrast simultaneously. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p47]
- Victims of the technique actually fulfill their agreements at higher rates (85%) than those asked directly (50%), and agree to future requests more often (84% vs 43%). [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p49]
- Targets of the technique feel more responsible for the final agreement and are more satisfied with it, explaining why they follow through despite being manipulated. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p50]

## Inferences

- Inference: The technique's double victory—feeling responsible while actually being manipulated—explains why victims don't resent the manipulation and continue compliant behavior.
- Inference: The Watergate example suggests the technique can influence even experienced politicians operating under significant scrutiny.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The long-term psychological effects on repeated targets of rejection-then-retreat tactics are not documented in this text.
