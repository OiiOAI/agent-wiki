# Dominant End

## Summary

A dominant end is a single end to which all other ends are subordinate, such that deliberation always concerns means to this end and all lesser ends are ordered as means to the one dominant end. Rawls examines why this conception is appealing—it would make rational choice perfectly clear by eliminating the problem of balancing competing aims. However, he argues that no such dominant end can be rationally justified: happiness is not a dominant but rather an inclusive end; specific goals like political power or wealth, when pursued as dominant ends, are inhuman and fanatical. Human good is heterogeneous because the aims of the self are heterogeneous.

## Key facts

- A dominant end is an end to which all other ends are subordinate, such that all lesser ends are ordered as means to this one end. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p488]
- The dominant-end theorist wants a method of choice which the agent himself can always follow: a first-person procedure that is generally applicable and guaranteed to lead to the best result. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p488]
- Happiness itself cannot be the dominant end, since it is attained by executing a rational plan already set out independently; it is an inclusive end. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p489]
- Human good is heterogeneous because the aims of the self are heterogeneous, making a single dominant end rationally implausible. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p490]
- To subordinate all aims to one end disfigures the self and puts it in the service of one of its ends for the sake of system. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p490]
