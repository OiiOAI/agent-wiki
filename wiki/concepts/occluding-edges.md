# Occluding Edges

## Summary

Occluding edges are the boundaries where one surface hides another from a point of observation. They arise from the opacity of surfaces and the clutter of the environment. Two types exist: sharp apical occluding edges (from polyhedral objects) and rounded occluding edges (the brow of curved convexities). At occluding edges, both the covered and covering surfaces are specified—the separation of hidden and unhidden surfaces at edges is specified by perspective structure, while their connection is specified by invariant structure.

## Key facts

- Occluding edges separate the optically uncovered surface from the optically covered surface of an object, and at the same time connect them—they separate and connect simultaneously. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p109]
- Sharp occluding edges are apical (at the apex of a convex dihedral) and do not slide along the surface as the point of observation moves, while rounded occluding edges slide along the surface. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p103]
- An occluding edge always requires a convexity of some sort—a protrusion of substance into the medium. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p103]
- At occluding edges, progressive deletion of optical structure occurs at the leading edge and progressive accretion at the trailing edge, specifying the motion of the object. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p106]

## Inferences

- Inference: The dual nature of occluding edges (separating and connecting) resolves the apparent paradox of perceiving hidden surfaces—not by seeing them directly, but by perceiving the systematic relationship between surfaces revealed at different viewpoints.
