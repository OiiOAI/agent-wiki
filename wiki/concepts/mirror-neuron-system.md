# Mirror Neuron System

## Summary

The mirror neuron system (MNS) refers to neurons that fire both when performing an action and when observing another perform the same action. Originally discovered in macaque premotor cortex, analogous systems have been identified in humans. The MNS is proposed to enable action understanding by providing a shared representation between self and other, and has been extended to suggest possible roles in empathy through shared affective representations.

## Key facts

- Mirror neurons fire both when a monkey performs a hand movement and when it observes another monkey or human performing the same hand movement. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p256]
- In humans, shared neuronal representations for observation and execution of actions include supplementary motor area, pre-SMA, premotor cortex, supramarginal gyrus, intraparietal sulcus, and superior parietal lobe. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p256]
- The mirror neuron system may provide automatic simulation of others' actions to enable understanding of their intentions and goals. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p256]

## Inferences

- Inference: The mirror neuron system has been extended from action understanding to empathy, with proposals that shared representations may underlie our ability to understand others' feelings, not just their actions
- Inference: Some researchers debate whether mirror systems can explain higher-order mentalizing about beliefs, suggesting they may be limited to understanding concrete actions and action-related intentions rather than abstract mental states

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The exact scope of the mirror neuron system's role in social cognition remains debated; it may be limited to action understanding rather than abstract belief reasoning
