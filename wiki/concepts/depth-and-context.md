# Depth and Context in Perception

## Summary

McGilchrist argues that depth in vision—distinct from mere distance—is essential for genuine perception and connection with reality. When the left hemisphere's focused attention sheers away context, it produces something lifeless and mechanical. Descartes' famous window example shows this: seeing 'hats and coats' without the living person beneath represents the failure of depth. Ruskin demonstrated that 'clarity' describes a type of knowledge (knowing what something is) rather than a degree of perception. Depth situates us in the same world as others and allows us to 'feel across' intervening space; its loss is a feature of both the Cartesian view and schizophrenia.

## Key facts

- Descartes, looking from a window and seeing men pass, wrote that he judged they were men but saw 'nothing but hats and coats which could conceal automatons'. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p258]
- Ruskin argued that 'clarity' describes not a degree of perception but a type of knowledge—we see clearly only what we need to identify something. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p259]
- Heidegger wrote that 'the world becomes picture' in modernity, and what is exists only to the extent that it is set up by man who represents and sets forth. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p259]
- Depth never implies detachment; it brings us into relationship with the other. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p260]
- In schizophrenia there is a loss of grip on the frame of reference and loss of the transparency that operates in normal understanding. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p260]
