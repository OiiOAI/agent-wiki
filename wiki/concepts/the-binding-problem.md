# The Binding Problem

## Summary

The binding problem refers to the difficulty of knowing how the various modular elements of brain function come to be united in the experience of the self. McGilchrist argues this is largely a problem created by the model of mind derived from left-hemisphere self-conscious mechanisms that identify parts of a living whole, then wonder how parts can be put together. Panksepp's vision of consciousness as a tree provides a neurological answer: what look like 'modules' are better seen as branches of a tree. Experience is already coherent in its wholeness at very low levels in the brain.

## Key facts

- The binding problem refers to the difficulty of knowing how various modular elements of brain function come to be united in the experience of the self. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p317]
- Experience is not a stitching together at the topmost level of a 'patchwork' of functions; experience is already coherent in its wholeness at very low levels in the brain. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p317]
- Ramachandran's experiments 'flatly contradict the theory that the brain consists of a number of autonomous modules acting as a bucket brigade', showing that connections are extraordinarily labile and dynamic. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p317]

## Inferences

- Inference: The 'modules' identified by cognitive psychology are better seen as branches of a tree; higher levels permit the growth of a unified whole rather than putting together bits.
