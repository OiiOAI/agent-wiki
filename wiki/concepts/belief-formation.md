# Belief Formation

## Summary

How humans actually form abstract beliefs differs dramatically from how we think we form them. The perceived process (hear → think about it → vet it → form belief) is replaced by the actual process (hear → believe it to be true → only sometimes, later, if we have time or inclination, think about it and vet it). Research by Daniel Gilbert demonstrated that our default is to believe what we hear and read is true, even when information is presented as false. This was shaped by evolution toward efficiency rather than accuracy.

## Key facts

- Harvard psychologist Daniel Gilbert concluded that people are credulous creatures who find it very easy to believe and very difficult to doubt. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking_in_Bets.epub#L600-L603]
- Gilbert's 1993 experiments showed that under any sort of pressure (time pressure or cognitive load), subjects presumed all statements were true regardless of their labeling. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking_in_Bets.epub#L610-L615]
- Abstract belief formation is likely among the few things that are uniquely human, making it relatively new in evolutionary time. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking_in_Bets.epub#L625-L628]
- Evolution favored false positives over false negatives for survival-essential skills—a lion in the grass is safer to assume is real. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking_in_Bets.epub#L630-L635]
- Many commonly held beliefs are untrue (baldness from maternal grandfather, dog age multiplied by seven, Abner Doubleday inventing baseball, using only 10% of our brain). [raw/books/strategy/Thinking_in_Bets.epub#L640-L650]

## Inferences

- Inference: The author argues that our belief-formation process was shaped by evolution for direct sensory experience, and when complex language evolved, we applied the same 'believe first, question later' system to secondhand information.
