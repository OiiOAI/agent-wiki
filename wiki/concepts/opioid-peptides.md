# Opioid Peptides

## Summary

Opioid peptides include endorphins, enkephalins, and dynorphins—three distinct neurochemical families that bind to mu, delta, and kappa receptors respectively. They mediate pleasure, pain modulation, and social-affective processes.

## Key facts

- Three distinct opioid peptide families exist: endorphins, enkephalins, and dynorphins, each derived from larger peptides synthesized by specific genes. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p62]
- Opioid peptides act on three receptor types: mu, delta, and kappa receptors, with morphine and heroin mimicking endorphin effects at mu receptors. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p62]
- B-endorphin counteracts homeostatic imbalances and negative emotional stimuli, creating pleasure and counters stress responses. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p65]
- Blocking mu receptors with naloxone or naltrexone blocks the rewarding effects of opiates and can reveal opioid involvement in social preferences and food choices. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p62]
