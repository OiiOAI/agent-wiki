# Moral Worth

## Summary

Moral worth is the third fundamental concept of ethics alongside the right and the good. A person of moral worth has broadly based properties of moral character that it is rational for members of a well-ordered society to want in one another. The fundamental virtues are among these broadly based properties.

## Key facts

- A good person has to a higher degree than the average the broadly based features of moral character that it is rational for persons in the original position to want in one another. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p389]
- The fundamental moral virtues—the strong and normally effective desires to act on the basic principles of right—are among the broadly based properties. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p388]
- The moral virtues must be distinguished from natural assets (intelligence, imagination, strength): the virtues are sentiments leading us to act on principles of right, while natural assets are developed capacities. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p389]
- The full theory of the good covers judgments of moral worth by using the principles of justice derived from the original position. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p387]

## Inferences

- Inference: The extension of goodness as rationality to moral worth requires the prior establishment of principles of justice, which is achieved through the original position.
