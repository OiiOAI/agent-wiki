# Blindsight

## Summary

Blindsight is a disorder in which patients with lesions in the primary visual cortex claim to see nothing in the blind parts of their visual field, yet when asked to guess, they can detect simple visual properties such as movement or color far better than chance. This demonstrates that visual information can guide behavior without conscious perception.

## Key facts

- Patients with lesions in the primary visual cortex claim to see nothing in the affected part of the visual field but can detect visual properties when guessing. [raw/books/neuroscience/Eric Kandel PRINCIPLES OF NEURAL SCIENCE Sixth Edition - Eric R Kandel; John Koester; Sarah Mack; Steven Siegelbaum.pdf#p1522]
- Despite having no sensory-based perception of objects in blind parts of the visual field, patients possess unconscious information about the objects that is available to guide their behavior. [raw/books/neuroscience/Eric Kandel PRINCIPLES OF NEURAL SCIENCE Sixth Edition - Eric R Kandel; John Koester; Sarah Mack; Steven Siegelbaum.pdf#p1522]
- Weiskrantz showed that patients with visual cortex damage could accurately reach for objects in their blind visual field while denying seeing them. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Deep History of Ourselves_ 4 - Billion - Year Story of How - Joseph E LeDoux; Caio Da Silva Sorrentino.pdf#p265]
- Blindsight demonstrates that stimuli can reach brain systems controlling behavior without supporting conscious perception. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Deep History of Ourselves_ 4 - Billion - Year Story of How - Joseph E LeDoux; Caio Da Silva Sorrentino.pdf#p265]
- The phenomenon was dubbed 'blindsight' — the ability to respond behaviorally to visual stimuli that cannot be reported verbally. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Deep History of Ourselves_ 4 - Billion - Year Story of How - Joseph E LeDoux; Caio Da Silva Sorrentino.pdf#p265]
- Blindsight patients can point to and differentiate between certain simple objects while at the same time denying that they see them. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p187]
- Blindsight is usually caused by fairly extensive damage to the primary visual cortex V1, in many cases only on one side of the head. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p187]
- Patients can point to the active light fairly accurately, usually within 5 or 10 degrees, despite denying they can see anything. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p187]
- Some patients can distinguish simple shapes such as an X from an O, provided they are big enough. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p187]
- The neural pathways involved in blindsight are still unclear; it was originally surmised that information went through the superior colliculus. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p188]
- There are direct though weak pathways from the LGN directly to areas in the cortex beyond V1, such as V4, that may remain sufficiently intact to lead to motor output but not enough for visual awareness. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p188]
- A patient can use some visual information while denying that he sees anything. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p188]

## Inferences

- Inference: Blindsight demonstrates that visual processing can occur without conscious awareness, supporting the view that most cognitive processing never enters consciousness.
- Inference: Blindsight provided early evidence that behavior can be controlled nonconsciously without conscious awareness — foundational for distinguishing conscious from nonconscious processing.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The exact neural pathways involved in blindsight are still unclear.
- Uncertain: Whether the superior colliculus is the sole pathway for blindsight is unlikely given recent experiments showing cone involvement in wavelength responses.
