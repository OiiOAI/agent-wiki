# Mithridatization

## Summary

The result of exposure to sub-lethal doses of a toxic substance in progressively larger quantities, conferring immunity to that poison. Named after King Mithridates IV of Pontus, who reportedly used this method to protect himself from assassination by poisoning. Taleb describes it as a precursor to antifragility, one step below robustness on the path to understanding systems that benefit from harm.

## Key facts

- Mithridates IV of Pontus protected himself against poisoning by ingesting sub-lethal doses of toxic material in progressively larger quantities. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p58]
- The method named Antidotum Mithridatium was so fashionable in Rome that it complicated Emperor Nero's attempts to poison his mother Agrippina, who had herself Mithridatized. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p58]
- Mithridatization is described as a 'starter-antifragility concept'—still at the more modest level of robustness, but on the way to antifragility. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p59]
- Mithridates eventually could not kill himself by poison and had to ask an ally to kill him with a sword, providing the lesson that one cannot be robust against everything. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p58]

## Inferences

- Inference: Mithridatization represents a simple, direct form of benefit from harm at the individual level, serving as an accessible entry point for understanding the more complex antifragility concept.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Historical accuracy of the Mithridates account, as Taleb presents it as legend rather than documented fact.
