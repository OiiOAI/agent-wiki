# Illusion (Psychological)

## Summary

In Freud's psychoanalytic framework, an illusion is distinguished from an error or delusion. Unlike errors, which are mistakes verifiable against reality, illusions are characterized by their motivational origin in wish fulfillment. They need not be false or unrealizable—examples like the alchemists' transmutation of gold proved not impossible. Religious doctrines exemplify illusions: they are derived from human wishes (protection, justice, immortality) rather than from experience or rational thought. This psychological definition allows that some illusions might accidentally be true, but their value lies in fulfilling desires independent of their truth status.

## Key facts

- Freud defines illusion as a belief where wish fulfillment is a prominent factor in motivation, without regard to its relationship to reality. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p6571]
- An illusion differs from an error because the latter is in contradiction with reality, while illusions may be true or false. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p6570]
- Illusions come near to psychiatric delusions in that they are derived from human wishes, but differ in their more complicated structure. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p6570]
- Columbus's belief in a new sea route to the Indies is cited as an example of an illusion driven by wish. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p6570]
