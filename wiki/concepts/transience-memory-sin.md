# Transience (Memory Sin)

## Summary

Transience is the first of the seven memory sins, referring to the gradual loss of memory content over time. It is exemplified by Hermann Ebbinghaus's classic forgetting curve, which shows rapid initial loss followed by more gradual decline. Schacter explores how transience reflects encoding deficiencies, the role of brain structures like the hippocampus and temporal lobe, and strategies for reducing transience through elaboration, retrieval practice, and memory-enhancing techniques.

## Key facts

- Ebbinghaus's forgetting curve demonstrates that approximately 40% of newly learned information is forgotten within 20 minutes, and about 90% is lost within one week without reinforcement. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p378]
- Transience increases with age, with older adults showing accelerated forgetting rates compared to younger adults. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p378]
- The hippocampus plays a critical role in transience, as demonstrated by case studies of amnesic patients with hippocampal damage. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p378]
- Transience can be reduced through retrieval practice, elaboration, and distributed study sessions. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p378]
- Photographing events can reduce memory for the experience itself, demonstrating the cost of cognitive offloading. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p378]

## Inferences

- Inference: Transience may be adaptive, allowing the brain to discard outdated or unnecessary information to make room for new experiences.
