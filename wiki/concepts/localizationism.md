# Localizationism

## Summary

Localizationism is the theory that the brain is made of specialized processing modules, genetically hardwired to perform specific functions in predetermined locations. The related phrase 'one function, one location' means that if a brain part is damaged, the brain cannot reorganize itself or recover that lost function. This view dominated neuroscience but was challenged by Bach-y-Rita's work on sensory substitution and plasticity.

## Key facts

- Localizationism sees the brain as made of parts, each in a preassigned location, each performing a single function, so that if damaged nothing could replace it. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p23]
- The theory originated with Descartes' idea of the brain as a complex machine and culminated in the view of the brain as a computer. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p23]
- Paul Broca first proposed serious localizationism in 1861 when he linked damage in the left frontal lobe to loss of speech. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p27]
- Evidence against localizationism was ignored during its dominance, including Jules Cotard's 1868 finding that children with massive left hemisphere damage could still speak. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p28]
