# Phantom Limb

## Summary

Phantom limb is a phenomenon where people who have lost a limb still perceive it as present and attached to their body. It demonstrates that our perception comes from the brain's model of the body rather than direct sensory input, and that these models can be wrong—perceiving things that don't physically exist.

## Key facts

- People who lose a limb often perceive that the missing limb is still there because the brain's model includes the missing limb and its location. [raw/books/neuroscience/A Thousand Brains _ A New Theory of Intelligence - Jeff Hawkins; Richard Dawkins; Basic Books.pdf#p166]
- Phantom limbs can 'move' into different positions and feel sensations like itching or pain located at particular locations on the nonexistent limb. [raw/books/neuroscience/A Thousand Brains _ A New Theory of Intelligence - Jeff Hawkins; Richard Dawkins; Basic Books.pdf#p166]
- Phantom limb demonstrates that our brain's model can be wrong—perceiving things that don't exist—showing that we live in a simulation of the world created by our brain, not direct perception of reality. [raw/books/neuroscience/A Thousand Brains _ A New Theory of Intelligence - Jeff Hawkins; Richard Dawkins; Basic Books.pdf#p167]
- Phantom limbs are experienced by 95 percent of amputees and often cause chronic phantom pain that persists for a lifetime. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p186]
- The term 'phantom limb' was first proposed by Silas Weir Mitchell, an American physician who treated wounded soldiers at Gettysburg. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p187]
- Phantom limbs are often very lively entities, with patients feeling their lost limbs gesticulating, waving, or reaching for objects. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p187]
- Some leg amputees experience orgasms in their phantom legs and feet during sex because genital maps invade foot maps. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p190]
- Some women who have had mastectomies experience sexual excitement when their ears, clavicles, and sternums are stimulated, due to proximity with nipples on the brain map. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p191]
- About half of amputees feel their phantom limbs are frozen, hanging in a fixed paralyzed position, or encased in cement. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p191]
- Some patients experience phantom memories of gangrene, ingrown toenails, blisters, and cuts felt in the limb before amputation, especially if that pain existed at the time of amputation. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p191]
- When grenades blow up in soldiers' hands, they can develop phantom pain that endlessly repeats the excruciating moment of the explosion. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p191]
- Patients who had their arms in slings or casts before amputation often develop phantoms frozen in the position of the arm just prior to amputation. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p191]
- Phantom pain may result from the motor command center sending movement commands that receive no feedback, causing the brain to escalate commands and create pain sensations. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p192]

## Inferences

- Inference: Phantom limb proves that perception is constructive—the brain generates our experience of the body based on internal models, not direct sensory data.
- Inference: Phantom limbs are a class of 'phantom pains' that also include postoperative pains after organ removal, such as menstrual cramps after hysterectomy or bladder pain after cystectomy.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The neural mechanisms that lead to phantom limb sensation and why some amputees experience more pain than others are not fully understood.
