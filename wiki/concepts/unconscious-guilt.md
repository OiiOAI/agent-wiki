# Unconscious Guilt

## Summary

Unconscious guilt is a sense of guilt that is not experienced consciously by the ego but produces powerful effects and operates as a critical agency. Freud introduced this concept to explain the negative therapeutic reaction and certain forms of neurosis. The unconscious sense of guilt is a product of the superego and represents the most powerful obstacle to recovery in psychoanalytic treatment.

## Key facts

- There are people in whom the faculties of self-criticism and conscience are unconscious and unconsciously produce effects of the greatest importance. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p5912]
- In a great number of neuroses an unconscious sense of guilt plays a decisive economic part and puts the most powerful obstacles in the way of recovery. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p5913]
- The sense of guilt expressed itself only as a resistance to recovery; the patient feels ill rather than guilty. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p5934]
- A great part of the sense of guilt must normally remain unconscious because the origin of conscience is intimately linked with the Oedipus complex which belongs to the unconscious. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p5936]
- In melancholia the ego gives itself up because it feels itself hated and persecuted by the superego instead of loved; living means being loved by the superego. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p5942]
- The need for punishment behaves like a piece of conscience, like a prolongation of conscience into the unconscious, and corresponds to aggressiveness that has been internalized and taken over by the superego. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p6940]
- The unconscious sense of guilt is satisfied by the suffering linked to the neurosis and for that reason holds fast to being ill. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p6940]
- When the superego was first instituted, the child's aggressiveness towards his parents was used in equipping that agency; for this reason the severity of the superego need not simply correspond to the strictness of the upbringing. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p6941]
- People with excessive unconscious sense of guilt betray themselves by the negative therapeutic reaction, in which being ill with its sufferings and impediments is exactly what is wanted. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p6941]
- Masochism is characterized as truly feminine, since the suppression of women's aggressiveness favors the development of powerful masochistic impulses which succeed in binding erotically the destructive trends diverted inwards. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p6946]

## Inferences

- Inference: The concept of unconscious guilt helps explain why some individuals become criminals: a powerful sense of guilt existed before the crime and was its motive rather than its result, as it was a relief to fasten this unconscious sense of guilt onto something real and immediate.
- Inference: The superego uses the child's aggressiveness toward parents when equipping that agency, because the child could not effect outward discharge due to erotic fixation and external difficulties.
