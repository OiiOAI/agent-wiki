# Self-Serving Bias

## Summary

Self-serving bias is the tendency to attribute success to ourselves and failures to external factors. In SAT tests, students boost their scores by around 50 points when asked a year later. Five roommates overestimated their trash-removal contributions to total 320%.

## Key facts

- We attribute success to ourselves and failures to external factors; if a company has an excellent year, the CEO catalogs his indispensable contributions, but a miserable year is blamed on exchange rates and government interference. [raw/books/strategy/The Art of Thinking Clearly _ Better thinking, Better - Rolf Dobelli; Nicky Griffin.pdf#p107]
- In SAT tests, when asked their results a year later, students tend to boost their scores by around 50 points. [raw/books/strategy/The Art of Thinking Clearly _ Better thinking, Better - Rolf Dobelli; Nicky Griffin.pdf#p108]
- Five roommates were asked how often they take out the trash; their self-estimated contributions totaled 320% instead of 100%. [raw/books/strategy/The Art of Thinking Clearly _ Better thinking, Better - Rolf Dobelli; Nicky Griffin.pdf#p108]
- Self-serving bias was identified by psychologist Fritz Heider, who studied how people make luck and skill attributions, noting we study outcomes like 'naïve scientists' looking for plausible reasons that also fit our wishes. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking_in_Bets.epub#L900-L906]
- In 75% of auto accident accounts, victims blamed someone else for their injuries; in multiple-vehicle accidents, 91% of drivers blamed someone else; in single-vehicle accidents, 37% still blamed someone else. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking_in_Bets.epub#L915-L921]
- Phil Hellmuth, the biggest winner in World Series of Poker history, famously said 'If it weren't for luck, I'd win every one,' exemplifying self-serving bias. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking_in_Bets.epub#L930-L938]
- Self-serving bias causes us to miss opportunities to examine our decisions after bad outcomes and reinforce decisions that shouldn't be reinforced after good outcomes. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking_in_Bets.epub#L960-L966]
- Self-serving bias causes people to attribute successes to internal factors (skill, ability) and failures to external factors (bad luck, circumstances). [raw/books/strategy/Thinking_in_Bets.epub#L2155-L2175]
- Robert Trivers's theory suggests self-serving bias may have evolved as a deception mechanism toward others while maintaining self-deception. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking_in_Bets.epub#L2155-L2175]
- Self-serving bias distorts learning from experience when luck intervenes in outcomes. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking_in_Bets.epub#L2155-L2175]
- The bias is related to but distinct from the fundamental attribution error. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking_in_Bets.epub#L2155-L2175]

## Inferences

- Inference: The pattern of taking credit for good outcomes and blaming bad outcomes on luck is 'predictably irrational,' meaning it follows consistent patterns that can be anticipated and addressed rather than being random or individual.
- Inference: Reducing self-serving bias requires explicitly accounting for the role of luck when evaluating decision outcomes.
