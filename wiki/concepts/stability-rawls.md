# Stability

## Summary

Stability refers to whether a conception of justice can generate its own supportive moral attitudes. A conception is more stable than another if the sense of justice it generates is stronger and more likely to overcome disruptive inclinations, and if its encouraged aims foster weaker impulses toward injustice. Rawls argues justice as fairness is more stable than alternatives.

## Key facts

- One conception of justice is more stable than another if the sense of justice it tends to generate is stronger and more likely to override disruptive inclinations. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p404]
- Stability depends upon a balance of motives: the sense of justice that a conception cultivates and the aims it encourages must normally win out against propensities toward injustice. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p404]
- The persons in the original position will adopt the more stable scheme of principles, other things equal. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p405]
- The stability criterion is not decisive; Bentham held both utility and psychological egoism, making stable justice impossible. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p405]
