# Scarcity Principle

## Summary

The scarcity principle states that opportunities seem more valuable when their availability is limited. Compliance professionals exploit this through limited-number tactics, deadline tactics, and appeals to exclusive information. The principle derives power from learned shortcuts and from psychological reactance against lost freedoms.

## Key facts

- "Limited number" tactics inform customers that products are in short supply that cannot be guaranteed to last. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p214]
- One appliance store sold 30-50 percent of "sold out" items by creating urgency around the last available unit, then "finding" more stock in the back room. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p215]
- "Deadline" tactics use time limits to generate interest where none may have existed, as when a theater advertised "Exclusive, limited engagement ends soon!". [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p216]
- Boiler-room operations use scarcity by first dangling an investment opportunity, then telling customers it is no longer possible to invest, then offering a final chance—creating urgency to "buy quickly without thinking.". [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p217]
- In a cookie study, identical cookies rated as more desirable, more attractive, and more costly when from a jar of two versus ten cookies. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p230]

## Inferences

- Inference: Exclusive information is especially persuasive—beef customers who learned of scarcity through "exclusive contacts" purchased six times more than those receiving only standard sales pitches, demonstrating a scarcity double whammy effect.
