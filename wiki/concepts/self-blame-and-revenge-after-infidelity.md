# Self-Blame and Revenge After Infidelity

## Summary

This chunk examines the two primary emotional responses to discovering a partner's infidelity: self-blame (turning the dagger inward) and revenge (directing anger outward). It explores the psychological logic of self-blame, the various forms revenge can take, the futility of revenge as documented by behavioral science, and the distinction between retributive and restorative justice in the context of intimate betrayal.

## Key facts

- Perel describes betrayal as involving 'a dagger sharp at both ends' that can be turned inward (self-blame) or outward (revenge). [raw/books/psychology/The State of Affairs - Esther Perel.pdf#p97]
- Buddy, upon discovering his wife's infidelity, experienced suicidal ideation while simultaneously blaming her: 'You see what you made me do?'. [raw/books/psychology/The State of Affairs - Esther Perel.pdf#p98]
- Perel distinguishes between taking responsibility for relational conditions that may have contributed to an affair and blaming oneself for the affair itself, which she considers a confusion that occurs in states of shock. [raw/books/psychology/The State of Affairs - Esther Perel.pdf#p99]
- Behavioral scientists have observed that revenge often keeps the unpleasantness of an offense alive; those who cannot exact a penalty tend to move on faster. [raw/books/psychology/The State of Affairs - Esther Perel.pdf#p102]
- Psychologist Steven Stosny states that 'bouts of anger and resentment always drop you down lower than the point at which they picked you up.'. [raw/books/psychology/The State of Affairs - Esther Perel.pdf#p107]
- Perel distinguishes retributive justice (seeking only punishment) from restorative justice (engaging in repair) and notes these correlate with different responses to betrayal—wounded hearts vs. wounded pride. [raw/books/psychology/The State of Affairs - Esther Perel.pdf#p108]
- The chapter presents Camille's story as an example of restorative justice: she used culturally-informed rituals to empower herself rather than simply punish her husband Amadou, ultimately gaining 'trust and confidence in myself.'. [raw/books/psychology/The State of Affairs - Esther Perel.pdf#p110]

## Inferences

- Inference: The chunk suggests that revenge motivated by wounded pride (narcissistic injury) is less likely to lead to reconciliation than revenge motivated by loss of connection (relational injury).
- Inference: Perel's clinical technique of having clients write revenge fantasies in a private journal for limited time periods ('seven minutes a day, max') suggests a controlled outlet for aggressive impulses.
