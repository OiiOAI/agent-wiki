# More of the Same

## Summary

A phenomenon where attempted solutions that apply 'more of the same' approach actually compound or perpetuate the problem. Examples include Prohibition worsening alcoholism, cheerfulness demands deepening depression, and will-power intensifying insomnia. The authors distinguish between situations where applying an opposite can restore equilibrium (simple negative feedback) versus situations where this approach fails and actually worsens the problem. When first-order change is attempted where second-order change is required, the 'solution' becomes the problem.

## Key facts

- More of the same solutions often compound the problem rather than resolve it. [raw/books/strategy/Change _ principles of problem formulation and problem - Paul Watzlawick, John H_ Weakland, Richard Fisch; foreword.epub#L107-L686]
- Prohibition of alcohol worsened alcoholism by creating a clandestine industry and public health problems from poor-quality bootleg products. [raw/books/strategy/Change _ principles of problem formulation and problem - Paul Watzlawick, John H_ Weakland, Richard Fisch; foreword.epub#L107-L686]
- Attempting to cheer up a depressed person typically deepens their depression by creating feelings of failure and ingratitude. [raw/books/strategy/Change _ principles of problem formulation and problem - Paul Watzlawick, John H_ Weakland, Richard Fisch; foreword.epub#L107-L686]
- Will-power attempts to fall asleep typically prevent sleep because sleep is a spontaneous phenomenon that cannot occur when willed. [raw/books/strategy/Change _ principles of problem formulation and problem - Paul Watzlawick, John H_ Weakland, Richard Fisch; foreword.epub#L107-L686]
- In marriage conflicts, each spouse's corrective behavior is seen by the other as the behavior requiring correction, creating escalating cycles of 'more of the same'. ## Inferences

- Inference: The phenomenon operates through circular rather than linear causality, where each action becomes both stimulus and response in an endless loop.
