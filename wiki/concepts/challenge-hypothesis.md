# Challenge Hypothesis

## Summary

The challenge hypothesis, proposed by John Wingfield in 1990, states that rising testosterone levels increase aggression only at the time of a challenge to status, rather than causing aggression outright. Testosterone's effects are contingent and amplifying, exacerbating preexisting tendencies rather than creating new behaviors. When testosterone rises after a challenge, it prompts whatever behaviors are needed to maintain status—and if maintaining status requires prosocial behavior, testosterone will promote that instead.

## Key facts

- Rising testosterone levels increase aggression only during challenges to status, not at baseline. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p108]
- Testosterone does not create new social patterns of aggression; it exaggerates preexisting ones. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p107]
- When testosterone rises after a challenge, it prompts whatever behaviors are needed to maintain status. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p109]
- Testosterone administration to middle-ranking male talapoin monkeys increased aggression toward lower-ranking individuals, not higher-ranking ones. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p107]
- Testosterone made subjects more generous in the Ultimatum Game when fairness was what counted as status. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p109]
- The Challenge Hypothesis was proposed by Wingfield et al. (1990) to explain patterns of testosterone secretion in relation to mating systems and breeding strategies. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p753]
- Subsequent research in wild chimpanzees and humans has tested and generally supported the Challenge Hypothesis framework. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p753]
- Fatherhood decreases testosterone in human males, representing a case where paternal behavior reduces testosterone rather than maintaining it for competition. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p753]

## Inferences

- Inference: The challenge hypothesis implies that social context determines whether testosterone promotes aggression or prosociality, suggesting that testosterone itself is not 'good' or 'bad' but amplifies whatever behavioral tendencies are contextually appropriate
- Inference: The Challenge Hypothesis represents a key theoretical integration in behavioral endocrinology, moving beyond simple correlations between hormones and aggression to consider contextual social factors
- Inference: Studies in chimpanzees (Muller & Wrangham, 2004) and meta-analyses (Archer, 2006) have provided evidence both supporting and complicating the original hypothesis

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Some primate studies show lack of correlation between testosterone and aggression, suggesting the hypothesis may not uniformly apply across species or contexts
