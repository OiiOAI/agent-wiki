# Paternalism

## Summary

Paternalism in Rawls' theory concerns principles that authorize others to act on behalf of individuals when their rational powers are undeveloped or fail, as with children or those seriously injured. The parties in the original position adopt paternalistic principles to protect themselves against weaknesses of reason and will. Paternalistic decisions must be guided by the individual's own settled preferences and interests, or by the theory of primary goods, and must be justified by the evident failure or absence of reason and will.

## Key facts

- In the original position, parties assume they are rational and able to manage their own affairs, so they do not acknowledge duties to self, but once the ideal conception is chosen, they will want to insure themselves against the possibility that their powers are undeveloped. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p229]
- Paternalistic decisions are to be guided by the individual's own settled preferences and interests insofar as they are not irrational, or failing knowledge of these, by the theory of primary goods. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p230]
- Paternalistic intervention must be justified by the evident failure or absence of reason and will and must be guided by principles of justice and what is known about the subject's more permanent aims. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p231]
- Even if two persons would later accept their conversion to each other's beliefs, we are not permitted to impose this treatment on them—their integrity as persons must be respected. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p231]
