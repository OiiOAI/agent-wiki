# Binding Problem

## Summary

The binding problem asks how inputs from different senses, scattered across the neocortex with various distortions, are combined into the singular non-distorted perception we experience. The Thousand Brains Theory solves this by proposing that columns vote to reach consensus rather than converging inputs to a single location.

## Key facts

- The input to the neocortex from the retina is a highly distorted and incomplete quilt of image patches with holes and a blind spot, yet our perception is uniform and complete—this is the binding problem. [raw/books/neuroscience/A Thousand Brains _ A New Theory of Intelligence - Jeff Hawkins; Richard Dawkins; Basic Books.pdf#p95]
- The Thousand Brains Theory solves the binding problem by proposing that thousands of models of every object exist, and columns vote to reach a consensus—varied inputs don't need to be bound or combined into a single model. [raw/books/neuroscience/A Thousand Brains _ A New Theory of Intelligence - Jeff Hawkins; Richard Dawkins; Basic Books.pdf#p108]
- It doesn't matter that columns have different types of inputs or that some represent small parts of the retina and others larger parts—the pattern projected to V1 can be distorted and mixed without causing problems. [raw/books/neuroscience/A Thousand Brains _ A New Theory of Intelligence - Jeff Hawkins; Richard Dawkins; Basic Books.pdf#p108]
- The voting mechanism allows the brain to unite numerous types of sensory input into a single representation of what is being sensed. [raw/books/neuroscience/A Thousand Brains _ A New Theory of Intelligence - Jeff Hawkins; Richard Dawkins; Basic Books.pdf#p101]
- The binding problem addresses how information about motion, depth, color, and form, carried by separate neural pathways, is organized into a cohesive perception related to the unity of conscious experience. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p313]
- Semir Zeki observed that if all visual areas reported to a single master cortical area, 'who or what does that single area report to? Who is looking at the visual image provided by that master area?'. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p314]
- Zeki concluded: 'There is no single cortical area to which all other cortical areas report exclusively, either in the visual or in any other system. In sum, the cortex must be using a different strategy for generating the integrated visual image.'. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p314]
- The unity of consciousness is described as a variant of the binding problem first identified in visual perception. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p391]
- The unity of conscious experience implies that the binding process must somehow connect and integrate all separate brain areas involved in different sensory and emotional processing. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p391]
- Gerald Edelman argued effectively that the neural machinery for the unity of consciousness is likely widely distributed throughout the cortex and thalamus, making it unlikely we will find consciousness through a simple set of neural correlates. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p391]

## Inferences

- Inference: The binding problem doesn't require a central integrator or convergence point—distributed voting provides a more parsimonious explanation consistent with the anatomical connectivity of the neocortex.
- Inference: The binding problem connects directly to the question of where consciousness is localized in the brain—distributed versus concentrated.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The exact neural implementation of cross-modal voting and how it achieves rapid consensus remains to be fully characterized.
