# Econs versus Humans

## Summary

Econs (idealized rational agents) and Humans (actual people) represent a fundamental distinction in behavioral economics. Econs have consistent preferences, process all information, and are immune to framing effects and cognitive biases. Humans rely on System 1 heuristics that are usually adaptive but produce systematic predictable errors, challenging the rational-agent model that underlies much of economics and policy.

## Key facts

- Econs pass the test of rationality: they satisfy the axioms of coherent choice (transitivity, substitution), read the fine print, and are immune to framing effects. Humans regularly violate these properties. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p533]
- The rational-actor model provides intellectual support for libertarian policy: if people are rational, protecting them from their choices is unnecessary and immoral (per Milton Friedman's 'Free to Choose'). [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p534]
- Kahneman and Tversky's research shows that Human deviations from rationality are not random errors but systematic and predictable, making them amenable to institutional design solutions rather than being dismissed as noise. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p540]
- Organizations can compensate for individual cognitive limitations by implementing checklists, reference class forecasting, premortems, and procedures that slow down decision-making and enforce reflection. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p541]

## Inferences

- Inference: The distinction between Econs and Humans is not merely descriptive but has profound normative implications: if humans are not Econs, then libertarian policies based on the assumption of rational actors may harm the very people they claim to protect.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Whether the behavioral economics corrections to rational-agent models can scale to address major societal challenges or remain primarily applicable to marginal policy improvements.
