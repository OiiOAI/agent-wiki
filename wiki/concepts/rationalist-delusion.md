# Rationalist Delusion

## Summary

Haidt's term for the historically long-lived Western delusion that reasoning is humanity's most noble attribute that makes us like gods. The delusion includes claims that (1) reasoning is the royal road to moral truth, (2) people who reason well are more likely to act morally, and (3) the rational caste should have more power. Haidt argues that moral philosophers are no more virtuous than other academics, refuting the claim that expertise in moral reasoning improves moral behavior.

## Key facts

- Webster's Third defines delusion as 'a false conception and persistent belief unconquerable by reason in something that has no existence in fact'. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p105]
- Eric Schwitzgebel found moral philosophers are no better than other philosophers or professors in giving to charity, voting, calling mothers, donating blood, or cleaning up after themselves. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p105]
- Academic books on ethics are more likely to be stolen or never returned from libraries than books in other areas of philosophy. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p106]
- Bentham and Kant—the founders of utilitarianism and deontology—may have had Asperger's syndrome, being high in systemizing and low in empathizing. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p133-137]
- Mill wrote that Bentham's 'incompleteness' of mind included 'no sympathy' for many natural and strongest feelings of human nature and 'deficiency of Imagination' for understanding minds different from his own. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p135]

## Inferences

- Inference: The rationalist delusion may explain why philosophical and scientific circles sometimes overvalue individual reasoning capacity and undervalue intuition, emotion, and social contexts
