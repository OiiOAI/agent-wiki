# Law of Crime Concentration

## Summary

The Law of Crime Concentration, formulated by criminologist David Weisburd, states that crime is highly concentrated in specific places rather than distributed across neighborhoods or regions. Most streets have little or no crime; a small number of 'hot spots' account for virtually all crime in an area. This challenges intuitive assumptions about crime and place, as blighted or impoverished-looking areas may have mostly fine streets, while seemingly respectable blocks can be violent hot spots.

## Key facts

- David Weisburd found that crime is highly concentrated at specific places, with most streets having no crime while a small number of 'hot spots' account for virtually all crime. [raw/books/psychology/Talking to Strangers - Gladwell, Malcolm.pdf#p224]
- Weisburd's Baltimore study defined a hot spot as any street segment with more than 18 police calls per year, while some streets had over 600 calls for service in one year. [raw/books/psychology/Talking to Strangers - Gladwell, Malcolm.pdf#p224]
- Claire White, Weisburd's colleague, stated that in Baltimore, 'We focus on all the bad people, but in reality there's mostly good people.'. [raw/books/psychology/Talking to Strangers - Gladwell, Malcolm.pdf#p225]
- Weisburd and White documented that a street segment with an exquisite nineteenth-century church and elegant proportions was a violent hot spot, while some bedraggled streets were perfectly fine. [raw/books/psychology/Talking to Strangers - Gladwell, Malcolm.pdf#p225]
- The lesson of Weisburd's research is that 'our ignorance of the unfamiliar is what fuels our fear,' as people tend to write off entire neighborhoods based on visible signs rather than actual crime data. [raw/books/psychology/Talking to Strangers - Gladwell, Malcolm.pdf#p225]

## Inferences

- Inference: Hot spot policing—concentrating resources on specific high-crime locations—may be more effective than traditional neighborhood-based policing.
- Inference: The law challenges the intuition that crime is linked to visible poverty or urban decay, since crime concentrates at specific addresses regardless of neighborhood appearance.
