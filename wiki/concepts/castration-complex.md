# Castration Complex

## Summary

The castration complex is a concept in psychoanalysis referring to the child's fear (particularly in the phallic stage) that their genitals will be removed or damaged. This fear emerges when the child discovers anatomical differences between sexes and responds to the threat or realization that females lack a penis. In Little Hans's case, his mother's earlier threat to have his penis removed returned with deferred effect ('nachträglich') when he learned that women have no penis, leading to phantasies about a plumber exchanging his small penis for a larger one.

## Key facts

- Hans's mother had previously threatened him regarding his penis when he was around three and a half years old, and this threat had a deferred effect during the analysis. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p3226]
- When Hans was told that women have no widdlers, he produced a phantasy that his mother was doing the same thing he had done—a defensive or protective phantasy. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p3226]
- Hans's final phantasy about the plumber giving him a bigger widdler represented overcoming his fear of castration. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p3237]
- Hans's fear of the big bath was connected to his castration complex, as he disliked his 'behind' being too small for the big bath. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p3237]
- The castration complex is intimately connected with the Oedipus complex, as the threat of castration serves as a powerful motive for abandoning the Oedipal wishes. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p3238]
- The patient initially rejected castration and held to his theory of intercourse by the anus, which really involved no judgement upon the question of its existence. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p5334]
- The patient ultimately recognized castration as a fact, but two contrary currents existed side by side—one abominated the idea, while the other was prepared to accept it and console itself with femininity as compensation. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p5335]
- The patient had a hallucination at age five in which he cut off his finger, which was interpreted as identification with women whose clitoris had been 'chopped off', connecting his mother's haemorrhages with the 'wound' of castrated women. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p5340]
- The patient's fear of castration was the motive for keeping the primal scene under repression and for excluding it from later conscious elaboration. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p5334]
- Phylogenetically, it was the father who practised castration as a punishment and later softened it into circumcision. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p5341]
- Fear of damaging or losing one's eyes is 'a terrible one in children' and many adults retain this apprehensiveness, serving as a substitute for the dread of castration. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p5493]
- The self-blinding of Oedipus was 'simply a mitigated form of the punishment of castration—the only punishment that was adequate for him by the lex talionis'. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p5493]
- In 'The Sand-Man,' Coppelius represents the castrating father and the destruction of eyes serves as a castration equivalent, while Olympia (the doll) represents Nathaniel's 'feminine attitude towards his father in his infancy'. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p5494]
- Dismembered limbs and severed hands have an uncanny effect due to their proximity to the castration complex. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p5504]
- The castration threat operates only if the boy has seen female genitals; without this visual confirmation, the threat seems inconceivable to him. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p7346]
- Results of the castration complex are 'multifarious and incalculable': the child's masculinity usually cannot stand up to this first shock; he renounces possession of his mother and develops passive attitudes toward his father. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p7347]
- In females, 'from the very first she envies boys its possession' and her whole development takes place under the colours of this envy; the lack of penis forces her into the Oedipus complex rather than ending it. ## Inferences

- Inference: The Little Hans case demonstrated that the castration complex can be a major factor in childhood neuroses, not just in adult psychoanalytic treatment.
- Inference: The 'plumber' phantasy showed how children work through anxieties symbolically before reaching a resolution.
- Inference: The patient's intense unconscious hostility toward his father (reaching the pitch of a death wish) and sense of guilt derived from his identification of the father with the castrator.
- Inference: The anxiety underlying the patient's phobias (wolf, butterfly) was fundamentally a fear of castration, which was displaced onto animal figures after being connected to the figure who first uttered the threat.
- Inference: The association of the eye with the male genitalia in the Sand-Man story reflects a symbolic substitution where the eye represents the penis, and threats to the eye unconsciously represent castration anxiety.
- Inference: The opposite relationship between castration complex and Oedipus complex in males versus females suggests that anatomical difference produces divergent psychic pathways, with females finding a 'feminine' resolution through their Oedipal attachment while males must renounce theirs.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Freud notes that the impact of the castration threat 'does not always have these dreaded consequences' and depends on quantitative factors.
