# Homeostatic Regulation by Sleep

## Summary

Non-REM sleep serves a homeostatic function, redistributing synaptic weights and narrowing the log-normal firing rate distribution. During sleep transitions from down to up states, high-firing neurons spike before low-firing neurons, strengthening synapses from high to low while weakening the reverse, counteracting changes that occur during asynchronous waking.

## Key facts

- During non-REM sleep transitions from down to up states, neurons fire in sequence with high-firing neurons tending to spike before low-firing neurons. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain from Inside Out - György Buzsáki MD, PhD.pdf#p342]
- This temporal segregation during sleep increases synaptic weights from high-firing onto low-firing neurons while decreasing weights from low-firing onto high-firing neurons, pulling both ends of the log-normal distribution toward the mean. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain from Inside Out - György Buzsáki MD, PhD.pdf#p342]
- During waking, the log-normal distribution widens as many neurons at the left tail become silent while fast-firing neurons increase spiking; during non-REM sleep the opposite occurs. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain from Inside Out - György Buzsáki MD, PhD.pdf#p341]
- The brain wakes up with a much narrower and sharper log-normal firing rate distribution than before sleep, illustrating sleep's homeostatic effect. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain from Inside Out - György Buzsáki MD, PhD.pdf#p341]

## Inferences

- Inference: This normalization mechanism during sleep is an important function of sleep, acting as a homeostatic counter to changes during the asynchronous wake state.
- Inference: Neuronal excitability and network-related neuronal heterogeneity maintain their relationship through sleep-wake cycles.
