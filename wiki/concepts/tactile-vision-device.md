# Tactile Vision Device

## Summary

The tactile vision device was Paul Bach-y-Rita's pioneering 1969 invention that enabled people blind from birth to see through touch. It consisted of a camera whose signals were processed by a computer and delivered to four hundred vibrating stimulators arranged on a metal plate against the user's back. Subjects could read, distinguish faces, perceive depth and perspective, and recognize objects. With practice, they experienced space as three-dimensional even though information entered from a two-dimensional array. This device, published in Nature, was one of the first bold applications of neuroplasticity.

## Key facts

- The 1969 device used a camera whose signals were processed by a computer and delivered to 400 vibrating stimulators on a plate against the user's back. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p21]
- Blind subjects learned to read, make out faces and shadows, distinguish distances, perceive perspective, and recognize partially obscured objects. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p21]
- With practice, subjects perceived space as three-dimensional even though information entered from a two-dimensional array on their backs. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p22]
- The article was reported in The New York Times, Newsweek, and Life, but the device and inventor soon slipped into relative obscurity. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p21]
