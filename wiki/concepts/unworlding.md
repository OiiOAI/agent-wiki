# Unworlding

## Summary

Unworlding, a term borrowed from Heidegger by Louis Sass, describes the loss of the sense of the overarching context that gives coherence to the world, which becomes fragmented and lacking in meaning. The world either loses its substantiality and 'otherness' or is seen as alien, devoid of human resonance or significance. This corresponds to what Sass identifies as the core phenomenological disturbance in both schizophrenia and modernist culture.

## Key facts

- In unworlding, the world is robbed of its substantiality, its 'otherness', its ontological status as an entity having independence from the perceiving subject. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p545]
- Alternatively, the world is seen as alien, devoid of human resonance or significance. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p545]
- In either case the ego is passivised—either as an impotent observer of inner experiences, or transformed into a machine-like entity in a world of static neutral objects. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p545]
- This produces what Sass identifies as an obvious perspectivism, or relativism—an uncertainty and multiplicity of points of view. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p545]

## Inferences

- Inference: Unworlding represents the phenomenological parallel to left-hemisphere dominance, where the world of concrete experience is replaced by abstract representations
