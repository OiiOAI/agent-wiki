# Affective Forecasting

## Summary

Affective forecasting (or affective prediction) is the process of predicting future emotional states. People systematically err in these predictions: they overestimate the impact of both positive and negative events (including major life changes like marriage and disability) because they focus on the event itself while neglecting adaptation and the other aspects of life that continue unchanged.

## Key facts

- Marriage provides a striking example of affective forecasting error: newlyweds report high life satisfaction, but the marriage graph shows a sharp decline immediately after the wedding—because the event is salient in evaluation and later recedes from attention. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p516]
- People who wanted to move to California for better weather show the focus illusion: they will report being happier after the move because the question reminds them of the move, but experienced well-being does not change because climate is not a major determinant of daily emotional experience. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p523]
- Gilbert and Wilson introduced the term 'miswanting' for the poor choices that result from affective forecasting errors, particularly the focus illusion making salient features seem more important than they are. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p526]
- People adapt to most life changes (including paralysis) within about a year, but fail to anticipate this adaptation when predicting their future happiness—leading to both excessive fear of negative events and excessive hope for positive ones. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p524]

## Inferences

- Inference: The combination of focus illusion and miswanting explains why people systematically buy things and make life changes expecting lasting happiness improvements that fail to materialize.
