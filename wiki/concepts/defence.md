# Defence

## Summary

Defence (Abwehr) is the psychical process by which the ego repels incompatible ideas from consciousness. Freud developed the concept that hysterical patients experience a 'not wanting to know'—a resistance that must be overcome in therapy. The ego had originally driven the pathogenic idea out of association, and the same force now opposes its return to memory. Defence succeeds in forcing the idea out of consciousness and out of memory, but the trace persists. This concept became fundamental to Freud's understanding of hysteria as 'defence hysteria' and led directly to the therapeutic task of overcoming this resistance through psychical work.

## Key facts

- Freud concluded that the patient's ego had been approached by an idea which proved to be incompatible and which provoked a repelling force whose purpose was defence against this incompatible idea. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p781]
- The hysterical patient's 'not knowing' is in fact a 'not wanting to know'—a not wanting which may be to a greater or lesser extent conscious. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p782]
- Freud observed that the resistance encountered during therapy is the same force that had shown itself in the form of repulsion when the symptom was generated. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p782]
- Freud distinguished between 'hypnoid hysteria' and 'defence hysteria', with the latter involving the active repulsion of incompatible ideas by the ego. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p796]
- The term 'defence' (Abwehr) first appears in Freud's published work in 'The Neuropsychoses of Defence' (1894). [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p936]
- Freud described three methods of defence: conversion (producing hysteria), transposition of affect (producing obsessions and phobias), and rejection together with affect (producing hallucinatory confusion/psychosis). [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p949]
- In defence hysteria, the splitting of consciousness results from an act of will on the part of the patient, initiated by an effort whose motive can be specified. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p939]
- Freud described 'flight into psychosis' as the most energetic defence, where the ego rejects the incompatible idea together with its affect and behaves as if it had never occurred. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p949]

## Inferences

- Inference: Defence represents the psychological origin of hysterical symptoms, linking the generation of pathology to the same mechanism that must be overcome in treatment.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Freud acknowledged uncertainty about whether hypnoid and defence hysteria have distinct origins or ultimately converge, suspecting that defence may be the primary factor in both.
