# Space-Time Relationship in the Brain

## Summary

The brain does not have dedicated sensors for space or time; instead, it constructs representations of space and time from sensory inputs and self-motion signals. Space and time appear to be represented by similar neuronal mechanisms and may be fundamentally unified in neural computation.

## Key facts

- Space and time may not be inseparable ingredients of neuronal computation, but instead mental constructions represented only by trained brains. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain from Inside Out - György Buzsáki MD, PhD.pdf#p245]
- Distance and duration appear to be represented in the brain by the same structures and likely by the same neurons and mechanisms. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain from Inside Out - György Buzsáki MD, PhD.pdf#p266]
- The brain does not encode any general and uniform space somewhere within its networks; instead, most behaviors require multiple coordinate systems. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain from Inside Out - György Buzsáki MD, PhD.pdf#p255]
- Experiencing time is experiencing space, as demonstrated by experiments showing that temporal and spatial scales are related in subjective experience. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain from Inside Out - György Buzsáki MD, PhD.pdf#p267]
- Hippocampal cells are neither place nor time cells; if the same neurons function as both, downstream reader mechanisms have no way to tell whether spikes represent distance or duration. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain from Inside Out - György Buzsáki MD, PhD.pdf#p269]
- Duration estimation experiments show that subjective time can be warped by many manipulations, demonstrating that duration is relative. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain from Inside Out - György Buzsáki MD, PhD.pdf#p265]

## Inferences

- Inference: The equivalence of space and time representation in the brain suggests that both emerge from a common computational substrate—sequence generation—rather than from dedicated sensors.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Whether dedicated time-computing mechanisms exist in the brain remains unresolved; current evidence suggests that time computation is distributed and content-dependent.
