# Anchoring Effect

## Summary

Anchoring occurs when people consider a particular value—an 'anchor'—when making numerical estimates, even when that anchor is clearly random and uninformative. It is one of the most reliable and robust effects in experimental psychology, with typical anchor indices around 50–55%. Two mechanisms produce anchoring: conscious adjustment from the anchor (System 2), and automatic priming of compatible associations (System 1). Anchoring influences expert judges, real-estate agents, and even German judges setting sentences based on dice rolls.

## Key facts

- A wheel-of-fortune experiment with random anchors (10 vs. 65) produced estimates of African UN nations of 25% and 45% respectively—a 44% anchor index, demonstrating that arbitrary anchors systematically bias estimates. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p157]
- Two mechanisms generate anchoring: adjustment (System 2, conscious, effortful) and priming (System 1, automatic, associative). Amos Tversky favored the adjustment view; Kahneman favored the priming/suggestion view. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p158]
- When a high anchor of $400 was mentioned before asking about environmental donations, average willingness to pay was $143; when the low anchor was $5, it dropped to $20—a 30% anchoring effect. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p165]
- Professional real estate agents showed a 41% anchor index despite claiming they were not influenced by listing prices; students showed 48%. The professionals denied the influence while exhibiting it. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p164]
- German judges with 15 years' experience set sentences averaging 8 months when dice showed 9, versus 5 months when dice showed 3—a 50% anchoring effect on real legal decisions. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p165]
- The last digits of Social Security numbers have been used as random anchors in experiments, producing normal-sized anchoring effects on estimates of quantities unrelated to the numbers. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p165]
- Galinsky and Mussweiler found that thinking about reasons opposite to the anchor reduces or eliminates the anchoring effect, demonstrating deliberate System 2 intervention can counter automatic priming. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p167]

## Inferences

- Inference: The fact that anchors as arbitrary as dice rolls or Social Security number endings produce normal-sized anchoring effects proves that anchoring does not operate through conscious belief that the anchor is informative. Instead, System 1 automatically activates compatible associations, biasing estimates before System 2 can intervene.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The exact boundary between the two anchoring mechanisms (adjustment vs. priming) in specific real-world contexts is not always clear.
