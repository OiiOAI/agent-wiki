# Self-Regulation and Exercise

## Summary

Self-regulation is a resource that can be depleted but also recharged like a muscle. Exercise is by far the best form of self-regulation, strengthening the brain's ability to control impulses, delay gratification, and maintain focus. Australian research showed that a two-month exercise program improved participants' smoking, drinking, caffeine consumption, healthy eating, impulse spending, temper control, procrastination, and appointment-keeping. Exercise helps addicts feel better about themselves and spreads self-control from exercise to other areas of life.

## Key facts

- Self-regulation is characterized as a resource that can be depleted but also recharged like a muscle; the more you use it, the stronger it gets. [raw/books/health/Spark _ The Revolutionary New Science of Exercise and the - John J_ Ratey; Eric Hagerman.pdf#p154]
- Australian researchers found that a two-month exercise program improved participants' smoking, drinking, caffeine and alcohol consumption, junk food intake, impulse spending, temper control, and appointment-keeping. [raw/books/health/Spark _ The Revolutionary New Science of Exercise and the - John J_ Ratey; Eric Hagerman.pdf#p154]
- When an addict engages in exercise involving work and commitment and follows through, that sense of self-control spreads to other areas of life. [raw/books/health/Spark _ The Revolutionary New Science of Exercise and the - John J_ Ratey; Eric Hagerman.pdf#p154]
- Exercise produces dopamine that plugs into receptors and blunts cravings, and over time produces more D2 receptors to restore balance in the reward system. [raw/books/health/Spark _ The Revolutionary New Science of Exercise and the - John J_ Ratey; Eric Hagerman.pdf#p155]
- For someone with negative body image, shifting focus from body to brain can provide powerful new motivation through exercise. [raw/books/health/Spark _ The Revolutionary New Science of Exercise and the - John J_ Ratey; Eric Hagerman.pdf#p155]

## Inferences

- Inference: The concept of self-efficacy (confidence in one's ability to change) is connected to self-regulation; both are strengthened through exercise's effects on the prefrontal cortex and reward system.
