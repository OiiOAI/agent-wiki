# Competitive Plasticity

## Summary

Competitive plasticity is the principle that brain map space is allocated through competition. Maps that receive more input and are used more grow larger, while unused maps shrink or are taken over. This explains why skills we don't practice are lost, why bad habits are hard to break, and why our native language dominates as we age.

## Key facts

- When a monkey's median nerve was cut, the maps for radial and ulnar nerves 'almost doubled in size' and invaded the unused median nerve map space. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p68]
- 'If we stop exercising our mental skills, we do not just forget them: the brain map space for those skills is turned over to the skills we practice instead.'. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p69]
- Competitive plasticity explains why adults have difficulty learning second languages—the native language dominates more map space the more it is used. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p69]
- Bad habits are difficult to break because when learned, they take over a brain map and each repetition claims more control, preventing use of that space for 'good' habits. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p70]
