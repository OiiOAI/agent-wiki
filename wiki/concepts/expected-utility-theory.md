# Expected Utility Theory

## Summary

Expected utility theory, formalized by von Neumann and Morgenstern in 1944, extends revealed preference theory to choices involving uncertainty. The theory posits that rational choosers select options that maximize the expected value of a utility function, where utility of each outcome is weighted by its probability. This framework built the foundations for much of game theory and dominated economic analysis until challenged by paradoxes from Allais and Ellsberg.

## Key facts

- Von Neumann and Morgenstern showed that a natural method of ranking lotteries according to expected value of a reward function rests on the highly intuitive independence axiom. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p23]
- The independence axiom states that if lottery p is preferred to q, then the weighted average of p with r must be preferred to the same weighting of q with r. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p23]
- The Allais paradox (1953) and Ellsberg paradox (1961) demonstrated reliable patterns of revealed preference that violated the independence axiom of expected utility theory. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p3]
- In expected utility theory, the utility function is unique up to affine (linear) transformations, meaning only the relative differences in utility values matter. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p35]
- EU theory requires completeness, transitivity, continuity, and substitution axioms for its mathematical representation. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p140]
- The substitution axiom states that if a person prefers one lottery to another, this preference should not be affected by a mixture of both lotteries with a common third lottery. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p140]
- EU theory does not allow decision makers to exhibit risk aversion without additional assumptions about the shape of the utility function. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p140]

## Inferences

- Inference: The challenges to expected utility theory from behavioral economics led to the development of alternative theories like prospect theory that incorporate psychological principles.
- Inference: Expected utility theory's normative flavor focused on idealized choices and efficient resource allocation rather than necessarily describing how people actually choose.
- Inference: EU theory serves as the foundational framework against which Prospect Theory was developed as a descriptive model.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Whether expected utility theory accurately describes human choice remains debated, with behavioral economists arguing for more psychologically grounded alternatives.
