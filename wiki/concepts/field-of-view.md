# Field of View

## Summary

The field of view, in ecological optics, is the solid angle of ambient light that can be registered by an animal's ocular system. It differs fundamentally from the visual field of introspection theory. The field of view is bounded, unlike the ambient array which is unbounded. Humans have roughly hemispherical fields of view with extensive overlap of the two eyes' fields, while lateral-eyed animals like horses have nearly panoramic fields. The field of view contains the occluding edges of the observer's own body.

## Key facts

- The field of view is the solid angle of ambient light that can be registered by the ocular system, bounded unlike the ambient array, and varies with eye placement—lateral eyes give panoramic vision, frontal eyes give hemispherical vision. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p136]
- When a point of observation is occupied by a human, about half the surrounding world is revealed to the eyes and the remainder is concealed by the head. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p137]
- The eyes are not merely sampling the array; they are turning, converging, accommodating, and making saccades, with each fixation being a momentary sample. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p136]
- The combined field of view of two eyes consists of two samples of the ambient array with overlap determined by interocular distance, resulting in disparity. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p143]

## Inferences

- Inference: The field of view's bounded nature means visual perception is not confined to what is currently visible—the observer can turn the head to reveal previously hidden portions, making vision panoramic over time.
