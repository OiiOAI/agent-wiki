# Genetic Assimilation

## Summary

Genetic assimilation, described by Conrad Waddington in the 1950s-1960s, is the evolutionary process by which natural selection progressively replaces flexible adaptive responses to environmental conditions with genetically determined predispositions. Developmentally anticipating an important response by making its expression obligatory rather than contingent on external signals provides a more efficient and less risky response.

## Key facts

- Waddington argued that after many generations, the production of adaptive responses becomes progressively more canalized, biased by genetic inheritance and less dependent on external signals. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p349]
- Genetic assimilation is not meant to imply one-to-one replacement of learned adaptations with instinctual counterparts, but rather describes an evolutionary trend toward increasing developmental constraint. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p350]
- For genetic assimilation to occur, the adaptive demands imposed by language must remain unchanged over hundreds or thousands of generations, which is problematic given that language change is orders of magnitude faster than genetic change. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p354]
- The deep structures of grammar have variable surface implementations from language to language, making them poorly localizable within the brain and therefore unlikely candidates for genetic assimilation. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p359]

## Inferences

- Inference: Grammatical knowledge cannot be genetically assimilated because universal grammatical features are implemented through highly variable surface structures that map differently to brain regions in different languages.
