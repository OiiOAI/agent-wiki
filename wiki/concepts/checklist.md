# Checklist

## Summary

A checklist is a simple cognitive tool that compensates for human limitations in memory, attention, and thoroughness. Gawande identifies two types: DO-CONFIRM checklists that verify tasks are completed, and READ-DO checklists that guide actions step-by-step. Checklists address both simple problems with forcing functions and complex situations by ensuring communication among team members.

## Key facts

- The modern aviation checklist originated in 1935 after the crash of the Boeing Model 299, which was deemed 'too much airplane for one man to fly.'. [raw/books/strategy/The checklist manifesto _ how to get things right - Atul Gawande.pdf#p32]
- With the checklist in hand, pilots flew the Model 299 a total of 1.8 million miles without one accident, leading to the B-17 bomber program. [raw/books/strategy/The checklist manifesto _ how to get things right - Atul Gawande.pdf#p33]
- Peter Pronovost created a central line infection checklist that reduced the ten-day line-infection rate from 11 percent to zero in Johns Hopkins ICU. [raw/books/strategy/The checklist manifesto _ how to get things right - Atul Gawande.pdf#p35]
- In complex environments, experts face two main difficulties: fallibility of human memory and attention, and the tendency to skip steps even when remembering them. [raw/books/strategy/The checklist manifesto _ how to get things right - Atul Gawande.pdf#p34]
- Checklists provide protection by reminding of minimum necessary steps, offering verification, and instilling discipline of higher performance. [raw/books/strategy/The checklist manifesto _ how to get things right - Atul Gawande.pdf#p34]

## Inferences

- Inference: Checklists serve dual purposes: ensuring simple steps aren't missed (construction schedules) and forcing communication among experts on complex problems (submittal schedules).
- Inference: The success of checklists depends not just on the document itself but on culture change that empowers all team members to enforce compliance.
