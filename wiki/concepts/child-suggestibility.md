# Child Suggestibility

## Summary

Child suggestibility refers to children's heightened vulnerability to adopting false information during memory retrieval. Research shows that preschoolers are especially susceptible to the corrupting influence of suggestion, particularly when interviewers use specific questions, repeated questioning, social incentives, and forced responding. Studies by Maggie Bruck and Stephen Ceci demonstrated that 58% of preschoolers reported detailed recollections of events they initially said never occurred when encouraged to imagine them. This research was critical in re-examining daycare abuse cases of the 1980s.

## Key facts

- Bruck and the psychologist Stephen Ceci found that when children were repeatedly asked about events their parents indicated had never occurred and encouraged to think about and imagine them, 58% reported detailed recollections of at least one such event. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p176]
- When children are interviewed twice and produce details in a second interview not mentioned in the first, the new details are highly likely to be inaccurate. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p176]
- Young children have special difficulties remembering source information—exactly when and where a particular incident occurred—which can lead them to mix together bits of different past episodes. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p177]
- In the McMartin-style interview study, five- and six-year-olds said 'yes' to just over half of misleading questions in the social incentive condition versus fewer than 10% in the control condition. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p178]
- Three-year-olds said 'yes' to 81% of misleading questions with social incentive techniques compared to 31% without. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p178]
- Forcing elementary school children to answer suggestive questions about a video created a serious source memory problem: children confused their own answers with what had happened in the video. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p179]
