# Planning Fallacy

## Summary

The planning fallacy describes the tendency to underestimate the time needed to complete tasks, even when aware of the phenomenon. Studies show students' realistic estimates are often no different from their optimistic estimates, and most fail to finish papers within their worst-case time predictions.

## Key facts

- Only 45% of students managed to finish papers within the time they said they were 99% confident they would finish in. [raw/books/productivity/How to Take Smart Notes_ One Simple Technique to Boost - Sönke Ahrens.pdf#p130]
- Experience doesn't seem to teach students to estimate time more accurately. [raw/books/productivity/How to Take Smart Notes_ One Simple Technique to Boost - Sönke Ahrens.pdf#p130]
- Parkinson's Law states that work expands to fill the time available for its completion. [raw/books/productivity/How to Take Smart Notes_ One Simple Technique to Boost - Sönke Ahrens.pdf#p131]
- Only 30% of students made their "realistic" deadline for thesis submission; on average they needed 50% more time than planned. [raw/books/strategy/The Art of Thinking Clearly _ Better thinking, Better - Rolf Dobelli; Nicky Griffin.pdf#p199]
- The Sydney Opera House was planned in 1957 for completion in 1963 at $7 million; it opened in 1973 after $102 million—14 times the estimate. [raw/books/strategy/The Art of Thinking Clearly _ Better thinking, Better - Rolf Dobelli; Nicky Griffin.pdf#p199]
- Two causes of the planning fallacy: wishful thinking and excessive focus on the project while overlooking outside influences. [raw/books/strategy/The Art of Thinking Clearly _ Better thinking, Better - Rolf Dobelli; Nicky Griffin.pdf#p199]
- Gary Klein's "premortem" session asks teams to imagine the project failed and write about how it happened. [raw/books/strategy/The Art of Thinking Clearly _ Better thinking, Better - Rolf Dobelli; Nicky Griffin.pdf#p200]
