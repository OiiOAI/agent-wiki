# Autonomy Support

## Summary

Autonomy support is the interpersonal style opposite to control. It involves taking the other person's perspective and working from there, actively encouraging self-initiation, experimentation, and responsibility. An autonomy-supportive approach may require setting limits but functions through encouragement rather than pressure. Research by Ryan and Koestner showed that limits set in an autonomy-supportive way (acknowledging the conflict, minimizing pressure) had a liberating effect on children, while controlling limit-setting had a debilitating effect. Autonomy support enhances intrinsic motivation, engagement, and well-being, and is contrasted with controlling approaches that undermine these outcomes.

## Key facts

- Autonomy support means being able to take the other person's perspective and work from there, actively encouraging self-initiation, experimentation, and responsibility. [raw/books/psychology/Why we do what we do _ understanding self - motivation - Edward L_ Deci, Richard Flaste.pdf#p42]
- Ryan and Koestner's study found that autonomy-supportive limit-setting had a liberating effect on children, while controlling limit-setting had a debilitating effect on their intrinsic motivation. [raw/books/psychology/Why we do what we do _ understanding self - motivation - Edward L_ Deci, Richard Flaste.pdf#p43]
- In the autonomy-supportive limit-setting condition, researchers acknowledged the conflict: 'I know that sometimes it's really fun to just slop the paint around, but here the materials and room need to be kept nice for the other children.'. [raw/books/psychology/Why we do what we do _ understanding self - motivation - Edward L_ Deci, Richard Flaste.pdf#p53]
- By setting limits in an autonomy-supportive way—aligning with the person being limited, recognizing them as a proactive subject rather than an object to be manipulated—it is possible to encourage responsibility without undermining authenticity. [raw/books/psychology/Why we do what we do _ understanding self - motivation - Edward L_ Deci, Richard Flaste.pdf#p53]
- Being autonomy supportive requires more effort and skill than being coercive. [raw/books/psychology/Why we do what we do _ understanding self - motivation - Edward L_ Deci, Richard Flaste.pdf#p42]
- Autonomy support means relating to others as human beings who are active agents worthy of support, rather than as objects to be manipulated for one's own gratification. [raw/books/psychology/Why we do what we do _ understanding self - motivation - Edward L_ Deci, Richard Flaste.pdf#p110]
- Autonomy support is not the same as permissiveness; it requires being clear, consistent, and setting limits in an understanding, empathic way. [raw/books/psychology/Why we do what we do _ understanding self - motivation - Edward L_ Deci, Richard Flaste.pdf#p114]
- Three key behaviors for promoting integration in experiments were: providing a rationale, acknowledging feelings, and minimizing pressure through language emphasizing choice rather than control. [raw/books/psychology/Why we do what we do _ understanding self - motivation - Edward L_ Deci, Richard Flaste.pdf#p111]
- Permissiveness is easy, but autonomy support is hard work that requires being clear, consistent, and setting limits while taking the other's perspective. [raw/books/psychology/Why we do what we do _ understanding self - motivation - Edward L_ Deci, Richard Flaste.pdf#p114]
- The more teachers or managers feel pressured to get results, the more controlling they become, which paradoxically undermines the outcomes they seek. [raw/books/psychology/Why we do what we do _ understanding self - motivation - Edward L_ Deci, Richard Flaste.pdf#p168]
