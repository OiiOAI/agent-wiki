# Aristotelian Principle

## Summary

The Aristotelian Principle states that human beings enjoy the exercise of their realized capacities, and this enjoyment increases the more the capacity is realized or the greater its complexity. It explains why people prefer more complex activities to simpler ones and is a basic psychological principle governing changes in the pattern of desires.

## Key facts

- The Aristotelian Principle: other things equal, human beings enjoy the exercise of their realized capacities, and this enjoyment increases the more the capacity is realized, or the greater its complexity. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p379]
- The principle implies that as a person's capacities increase through training, he will come to prefer more complex activities that call upon his newly realized abilities. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p380]
- The Aristotelian Principle is a principle of motivation that accounts for many major desires and explains preferences for more complex activities. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p380]
- The principle contains a variant of the principle of inclusiveness: the clearest cases of greater complexity are activities that include all skills of another plus additional ones. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p379]
- The Aristotelian Principle ties in with the primary good of self-respect and has a central position in the moral psychology underlying justice as fairness. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p385]

## Inferences

- Inference: Complex activities are more enjoyable because they satisfy desires for variety, allow for feats of ingenuity, permit personal expression, and have fascinating overall form.
