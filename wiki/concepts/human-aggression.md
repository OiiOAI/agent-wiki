# Human Aggression

## Summary

Human aggression stems from underlying insecurity and feelings of helplessness. Greene argues that unlike other animals, humans are aware of mortality, uncertainty, and dependence on unpredictable others. This awareness creates chronic feelings of vulnerability that drive the desire for power and control. Aggression exists on a spectrum, with most people occasionally crossing social 'guardrails' but returning to acceptable behavior. Chronic aggressors, however, cannot tolerate helplessness and repeatedly violate these limits. The chapter discusses genetic components (per Melanie Klein's observations of anxious infants), early family influences (per Erich Fromm), and the phenomenon of aggression becoming addictive.

## Key facts

- Humans are aware of mortality, the insecurity of their position in life, the unpredictability of others, and their dependence on people who often fail them. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p601]
- The desire for power is a natural response to awareness of essential weakness and vulnerability, not inherently evil or antisocial. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p601]
- Social codes and laws act as 'guardrails' that most people stay within to gain power while remaining liked and respected. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p602]
- Chronic aggressors feel chronically insecure and fragile and cover this with inordinate power and control; their need for power is too immediate and strong for them to accept limits. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p603]
- Melanie Klein observed that some infants were born with greater anxiety and greed, suckling as if attacking the breast and needing more coddling—this may be a genetic component of chronic aggression. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p603]
- According to Erich Fromm, domineering parents who repress children's need for power often create adults who like to dominate and tyrannize others. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p604]
- Aggression can become addictive, providing jolts of adrenaline that chronic aggressors come to crave, making socially acceptable stimulation seem tepid. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p605]
- The aggressor's trap: the more power they gain, the more vulnerabilities they create, sparking greater need for aggression—a dynamic Greene illustrates with Rockefeller. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p605]
- Aggressors see people around them as objects to use, degrading any empathy they may have had in their pursuit of control. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p606]
- Chronic aggression is more common among men, who generally find it harder to manage feelings of dependency and helplessness. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p604]

## Inferences

- Inference: The chapter suggests that human aggression is universal and persistent, not merely a product of modern civilization. Greene references anthropological evidence that warfare existed tens of thousands of years before civilization, contradicting both the 'noble savage' myth and the 'progress' myth that humans are evolving beyond violence.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The genetic component of chronic aggression is presented as speculative ('It is possible that there is a genetic component'), based on Klein's clinical observations rather than definitive research.
