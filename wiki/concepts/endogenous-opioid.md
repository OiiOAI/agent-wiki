# Endogenous Opioid

## Summary

Endogenous opioids are neuropeptides produced naturally in the body that have analgesic properties similar to morphine. Four major families exist: endorphins (including beta-endorphin and endomorphins), enkephalins (met-enkephalin and leu-enkephalin), dynorphins (dynorphin A and B), and orphanin FQ/nociceptin. These peptides act on opioid receptors (mu, delta, kappa, and ORL1), which are G protein-coupled receptors concentrated in pain-modulatory regions including the periaqueductal gray, nucleus raphe magnus, and superficial dorsal horn.

## Key facts

- Three major classes of opioid peptides are endorphins, enkephalins, and dynorphins. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p601]
- Enkephalins are active at both mu and delta receptors. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p601]
- Dynorphin is a relatively selective agonist of the kappa receptor. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p601]
- The mu receptors are highly concentrated in the superficial dorsal horn of the spinal cord, the ventral medulla, and the periaqueductal gray matter. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p601]
- The observation that the opiate antagonist naloxone blocks stimulation-produced analgesia provided the first clue that the brain contains endogenous opioids. - Microinjection of low doses of morphine into the periaqueductal gray region produces powerful analgesia. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p602]

## Inferences

- Inference: The strategic distribution of opioid receptors in pain-modulatory regions and the presence of endogenous opioid peptides at synaptic junctions in the dorsal horn suggest that opioid peptides normally regulate nociceptive transmission.
