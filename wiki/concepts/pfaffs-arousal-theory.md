# Pfaff's Arousal Theory

## Summary

Donald Pfaff's theory of arousal, presented in his 2005 book, operationalises 'generalised arousal' as higher in an animal who is (S) more alert to sensory stimuli, (M) more motorically active, and (E) more emotionally reactive. Pfaff connects arousal with information theory, arguing that CNS arousal responds to environmental situations with high entropy, uncertainty and information content.

## Key facts

- Pfaff proposes generalised arousal is higher in an animal who is (S) more alert to sensory stimuli, (M) more motorically active, and (E) more emotionally reactive. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p277]
- Pfaff's principal component analyses suggest generalised arousal accounts for 30-45% of behaviour across wide ranges of data. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p277]
- Pfaff argues CNS arousal systems battle the Second Law of Thermodynamics by responding selectively to environmental situations with high entropy, high uncertainty and high information content. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p279]
- Pfaff connects Shannon's information theory with arousal, noting that perfectly regular events have zero information content while uncertain events have high information content. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p278]
- Pfaff concludes that arousal theory and information theory were 'made for each other'. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p280]
- Pfaff argues that because CNS arousal depends on surprise and unpredictability, its quantification depends on the mathematics of information. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p278]

## Inferences

- Inference: Pfaff's work provides empirical support for connecting information-theoretic concepts to biological arousal, which Solms uses to bridge Friston's Free Energy Principle with neurobiology.
