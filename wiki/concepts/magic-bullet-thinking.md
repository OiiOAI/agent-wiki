# Magic Bullet Thinking

## Summary

Magic bullet thinking refers to the tendency in modern medicine and nutritional science to seek single-ingredient solutions to complex problems, exemplified by the assumption that one specific nutrient can treat or prevent a specific mental disorder. The authors argue this paradigm has profoundly hindered progress in nutritional mental health research. Hundreds of studies from 1920 to 2000 examined one nutrient at a time for mental health, producing inconsistent results that failed to change clinical practice. In contrast, the authors advocate for broad-spectrum multinutrient approaches because brain metabolic pathways require many micronutrients working synergistically as cofactors. The same critique applies to the search for single genes responsible for mental illness, which has also largely failed.

## Key facts

- Bonnie's review of treatment studies for mental disorders from 1920 to 2000 found hundreds of publications studying one nutrient at a time, with modest improvements insufficient to change clinical practice. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Better Brain - Bonnie J. Kaplan.pdf#p38]
- The search for single candidate genes for mental disorders has been described as a 'waste of 1000 research papers' (Ed Yong, The Atlantic, May 2019), with decades and hundreds of millions of dollars invested in a largely unsuccessful approach. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Better Brain - Bonnie J. Kaplan.pdf#p58]
- The authors state: 'No single nutrient can successfully treat the complexity of brain and mental disorders' and 'There is no single magic nutrient to improve brain function.'. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Better Brain - Bonnie J. Kaplan.pdf#p38]
- The success of single-nutrient treatments for single-nutrient deficiency diseases (scurvy and vitamin C; pellagra and niacin) created a paradigm that researchers later incorrectly applied to complex mental disorders. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Better Brain - Bonnie J. Kaplan.pdf#p38]
