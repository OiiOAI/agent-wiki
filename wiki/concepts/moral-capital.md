# Moral Capital

## Summary

Moral capital refers to the resources that sustain a moral community. Haidt defines it as the degree to which a community possesses interlocking sets of values, virtues, norms, practices, identities, institutions, and technologies that mesh well with evolved psychological mechanisms and thereby enable the community to suppress or regulate selfishness and make cooperation possible. It is distinct from social capital in that it encompasses the complete environmental context within which relationships are embedded, making people more virtuous.

## Key facts

- Moral capital is defined as the degree to which a community possesses interlocking sets of values, virtues, norms, practices, identities, institutions, and technologies that mesh well with evolved psychological mechanisms and thereby enable the community to suppress or regulate selfishness and make cooperation possible. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p315]
- Moral capital is not always an unalloyed good—it leads to suppression of free riders but does not automatically lead to other forms of fairness such as equality of opportunity. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p316]
- High moral capital can be obtained within a cult or fascist nation, as long as most people truly accept the prevailing moral matrix. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p316]
- Haidt suggests that liberalism tends to overreach, change too many things too quickly, and reduce the stock of moral capital inadvertently. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p317]
- Haidt defines moral capital as a property of a community or social system, not an individual, distinguishing it from prior uses of the term referring to individual integrity. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p419]
- Religious participation correlates with higher moral capital: Putnam and Campbell found that religious Americans are more likely to give to charity, volunteer, and help strangers than secular Americans. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p413]
- Increasing income inequality erodes moral capital by reducing social trust, according to Wilkinson and Pickett's research cited by Haidt. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p419]
- Religious communities generate moral capital partly through costly signaling: rituals and prohibitions that would be irrational for individuals but benefit groups by screening for committed members. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p411]

## Inferences

- Inference: Moral capital is distinguished from social capital by its focus on the complete environmental context that supports moral behavior, not just the social relationships themselves.
- Inference: Moral capital helps explain why religious and tight-knit communities often outperform secular ones in sustaining cooperation, even when members could benefit from free-riding.
