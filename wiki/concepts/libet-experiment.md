# Libet Experiment

## Summary

The Libet experiment, conducted in the 1980s by neuroscientist Benjamin Libet at UCSF, demonstrated that a 'readiness potential'—a brain signal indicating impending movement—appears approximately half a second before the reported time of conscious intent to move. This finding raised profound questions about free will, suggesting the brain 'decides' before conscious awareness of the decision.

## Key facts

- In the Libet experiment, subjects hooked up to EEG were instructed to flick their wrist whenever they felt like it and note the time of their conscious decision to do so; readiness potentials appeared about half a second before the reported time of conscious intent to move. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p610]
- Libet himself offered a counterinterpretation: while the brain may prepare to initiate a behavior before conscious awareness, the lag time provides the potential to consciously choose to veto that action—what V.S. Ramachandran termed 'free won't.'. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p610]
- Critics argued that the supposed half-second lag is an artifact of experimental design—the lag between the instant of decision and when attention is focused on the clock—rather than a real neural delay. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p610]
