# Maneuver Warfare

## Summary

Maneuver warfare is a military doctrine emphasizing speed, deception, fluidity of action, and attacking the enemy's cohesion and will rather than seeking attrition through concentrated firepower. John Boyd synthesized insights from Sun Tzu, German Blitzkrieg tactics, and historical campaigns to articulate a theory of conflict centered on operating at a quicker tempo than the adversary to cause confusion, disorder, and collapse before physical destruction occurs.

## Key facts

- Boyd identified the essence of maneuver warfare in Sun Tzu's concepts of cheng and ch'i (orthodox and unorthodox approaches), where the best commander wins by unraveling the enemy before battle. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p325]
- Boyd contrasted Sun Tzu's approach with von Clausewitz's, saying 'Sun Tzu tried to drive his adversary bananas while Clausewitz tried to keep himself from being driven bananas.'. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p327]
- Boyd showed that the Blitzkrieg was the 'perfect tactical application of the OODA Loop,' using high operational tempo and rapid exploitation of opportunity through 'mission orders' rather than micromanagement. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p330]
- The German infiltration tactics of 1918, later refined into Blitzkrieg, used small groups that bypassed strong points, 'like water going downhill, bypassing obstacles, always moving, probing.'. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p327]
- Boyd taught that maneuver warfare uses 'moral conflict'—actions that increase menace, uncertainty, and mistrust in the enemy while increasing initiative and harmony within friendly forces. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p331]
- Mike Wyly observed in Vietnam that young Marines had learned guerrilla tactics to survive—how to disperse and fight like the enemy—which aligned with maneuver warfare principles. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p369]
- Wyly taught his students at the Amphibious Warfare School to bypass resistance, ignore their flanks, and let the enemy worry about his flanks. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p375]
- Boyd said concepts must be 'implicit'—if they are explicit, you cannot do them fast enough on the battlefield. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p376]
- The 'Young Turks' at Camp Lejeune, including Bill Woods and G.I. Wilson, were officers studying maneuver warfare under the protection of General Al Gray. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p377]
- General Al Gray ordered the first free-play exercises at Fort Pickett in 1981, where there was no predetermined scenario and outcomes were uncertain, testing combat leaders. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p378]
- Careerists hated free-play exercises because losing could end chances for promotion, while true combat leaders loved them. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p378]
- In Grenada in 1983, Lieutenant Colonel Ray Smith's Marines bypassed enemy strongholds, created confusion, and moved like 'water flowing downhill,' with hundreds of enemies surrendering because 'the Marines are everywhere.'. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p381]
- FMFM-1 Warfighting was the Marine Corps' new tactics manual incorporating maneuver warfare, signed by Commandant Al Gray as official doctrine. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p385]
- The Gulf War demonstrated maneuver warfare when coalition forces used multiple thrusts and deception, causing fifteen Iraqi divisions to surrender to two Marine divisions. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p419]
- Brigadier General Richard Neal explained coalition success by saying 'We kind of got inside his decision cycle,' directly echoing Boyd's terminology. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p419]

## Inferences

- Inference: Boyd's critique of von Clausewitz centered on two flaws: wanting decisive battles (causing bloodbaths) and minimizing only one's own friction rather than maximizing the enemy's friction.
- Inference: The Marine Corps' adoption of maneuver warfare represented a fundamental shift from the attrition-based doctrine that had failed in Vietnam.
- Inference: The Army's failure in the Gulf War—stopping for three nights due to fear of exposed flanks—demonstrated the difference between nominal adoption and true implementation of maneuver warfare.
