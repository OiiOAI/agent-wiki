# Ultimatum Game

## Summary

The Ultimatum Game is a two-player bargaining game introduced by Guth et al. (1982) that examines strategic thinking and fairness considerations. A proposer suggests how to divide an endowment; the responder can accept or reject. If rejected, both receive nothing.

## Key facts

- The key result of ultimatum experiments is that most proposers offer between 40% and 50% of the endowed amount, and that this split is almost always accepted by responders. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p54]
- When the proposal falls to 20% of the endowment it is rejected about half of the time, and rejection rates increase as the proposal falls to 10% and lower. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p54]
- Neuroimaging studies show that unfair offers from humans activate bilateral anterior insula, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC), and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC). [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p73]
- Higher anterior insula response to unfair offers correlates with higher rejection rates. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p74]
- In the ultimatum game, proposers offer significantly more in the ultimatum game than in the dictator game, because responders can reject unfair offers. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p252]
- fMRI studies of the ultimatum game report activation of bilateral DLPFC, bilateral anterior insula, and ACC in the contrast between unfair and fair offers. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p212]
- Right VLPFC activation when unfair offers are accepted may indicate that this region down-regulates resentment associated with unfair offers. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p212]

## Inferences

- Inference: The rejection of low offers suggests that people value fairness or experience negative emotions when treated unfairly, beyond pure monetary self-interest.
- Inference: The willingness to reject unfair offers at personal cost suggests that fairness norms are valued beyond mere monetary considerations, consistent with social preference theories
