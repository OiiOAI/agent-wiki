# Schema Theory and Memory

## Summary

Schema theory, introduced by Sir Frederic Bartlett in 1932, proposes that memory is not a passive re-excitation of fixed traces but an active process of rebuilding organized mental representations. H.M.'s ability to learn new information through crossword puzzles, but only when it could be linked to preexisting preoperative schemas, provided evidence that existing knowledge structures facilitate the acquisition and consolidation of new semantic information.

## Key facts

- Sir Frederic Bartlett introduced the concept of schemas in 1932, viewing remembering as the capacity to creatively rebuild inner representations rather than re-exciting fixed traces. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p261]
- Henry could solve 'pre-post' crossword puzzles (post-1953 clues linked to pre-1953 answers) and showed improvement across five days of testing by linking new information to preoperative schemas. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p261]
- He successfully learned six pre-post associations: polio, Hiss, Gone with the Wind, Ike, St. Louis, and Warsaw. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p261]
- Rat experiments at the University of Edinburgh (2007) showed that learning an associative schema allowed rapid one-trial learning of new flavor-place associations that were retained even after hippocampal lesions. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p262]
- Henry's preoperative schemas included knowledge about politicians, movie stars, and technology acquired during his first 27 years of life. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p263]

## Inferences

- Inference: Schema theory explains why H.M. could occasionally consolidate new facts—he relied on enduring structured representations of old knowledge to understand, store, and recall new information.
- Inference: The Edinburgh rat studies suggest that once new associations are integrated into an existing schema, they can be consolidated outside the hippocampus in cortical networks.
