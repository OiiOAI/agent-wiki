# Errant Mental Models

## Summary

Errant mental models occur when an incomplete or wrong mental model is used to interpret information, leading to poor comprehension and projection (Levels 2 and 3 SA). Mode errors—where people believe the system is in one mode when it is really in another—are a special case. Studies found that even very blatant cues that a wrong mental model had been activated were not properly detected 66% of the time. People tend to explain away conflicting cues to fit their selected mental model.

## Key facts

- Errant mental models occur when an incomplete or wrong mental model is used to interpret information, leading to poor comprehension and projection. [raw/books/strategy/Designing for Situation Awareness _ An Approach to - Jones, Debra G_; Endsley, Mica R.pdf#p59]
- Mode errors occur when people misunderstand information because they believe the system is in one mode when it is really in another. [raw/books/strategy/Designing for Situation Awareness _ An Approach to - Jones, Debra G_; Endsley, Mica R.pdf#p59]
- A study found 66% of the time air traffic controllers did not properly detect cues that a wrong mental model had been activated. [raw/books/strategy/Designing for Situation Awareness _ An Approach to - Jones, Debra G_; Endsley, Mica R.pdf#p60]
- People tend to explain away conflicting cues to fit the mental model they have selected, even when such explanations are far-fetched. [raw/books/strategy/Designing for Situation Awareness _ An Approach to - Jones, Debra G_; Endsley, Mica R.pdf#p60]
- Standardization and limited use of automation modes help minimize errant mental model errors. [raw/books/strategy/Designing for Situation Awareness _ An Approach to - Jones, Debra G_; Endsley, Mica R.pdf#p60]
- Errant mental models are inaccurate mental representations of how a system functions that can be devastating for situation awareness. [raw/books/strategy/Designing for Situation Awareness _ An Approach to - Jones, Debra G_; Endsley, Mica R.pdf#p314]
- Without an accurate mental model, correctly assimilating and processing information to form accurate Levels 2 and 3 SA is difficult or impossible. [raw/books/strategy/Designing for Situation Awareness _ An Approach to - Jones, Debra G_; Endsley, Mica R.pdf#p314]
- Design strategies for minimizing errant mental models include mapping system functions to user goals and established mental models (Principle 19). [raw/books/strategy/Designing for Situation Awareness _ An Approach to - Jones, Debra G_; Endsley, Mica R.pdf#p314]
- Providing consistency and standardization across controls on different displays and systems (Principle 23) helps minimize errant mental models. [raw/books/strategy/Designing for Situation Awareness _ An Approach to - Jones, Debra G_; Endsley, Mica R.pdf#p314]
- Enforcing automation consistency so operators can expect automation to function similarly in different modes (Principle 40) reduces errant mental model hazards. [raw/books/strategy/Designing for Situation Awareness _ An Approach to - Jones, Debra G_; Endsley, Mica R.pdf#p314]
- Providing system and automation transparency and observability (Principles 20 and 44) promotes selection of an accurate mental model. [raw/books/strategy/Designing for Situation Awareness _ An Approach to - Jones, Debra G_; Endsley, Mica R.pdf#p314]
- Making modes and system states salient (Principle 39) helps operators select the appropriate mental model. [raw/books/strategy/Designing for Situation Awareness _ An Approach to - Jones, Debra G_; Endsley, Mica R.pdf#p314]
