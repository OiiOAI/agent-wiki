# Average Utility Principle

## Summary

The Average Utility Principle directs society to maximize not total utility but average utility (per capita). Rawls examines this principle as an alternative to the classical principle of utility and argues that parties in the original position would prefer it over the classical principle, though still reject both in favor of the two principles of justice. The principle relies on probabilistic reasoning and the principle of insufficient reason.

## Key facts

- The Average Principle directs society to maximize the percentage weighted sum of expectations, multiplying expectations by the fraction of society at corresponding positions. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p156]
- The Average Principle would be recognized from a situation where a single rational individual chooses which of several societies to join, computing expected well-being by assuming equal chance of being any individual. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p157]
- Rawls argues the parties discount likelihoods arrived at solely on the basis of the principle of insufficient reason due to the fundamental importance of the original agreement and the desire for responsible decisions affecting descendants. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p162]
- A fundamental difficulty with the Average Principle is that its expectation lacks unity, being founded not on one system of aims but on the preferences of different persons. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p166]

## Inferences

- Inference: The Average Principle is the ethic of a single rational individual with no aversion to risk who tries to maximize prospects from the standpoint of the initial situation.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Rawls notes but does not fully resolve deep problems about whether the constructed expectation is meaningful when utilities are based on different persons' preferences rather than a single system of preferences.
