# Visual Cliff

## Summary

The visual cliff is an experimental apparatus (developed by E.J. Gibson and R.D. Walk, 1960) consisting of a sheet of glass that can be made either visible (textured paper beneath) or invisible (paper far below) while still providing mechanical support. It tests whether animals and human infants can perceive the affordance of a 'falling-off edge' versus a safe 'stepping-down edge' in the surface of support.

## Key facts

- The visual cliff apparatus uses glass that affords support mechanically but provides optical information for support only when textured paper is visible beneath it. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p181]
- Human infants at crawling stage would pat the glass but would not venture onto the invisible (deep) side, suggesting they misperceived the affordance of transparent glass for support. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p165]
- Nearly all terrestrial animals tested chose the shallow edge instead of the deep one, suggesting innate capacity to perceive falling-off places rather than innate ideas of geometry. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p182]
- The results are more intelligible in terms of perceiving the affordance of a cliff edge (a 'falling-off place') than in terms of traditional depth perception cues. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p181]

## Inferences

- Inference: The perception of edges in the surface of support (falling-off vs. stepping-down) is fundamental to terrestrial animals and is more basic than abstract depth or distance perception.
