# Place-Learning

## Summary

Place-learning refers to the ability to learn the affordances of places and distinguish among them, culminating in the state of being oriented to the whole habitat. Gibson argues that places are distinct from objects—they cannot be displaced but merge into adjacent places, and each vista serves as its own landmark. Finding hidden places involves seeing which occluding edge hides the goal.

## Key facts

- A place is one of many adjacent places that make up the habitat, persisting in location relative to other places—it cannot be displaced like an object. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p261]
- Vistas are serially connected—at the end of an alley the next alley opens up; at the edge of a doorway the next room opens up. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p220]
- To find the way to a hidden place, one needs to see which vista has to be opened up next, or which occluding edge hides the goal. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p220]
- When vistas have been put in order by exploratory locomotion, the invariant structure of the house, town, or whole habitat will be apprehended—the hidden and unhidden become one environment. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p221]
- The adjacent order of places cannot be permuted or shuffled—hiding places, falling-off places, and other places are immobile. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p261]

## Inferences

- Inference: Place-learning is fundamentally different from object learning because places have invariant spatial relationships that cannot change, whereas objects can be moved and their positions permuted.
