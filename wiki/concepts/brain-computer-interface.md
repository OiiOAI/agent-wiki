# Brain-Computer Interface

## Summary

Brain-computer interfaces connect neurons in the brain to silicon chips in computers, potentially allowing direct access to computational resources through thought. While useful for medical applications like controlling prosthetic limbs, Hawkins questions the motivation for healthy people to deeply merge with computers and notes that neither brain uploading nor merging addresses the fundamental existential risks facing humanity.

## Key facts

- Spinal-cord injuries can be addressed by implanting electrodes that allow people to control robot arms or computer mice by thinking. [raw/books/neuroscience/A Thousand Brains _ A New Theory of Intelligence - Jeff Hawkins; Richard Dawkins; Basic Books.pdf#p193]
- Only a few hundred or even a few dozen electrodes from the brain to a computer can be sufficient to control basic movements of a limb. [raw/books/neuroscience/A Thousand Brains _ A New Theory of Intelligence - Jeff Hawkins; Richard Dawkins; Basic Books.pdf#p193]
- Technical challenges include implanting millions of electrodes, avoiding tissue rejection, and reliably targeting millions of individual neurons. [raw/books/neuroscience/A Thousand Brains _ A New Theory of Intelligence - Jeff Hawkins; Richard Dawkins; Basic Books.pdf#p193]
- Hawkins considers the 'merge with computers' proposal achievable for limited purposes but not to the point of fully uniting brain and machine. [raw/books/neuroscience/A Thousand Brains _ A New Theory of Intelligence - Jeff Hawkins; Richard Dawkins; Basic Books.pdf#p194]
- Neither brain uploading nor merging with computers addresses the existential risks facing humanity from the old brain and false beliefs. [raw/books/neuroscience/A Thousand Brains _ A New Theory of Intelligence - Jeff Hawkins; Richard Dawkins; Basic Books.pdf#p194]
- Phil Kennedy implanted electrodes in the left motor region of locked-in patients, allowing them to move a computer cursor by imagining moving their left hand. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p346]
- The BrainGate system with 96 electrodes was implanted in Matthew Nagle, a quadriplegic patient who could open e-mail, draw, adjust TV controls, and move a robotic hand by thought alone. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p348]
- Apostolos Georgopoulos found that recording only 100-150 neurons could accurately predict direction of muscular movement in three-dimensional space. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p349]
- Jonathan Wolpaw developed EEG-based BCIs where people could learn to control brain waves to move a computer cursor. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p351]
- Richard Andersen proposes using the posterior parietal cortex rather than motor cortex, as it specifies goals rather than specific biomechanical movements. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p350]

## Inferences

- Inference: BCIs represent a paradigm shift where neurons that normally contribute to motor control must now perform the entire job of producing motor output, requiring the brain to learn entirely new output pathways.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Current BCI performance remains variable and cursor movements are described as slow and jerky. The ideal balance between controlling a process versus selecting a goal in BCI design remains to be determined.
