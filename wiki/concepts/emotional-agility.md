# Emotional Agility

## Summary

Emotional agility is the capacity to recognize and accept one's internal states—thoughts, feelings, and impulses—while maintaining flexibility in behavior so that values-based actions can be taken. It involves being aware of emotions, accepting them without judgment, and using that awareness to guide behavior effectively. Unlike emotional intelligence, which focuses on recognizing emotions in oneself and others, emotional agility emphasizes the dynamic process of responding to emotions rather than just identifying them.

## Key facts

- Emotional agility is defined as being flexible with thoughts and feelings to respond optimally to everyday situations. [raw/books/psychology/Emotional Agility - Susan David.pdf#p13]
- Emotional rigidity—getting hooked by thoughts, feelings, and behaviors that don't serve us—is associated with depression and anxiety. [raw/books/psychology/Emotional Agility - Susan David.pdf#p13]
- Emotional agility is not about controlling thoughts or forcing positive thinking, as research shows such attempts usually don't work and can be counterproductive. [raw/books/psychology/Emotional Agility - Susan David.pdf#p13]
- The concept is based on Viktor Frankl's idea that 'between stimulus and response there is a space, in that space is our power to choose our response'. [raw/books/psychology/Emotional Agility - Susan David.pdf#p13]
- Emotionally agile people demonstrate flexibility, tolerate high levels of stress, remain engaged and open, and act according to their core values. [raw/books/psychology/Emotional Agility - Susan David.pdf#p13]
- Emotional agility has been shown to help people with negative self-image, heartbreak, pain, anxiety, depression, procrastination, and tough transitions. [raw/books/psychology/Emotional Agility - Susan David.pdf#p13]
