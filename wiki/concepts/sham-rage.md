# Sham rage

## Summary

Sham rage was a term used by early investigators to describe aggressive displays induced by electrical brain stimulation (ESB), based on the assumption that animals were not experiencing true affect. This seemed plausible because some subjects could be petted even while hissing and snarling. However, such sites appear to be quite low in the brain stem and in the minority. Current evidence suggests that most electrode placements above the mesencephalon do evoke a central state indistinguishable from normal anger, and humans stimulated at such sites report experiencing intense rage.

## Key facts

- Early investigators called aggressive displays induced by ESB 'sham rage,' based on the assumption that animals were not experiencing true affect. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p106]
- Some subjects could be petted even while they were hissing and snarling, supporting the sham rage interpretation. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p106]
- Sites that evoke only motor patterns without affective experience appear to be quite low in the brain stem and in the minority. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p106]
- Most electrode placements above the mesencephalon do evoke a central state indistinguishable from normal anger. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p106]
- Humans stimulated at brain sites that evoke rage have reported experiencing a feeling of intense rage, providing compelling evidence against the sham rage interpretation. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p106]

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The specific brain stem sites that may only activate motor patterns without affective experience have not been fully characterized.
