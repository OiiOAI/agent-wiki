# Machine

## Summary

In Carse's philosophy, 'machine' represents one of two fundamental attitudes toward nature. It denotes a mode of engagement where nature is treated as a mechanical system of cause-and-effect to be controlled and manipulated. Unlike the garden, the machine requires force introduced from without—it must be designed, constructed, and fueled by external agents. Machinery is inherently contradictory: it is used to increase power while actually decreasing freedom, and operating machines requires operators to become machine-like themselves.

## Key facts

- A machine is driven by a force introduced from without; no machine has the source of its spontaneity within itself. [raw/books/philosophy/Finite and Infinite Games_ A Vision of Life as Play and - Carse, James.pdf#p151]
- To operate a machine one must operate like a machine; using a machine to do what we cannot do, we find we must do what the machine does. [raw/books/philosophy/Finite and Infinite Games_ A Vision of Life as Play and - Carse, James.pdf#p156]
- Machines do not make us into machines when we operate them; we make ourselves into machinery to operate them. [raw/books/philosophy/Finite and Infinite Games_ A Vision of Life as Play and - Carse, James.pdf#p156]
- Machinery is contradictory: we use it against ourselves, against itself, and against each other. [raw/books/philosophy/Finite and Infinite Games_ A Vision of Life as Play and - Carse, James.pdf#p157]
- The goal of technology is to eliminate itself, to become silent, invisible, carefree—when machinery functions perfectly it ceases to be there, but so do we. [raw/books/philosophy/Finite and Infinite Games_ A Vision of Life as Play and - Carse, James.pdf#p147]
- The more waste a society produces, the more vigorously it must deny producing any waste at all. [raw/books/philosophy/Finite and Infinite Games_ A Vision of Life as Play and - Carse, James.pdf#p167]
