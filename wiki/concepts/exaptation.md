# Exaptation

## Summary

Exaptation is the evolutionary process by which a structure that was selected for one function turns out to be useful for some other, often completely novel, purpose. The book applies this concept to human cognition: thinking may have evolved for controlling action, but the expansion of neural resources and the recursive architecture of our cognitive systems gave us the ability to think about our thoughts. This 'cognitive exaptation' freed our minds, enabling open-ended creative thought, imagination, and the capacity for art, music, science, and abstract reasoning. The evolution of feathers (initially for thermoregulation, later for flight) is a classic biological example of exaptation.

## Key facts

- Exaptation is when a structure selected for one function turns out to be useful for some other, often completely novel, purpose. [raw/books/neuroscience/Free Agents _ How Evolution Gave Us Free Will - Kevin J_ Mitchell.pdf#p293]
- Co-opting feathers to support flight, even though they were probably initially selected for thermoregulation, is a prime example of exaptation. [raw/books/neuroscience/Free Agents _ How Evolution Gave Us Free Will - Kevin J_ Mitchell.pdf#p293]
- In humans, a kind of cognitive exaptation occurred: thinking evolved for controlling action, but the recursive architecture gave us the ability to think about our thoughts, creating a world of ideas and freeing our minds. [raw/books/neuroscience/Free Agents _ How Evolution Gave Us Free Will - Kevin J_ Mitchell.pdf#p294]

## Inferences

- Inference: The emergence of consciousness may represent such a cognitive exaptation—evolving initially for better cognitive control and self-regulation, but enabling the transformative capacity for imagination and abstract thought.
