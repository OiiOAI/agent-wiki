# Artificial intelligence

## Summary

Artificial intelligence (AI) originated in 1956 with the goal of creating machines that could simulate human intelligence. The field divides into 'weak AI' (software for specific problem-solving tasks that has permeated daily life) and 'strong AI' (machines that could genuinely comprehend and be self-aware). Debate continues about whether machines can ever achieve consciousness.

## Key facts

- John Searle coined 'strong AI,' which presupposes that appropriately programmed computers can literally understand and have cognitive states. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p367]
- Searle argues that all conscious states are caused by lower-level brain processes and require a body—consciousness is an emergent physical property of the entire body's input. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p367]
- Deep Blue beat world chess champion Garry Kasparov not by being smarter but by evaluating 200 million positions per second—it played chess without understanding chess. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p367]
- No computer has passed the Turing Test, proposed by Alan Turing in 1950 to determine if machines can think. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p370]
- Kurzweil enumerates differences between brains and computers: brains are slower but massively parallel, constantly rewiring, use emergent properties, and are democratic with distributed networks. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p369]
