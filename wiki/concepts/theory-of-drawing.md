# Theory of Drawing

## Summary

Gibson proposes a theory of drawing based on the 'fundamental graphic act'—the making of traces on a surface that constitute a progressive record of movement. Drawing proper involves making lines that record movements of the tool while also recording awareness in terms of invariants picked up from the environment. He argues against the traditional theory that drawing is copying (from life, memory, or imagination), claiming instead that what a draftsman does is mark a surface to display invariants and record awareness. Drawing is never copying; only another drawing can be copied.

## Key facts

- The fundamental graphic act is the making of traces on a surface that constitute a progressive record of movement. The permanent trace is what interests the child. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p294]
- Drawing is never copying. It is impossible to copy a piece of the environment. Only another drawing can be copied. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p298]
- The child learning to draw depicts the invariants that he has learned to notice. He does not draw in patchwork perspective because he never had the experience of a patchwork. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p305]
