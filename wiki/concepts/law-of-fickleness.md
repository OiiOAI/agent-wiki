# Law of Fickleness

## Summary

The Law of Ficklessness describes a fundamental paradox in human leadership: people simultaneously crave leadership and fear it, wanting to be led while also wanting freedom, desiring protection and prosperity without sacrifice, and both worshipping and wanting to kill their rulers. This ambivalence toward leaders originates in childhood associations with parental figures and is exacerbated by the knowledge that those in power will likely use privileges to accumulate more power. The paradox makes loyalty 'notoriously fickle and volatile.' True authority—defined as augmented presence and credibility—resolves this paradox by establishing genuine trust through vision and empathy, creating a bulwark against the natural volatility of public support.

## Key facts

- People are always ambivalent about those in power: they want to be led but also to feel free; they want protection and prosperity without sacrifices; they both worship the king and want to kill him. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p535]
- This ambivalence toward leaders operates because people intuitively recognize the need for leaders while also fearing and despising those above them due to concerns about abuse of power and loss of autonomy. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p556]
- The ambivalence tips negative when leaders show signs of abuse, insensitivity, or incompetence, leading to sudden shifts in loyalty that can be shocking in their intensity. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p557]
- Leaders often mistake expressions of deference for genuine support, not realizing people almost always show such behavior because their fate is in leaders' hands and they cannot afford to show true feelings. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p557]
- Historically, revered chiefs and kings were routinely put to death if they showed weakness, lost battles, or were seen as favoring their own clan—releasing pent-up hostility toward leaders. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p557]
- Authority (from Latin auctoritas, meaning 'to increase or augment') represents an augmented presence, increased prestige and credibility earned through embodying higher ideals that transcend daily politics. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p558]
- The twin pillars of authority are: (1) vision—the ability to provide far-reaching perspective and work for the greater good, and (2) empathy—attunement to the shifting moods of group members and responsiveness to their needs. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p559]

## Inferences

- Inference: The law explains why leaders who appear to be 'friends' or 'equals' to their subordinates undermine their own authority—they signal weakness rather than strength, stirring the ambivalence toward resentment.
