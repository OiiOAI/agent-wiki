# Evolution of Aging

## Summary

The evolutionary theory of aging explains why organisms age through two complementary mechanisms: the Medawar effect (accumulation of late-acting harmful mutations due to weak selection against them) and the Williams effect (accumulation of mutations beneficial early in life but harmful later). Together these create positive feedback pushing toward shorter lifespans.

## Key facts

- Peter Medawar proposed in the 1940s that mutations with late-acting harmful effects accumulate because almost no individuals survive long enough for selection to act against them. [raw/books/neuroscience/Other Minds_ The Octopus, the Sea, and the - Peter Godfrey - Smith.pdf#p130]
- George Williams proposed in 1957 that mutations with beneficial early effects and harmful late effects will be favored by selection despite their long-term cost. [raw/books/neuroscience/Other Minds_ The Octopus, the Sea, and the - Peter Godfrey - Smith.pdf#p132]
- The Medawar and Williams effects create positive feedback: as lifespan shortens, selection pressure against late-acting mutations weakens further. [raw/books/neuroscience/Other Minds_ The Octopus, the Sea, and the - Peter Godfrey - Smith.pdf#p132]
- Trees avoid the Medawar-Williams effects because very old trees are more fertile than young ones, maintaining selection pressure for longevity. [raw/books/neuroscience/Other Minds_ The Octopus, the Sea, and the - Peter Godfrey - Smith.pdf#p133]
- Cephalopod short lifespans result from their vulnerability to predators combined with the Medawar-Williams evolutionary dynamics. [raw/books/neuroscience/Other Minds_ The Octopus, the Sea, and the - Peter Godfrey - Smith.pdf#p137]

## Inferences

- Inference: The deep-sea octopus's 4.5-year brooding confirms that octopus bodies have no physiological barrier to long life—lifespan is tuned by predation risk through Medawar-Williams dynamics.
