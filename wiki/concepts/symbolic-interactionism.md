# Symbolic Interactionism

## Summary

Symbolic interactionism was Chicago sociology's most important theoretical product, developed largely out of Georg Simmel's interest in action and interaction and George Herbert Mead's interest in consciousness. The approach focuses on actors, action, and interaction, and the meanings that emerge through social interaction. It is a social-psychological theory that stands in contrast to primarily societal theories offered by European theorists. Although symbolic interactionism lost ground to structural functionalism in the mid-twentieth century, it remained alive through figures like Herbert Blumer (who coined the term in 1937) and Erving Goffman's dramaturgical analysis. The tradition continued with major exponents dispersed throughout the country and the world.

## Inferences

- Inference: The 'I' and 'me' distinction provides a mechanism within Mead's framework for both social stability (through the 'me' ensuring conformity) and social change (through the 'I' introducing novelty), resolving the apparent tension between social constraint and individual agency.
