# Reciprocal Exchange

## Summary

Social interactions involving the exchange of goods, services, or cooperation based on mutual expectations. Tasks such as Trust, Prisoner's Dilemma, and Public Goods games are used to study the neural basis of reciprocal behavior, which involves the brain's reward system including the mesolimbic dopamine system and striatum.

## Key facts

- The mesolimbic dopamine system is a strong candidate for a common neural reward metric that allows comparison of options offered in different forms. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroscience of Decision Making - Mandel, David R.,Vartanian, Oshin.pdf#p323]
- Midbrain dopamine neurons track reward prediction errors, and the caudate nucleus is involved in learning contingencies between response and outcome. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroscience of Decision Making - Mandel, David R.,Vartanian, Oshin.pdf#p323]
- In the Prisoner's Dilemma Game, reciprocated cooperation leads to increased activation in caudate and nucleus accumbens compared with earning identical money without social involvement. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroscience of Decision Making - Mandel, David R.,Vartanian, Oshin.pdf#p324]
- Unreciprocated cooperation shows corresponding decrease in activation in the striatum, and striatal activation predicts increased cooperation in subsequent rounds of iterated games. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroscience of Decision Making - Mandel, David R.,Vartanian, Oshin.pdf#p324]
- In a multi-round Trust Game, trustee caudate activation corresponded to an 'intention to trust' signal that gradually shifted temporally, similar to reward prediction errors in reinforcement learning. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroscience of Decision Making - Mandel, David R.,Vartanian, Oshin.pdf#p324]

## Inferences

- Inference: The human striatum is centrally involved in social decisions, above and beyond any financial outcome, encoding abstract valuations such as feelings of mutual cooperation.
- Inference: Social decision making may recruit basic reward mechanisms to construct a model of the social domain.
