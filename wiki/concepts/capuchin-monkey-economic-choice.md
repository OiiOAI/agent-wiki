# Capuchin Monkey Economic Choice

## Summary

Research by Chen, Lakshminarayanan, and Santos demonstrates that capuchin monkeys (Cebus apella) exhibit economic choice behavior remarkably similar to humans, obeying price theory principles and displaying key behavioral economics biases including loss aversion, reference dependence, framing effects, and the endowment effect.

## Key facts

- Capuchins obey price theory: when the price of one good dropped by half, capuchins shifted consumption to the cheaper good, responding rationally to price shocks. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p86]
- Capuchins prefer options that stochastically dominate: when choosing between traders offering the same good, capuchins reliably preferred the risky experimenter who gave a higher average payoff (1.5 pieces vs 1 piece of apple). [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p86]
- Capuchins display reference dependence: they reliably preferred the experimenter who delivered apple pieces by way of a perceived gain over one who delivered the same average payoff by way of a perceived loss. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p88]
- Capuchins exhibit loss aversion: monkeys robustly preferred the experimenter who gave what was expected (one piece) over one who always delivered a loss (promised two, gave one), despite both delivering the same payoff on every trial. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p88]
- Capuchins show a reflection effect: in gains they avoided risk (preferred safe experimenter), but in losses they preferred risk (preferred risky experimenter), mirroring human risk preference reversal. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p89]
- Capuchins exhibit an endowment effect: when endowed with one of two equally preferred goods, capuchins reliably preferred to keep the endowed good rather than trade for the other, even when compensated for transaction costs. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p91]

## Inferences

- Inference: Human behavioral biases (loss aversion, reference dependence, framing effects) likely result not from species-unique market experiences or cultural learning, but from more basic evolved strategies present in our common ancestor with other New World primate species.
- Inference: Behavioral biases may emerge in the absence of market experience, not only in capuchins but potentially in humans as well.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The precise neural mechanisms underlying capuchin economic biases remain to be identified through physiological investigation.
- Uncertain: The extent to which infant humans show these biases before market experience is still being investigated.
