# Triune Brain Theory

## Summary

Paul MacLean's influential but now largely discredited theory proposing that the mammalian forebrain consists of three evolutionarily distinct parts: a reptilian complex (basal ganglia), a paleomammalian complex (limbic system), and a neomammalian complex (neocortex), each with specialized functions.

## Key facts

- Paul MacLean synthesized findings in the 1950s proposing the triune brain, consisting of reptilian, paleomammalian, and neomammalian complexes arranged hierarchically. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Deep History of Ourselves_ 4 - Billion - Year Story of How - Joseph E LeDoux; Caio Da Silva Sorrentino.pdf#p192]
- According to MacLean's theory, the reptilian complex (basal ganglia) was responsible for primitive species-specific behaviors like aggression, dominance, and territoriality. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Deep History of Ourselves_ 4 - Billion - Year Story of How - Joseph E LeDoux; Caio Da Silva Sorrentino.pdf#p193]
- The paleomammalian complex (limbic system) was proposed to enable emotions for the first time in mammals, with connections to the hypothalamus controlling behavioral and visceral functions. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Deep History of Ourselves_ 4 - Billion - Year Story of How - Joseph E LeDoux; Caio Da Silva Sorrentino.pdf#p193]
- The theory is problematic because 'neocortex' did not first appear in late mammals, 'paleocortex' and limbic areas did not first appear in early mammals, and basal ganglia appeared before reptiles. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Deep History of Ourselves_ 4 - Billion - Year Story of How - Joseph E LeDoux; Caio Da Silva Sorrentino.pdf#p195]
- The limbic system theory was based on flawed assumptions that limbic areas handle emotion but not cognition, while neocortex handles cognition but not emotion—both assumptions are wrong. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Deep History of Ourselves_ 4 - Billion - Year Story of How - Joseph E LeDoux; Caio Da Silva Sorrentino.pdf#p195]

## Inferences

- Inference: The triune brain theory's influence persists despite scientific problems because it intuitively appeals to common notions about brain organization.
