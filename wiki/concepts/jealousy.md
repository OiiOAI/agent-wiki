# Jealousy

## Summary

Jealousy is described as a complex emotion combining possessiveness, suspicion, rage, and humiliation. Perel notes a cultural divide: in Latin American cultures, jealousy is viewed as natural and even as a sign of love ('erotic rage'), while in Anglo-Saxon Protestant cultures, it became pathologized after the 1970s as incompatible with modern ideals of free choice and equality. Jealousy contains inherent paradoxes—we need to love to be jealous, yet love 'should' eliminate jealousy.

## Key facts

- Jealousy combines possessiveness, suspicion, rage, and humiliation, threatening one's core sense of self. [raw/books/psychology/The State of Affairs - Esther Perel.pdf#p84]
- In Latin American cultures, jealousy is seen as an 'erotic rage' and a natural companion of love. [raw/books/psychology/The State of Affairs - Esther Perel.pdf#p84]
- After 1970, jealousy in American culture became increasingly viewed as an inappropriate remnant of old marriage models based on ownership and dependency. [raw/books/psychology/The State of Affairs - Esther Perel.pdf#p85]
- Philosopher Roland Barthes described the jealous person as suffering four times: from jealousy itself, self-blame for feeling it, fear of wounding the other, and the banality of the feeling. [raw/books/psychology/The State of Affairs - Esther Perel.pdf#p86]
- The American literature on infidelity largely ignores jealousy, focusing instead on betrayal, violated trust, and lying. [raw/books/psychology/The State of Affairs - Esther Perel.pdf#p84]

## Inferences

- Inference: The cultural suppression of jealousy in modern Western relationships may paradoxically increase individual suffering by denying a natural emotional response.
- Inference: Jealousy's role as 'gatekeeper of monogamy' in some cultures suggests it serves a bonding function by maintaining awareness of potential rivals.
