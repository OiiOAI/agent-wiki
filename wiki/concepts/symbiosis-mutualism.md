# Symbiosis and Mutualism

## Summary

Symbiosis refers to close and often long-term interactions between members of different species. When both partners benefit, the relationship is called mutualism. Examples include ants farming fungi in underground gardens, ants cultivating and protecting aphids, cleaner fish serving larger fish, and the relationship between mitochondria and eukaryotic cells. Such relationships evolve when each partner can bring different 'skills' to the partnership.

## Key facts

- Parasol ants in South America cultivate fungus gardens in underground chambers, chewing leaves into fragments to make compost for the fungi. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p218]
- Aphids excrete sugar-rich 'honeydew' which ants 'milk' by stroking the aphid's hind-quarters; in return, ants protect aphids from predators. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p218]
- Mitochondria are plausibly argued to be, in origin, symbiotic bacteria that joined forces with our type of cell very early in evolution. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p219]
- Lichens are intimate symbiotic unions between fungi and green algae where neither partner could live without the other. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p219]
- The cleaner fish relationship involves about fifty species of small fish and shrimps picking parasites off larger fish, with clients refraining from eating the cleaners. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p224]

## Inferences

- Inference: Fundamental asymmetries between species can lead to evolutionarily stable strategies of mutual cooperation
- Inference: Associations of mutual benefit will evolve if each partner can get more out than they put in, whether between members of the same or different species
