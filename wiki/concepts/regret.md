# Regret

## Summary

Regret is a counterfactual emotion triggered by comparing the realized outcome with an alternative that was rejected or available but not taken. It is more intense when the deviation from normal behavior was a result of action rather than inaction, and when the alternative outcome is concrete and easily imagined. Anticipated regret motivates risk-averse choices and contributes to the status quo bias, while actual regret serves as a self-punishment mechanism that influences future decisions.

## Key facts

- Participants asked who would feel more regret about the same outcome—Mr. Brown who rarely offers rides and was robbed versus Mr. Smith who frequently offers rides and was robbed—88% said Mr. Brown, because the unusual action makes the counterfactual 'if only I had not offered a ride' more vivid and painful. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p448]
- Participants who were asked 'Would you take another card?' showed more regret when they lost than those asked 'Would you stay?' even though both groups faced the same outcome from the same decision, because the phrasing primed the action frame and the associated counterfactual. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p450]
- The asymmetry between action and inaction extends to blame: when others evaluate the same outcomes, they blame Mr. Smith (who took the risk) more than Mr. Brown (who did not), even though Mr. Brown experienced more regret—showing that personal regret and social blame are driven by different reference points. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p448]
- Physicians are less likely to recommend unconventional treatments because proposing an unusual approach that fails generates more regret and blame than the credit received for success, even though the unconventional option may have higher expected value. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p450]

## Inferences

- Inference: Awareness of the action/inaction asymmetry can be used to reduce regret: explicitly acknowledging that one chose not to act because that was the conventional choice at the time, rather than because of a specific reason, can prevent the retrospective amplification of foregone alternatives.
