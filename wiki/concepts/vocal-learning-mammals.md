# Vocal learning in mammals

## Summary

Vocal learning in mammals is highly limited compared to birds. Except for some dolphins, whales, and possibly Hoover the seal, other mammals have quite limited vocal abilities. Mammals are poor at learning to produce new vocalizations beyond minimal variants on innate ones. The key to the vocal difference between birds and mammals relates to differences in anatomy: birds evolved to use skeletal muscle control for sound production while mammals use visceral motor systems that produce stereotypic preprogrammed movements.

## Key facts

- Except for some dolphins and whales, other mammals have quite limited vocal abilities and are poor at learning to produce new vocalizations. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p245]
- Monkeys and apes are well known for their mimickry, except when it comes to vocalization—this is not a failure of general cognitive abilities but the lack of a special skill for producing diverse vocal sounds. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p246]
- Dolphins and whales produce sounds using air passing through specialized sinuses leading to their blowholes, which are controlled by skeletal motor nuclei, unlike terrestrial mammals that use the visceral motor-controlled larynx. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p259]
- In birds, the syrinx is controlled by the ventral subnucleus of the hypoglossal nucleus, which corresponds to the part that controls the geniohyoid muscle in mammals—a visceral motor function that has been transferred to skeletal motor control in birds. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p258]

## Inferences

- Inference: The shift to skeletal muscle control of sound production in birds and cetaceans allowed for more flexible vocal learning because skeletal motor systems are capable of plasticity and modification, unlike visceral motor systems that operate autonomously according to set programs.
