# RAGE System

## Summary

The RAGE system is an emotional operating system that generates aggressive and defensive responses when organisms face frustration, threat, or interference with goal-directed behavior. It produces the internal state of anger that motivates attack and defensive behaviors.

## Key facts

- RAGE can be reliably evoked in all mammals by electrical stimulation of specific subcortical brain regions. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p28]
- Animals either like or dislike brain stimulation as inferred from behavioral criteria such as conditioned approach and avoidance. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p28]
- The RAGE system generates coherent emotional response patterns that coordinate behavioral and physiological responses. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p28]
- RAGE circuits run from medial areas of the amygdala through discrete zones of the hypothalamus down into the periaqueductal gray of the midbrain. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p102]
- Electrical stimulation of the perifornix region of the lateral hypothalamus produces quiet-biting attack, while stimulation of medial hypothalamic areas produces rage-like behaviors. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p105]
- During affective attack, animals exhibit piloerection, autonomic arousal, hissing, and growling, while during quiet-biting attack they exhibit methodical stalking. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p105]
- Frustration from interrupted goal-directed behavior increases aggression in proportion to desire intensity and frequency of thwarting. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p104]
- Males are generally more aggressive than females partly due to organizational and activational effects of testosterone on aggression circuits. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p102]
- Animals with constitutionally low brain serotonin activity are more prone to aggression and impulsive emotional acting out. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p102]
- The core of the RAGE system runs from medial amygdaloid areas downward via the stria terminalis to the medial hypothalamus, and from there to specific locations within the PAG of the midbrain. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p106]
- The RAGE system is organized hierarchically, meaning aggression evoked from the highest areas in the amygdala is critically dependent on the lower regions, while aggression from lower sites does not depend critically on the integrity of the higher areas. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p106]
- Lesions of both medial hypothalamic and PAG zones dramatically diminish rage evoked from the amygdala, but not vice versa. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p106]
- From diencephalic zones around the medial hypothalamus, the aggressive tendency is critically dependent on the integrity of the PAG but not of the medial amygdala. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p106]
- PAG sites that support rage behaviors receive inputs primarily from six areas of the brain, including several areas of the cortex, the medial hypothalamus, and several zones of the lower brain stem. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p107]
- Humans stimulated at brain sites that evoke rage have reported experiencing a feeling of intense rage, providing compelling evidence that ESB evokes a true affective feeling. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p106]
- Animals readily learn to turn off ESB that generates affective attack, indicating they have unpleasant affective experiences during such stimulation. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p106]

## Inferences

- Inference: The RAGE system likely involves hypothalamic and periaqueductal gray regions based on electrical stimulation studies
- Inference: The RAGE and SEEKING circuits may have mutually inhibitory interactions, so that heightened expectations promote frustration when rewards fail to materialize.
- Inference: The six major inputs to PAG for RAGE include: (1) frontal cortex (medial areas with reward-relevance neurons, plus lateral frontal eye fields), (2) orbitoinsular cortex (especially insular area where pain and hearing converge), and (3) medial hypothalamus. These areas may represent different types of irritations that can trigger anger.
- Inference: The primary evolved function of anger appears to be motivating individuals to compete effectively for environmental resources.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The developmental trajectory of RAGE expression is less well studied than other systems
- Uncertain: Whether distinct aggression circuits exist for predatory versus affective attack versus intermale competition remains incompletely resolved.
- Uncertain: The relative importance of different cortical inputs for the generation of frustration is not clear
