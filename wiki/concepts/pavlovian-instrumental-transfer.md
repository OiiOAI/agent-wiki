# Pavlovian-Instrumental Transfer

## Summary

Pavlovian-instrumental transfer (PIT) refers to the phenomenon whereby stimuli associated with appetitive or aversive outcomes through Pavlovian conditioning influence the vigor and choice of instrumental actions. This transfer depends on the amygdala and nucleus accumbens, and can lead to both beneficial and deleterious behaviors, including some economic anomalies.

## Key facts

- Pavlovian-instrumental transfer boosts the vigor with which subjects perform instrumental responses based on stimuli associated with particular outcomes. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p174]
- Specific PIT, where stimuli predicting the same outcome as the instrumental behavior boost response, depends on the basolateral amygdala and nucleus accumbens shell. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p175]
- General PIT, where stimuli predicting motivationally relevant outcomes boost responses even for different instrumental outcomes, depends on the central amygdala and nucleus accumbens core. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p175]
- Conditioned suppression refers to stimuli predicting aversive outcomes suppressing appetitive instrumental responding. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p175]
- Pavlovian responses may interfere more with instrumental habits than with goal-directed actions. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p175]
- In Pavlovian-instrumental transfer, presentation of stimuli trained as predictors of specific outcomes selectively biases choice toward actions associated with those same outcomes in a free-choice test. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p371]
- Corbit and Balleine (2005) demonstrated that rats trained with tone predicting grain pellets and noise predicting sucrose, then instrumentally trained with different levers earning each food, showed selective bias in choice when tested with the cues present. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p371]
- Degrading a cue's contingent predictive relationship by delivering outcomes both during and outside the cue eliminates Pavlovian-instrumental transfer, showing the effect depends on the cue's status as a net predictor. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p371]

## Inferences

- Inference: Pavlovian-instrumental transfer provides a mechanistic explanation for framing effects in economic choice, where positively vs negatively framed options automatically engage approach or withdrawal responses.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Interactions between model-based Pavlovian predictions and model-free instrumental actions have not been systematically studied.
