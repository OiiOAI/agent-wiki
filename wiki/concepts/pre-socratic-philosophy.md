# Pre-Socratic Philosophy

## Summary

Pre-Socratic philosophy began in the sixth century BC with an attempt to reconcile the apparent unity of the phenomenal world with its obvious diversity through positing a common originary principle (arch). McGilchrist interprets this tradition through his hemispheric hypothesis: early thinkers like Thales, Anaximander, and Heraclitus showed insights into the right-hemisphere world, while Parmenides represented the turn toward left-hemisphere abstraction and the dismissal of phenomenal reality as deception.

## Key facts

- Pre-Socratic philosophy attempted to reconcile the apparent unity of the phenomenal world with its diversity by positing a common originary principle (arch). [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p372]
- Early pre-Socratics (Thales, Anaximander, Heraclitus) showed insights into the right-hemisphere world: attention to process, the unbounded, the implicit, and the coming together of opposites. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p373]
- Parmenides represented the turn toward left-hemisphere abstraction: denying the testimony of the senses, prioritizing logical system over experience, and seeing the phenomenal world as deception. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p377]
- Plato's belief that knowledge must be unfailing and general led to the position that we cannot know things that are changing or particular, making knowledge equivalent to abstract, ideal Forms. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p378]
