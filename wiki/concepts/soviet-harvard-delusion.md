# The Soviet-Harvard Delusion

## Summary

A delusion combining Soviet-style top-down planning with Harvard-style academic overconfidence in knowledge. The Soviet-Harvard illusion refers to the belief that things can be planned, designed, and managed from above through academic knowledge, when in reality innovation and progress come from antifragile tinkering and trial-and-error at the grassroots level. This delusion underlies much of fragilista interventionism.

## Key facts

- The Soviet-Harvard delusion combines unscientific overestimation of the reach of scientific knowledge with top-down planning assumptions, leading to pseudo-stabilization and harm to systems. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p15]
- There is compelling evidence that the world does not function thanks to programmed design, university research, and bureaucratic funding—this is an illusion called 'lecturing birds how to fly.'. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p25]
- Technology is the result of antifragility exploited by risk-takers through tinkering and trial and error, with nerd-driven design confined to the backstage while history books are written by academics. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p25]
- Hayek understood organic price formation but did not extend his ideas about fragility to risk; for Hayek, bureaucrats were inefficient, not fragilistas. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p38]

## Inferences

- Inference: The Soviet-Harvard delusion explains why top-down interventions in complex systems consistently fail—the interventions assume predictability and controllability that do not exist in complex adaptive systems.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Whether the term 'Soviet-Harvard' adequately captures the phenomenon or if it overgeneralizes from specific historical examples.
