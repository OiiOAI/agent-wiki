# Theories of Dreaming and Its Function

## Summary

Freud categorized existing theories of dreams into three groups based on their assumptions about the amount and nature of psychical activity during dreaming.

## Key facts

- The first group of theories holds that the whole of psychical activity continues in dreams, with Delboeuf as a major proponent; these theories cannot suggest any function for dreaming. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p1350]
- The second group presupposes dreams imply a lowering of psychical activity, a loosening of connections, and impoverishment of material; Binz held that dreams are somatic processes useless and often pathological. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p1352]
- Robert's theory describes dreams as a somatic process of excretion; dreams serve as a safety-valve for the overburdened brain, possessing power to heal and relieve. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p1353]
- The third group ascribes to the dreaming mind a capacity for special psychical activities, such as Burdach's view of dreaming as refreshment collecting new strength for the day's work. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p1357]

## Inferences

- Inference: Scherner's theory, which Freud examines in detail, represents dream imagination as liberated from reason's domination, employing somatic stimuli as material for its artistic constructions.
- Inference: The medical/physiological view contrasts with the philosophical view in attributing dreams primarily to somatic rather than psychical causes.
