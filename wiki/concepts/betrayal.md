# Betrayal

## Summary

Betrayal in the context of infidelity involves deceit, abandonment, rejection, and humiliation. Perel argues that modern infidelity is particularly traumatic because it shatters the grand ambitions of contemporary romantic love—beliefs that one is special, chosen, and irreplaceable. The discovery of an affair attacks identity itself, not just the relationship. Post-affair recovery involves three phases: crisis, meaning-making, and visioning.

## Key facts

- Infidelity is betrayal on multiple levels: deceit, abandonment, rejection, and humiliation. [raw/books/psychology/The State of Affairs - Esther Perel.pdf#p54]
- When marriage was an economic arrangement, infidelity threatened economic security; today marriage being romantic, infidelity threatens emotional security. [raw/books/psychology/The State of Affairs - Esther Perel.pdf#p52]
- The betrayed partner often suffers a loss of coherent life narrative, which Fraenkel describes as 'robbing someone of his or her story.'. [raw/books/psychology/The State of Affairs - Esther Perel.pdf#p61]
- Infidelity is not just loss of love but loss of self-identity. [raw/books/psychology/The State of Affairs - Esther Perel.pdf#p63]
- The shift from shame to guilt is crucial for the unfaithful partner—shame is self-absorption while guilt is an empathic, relational response to hurt caused. [raw/books/psychology/The State of Affairs - Esther Perel.pdf#p67]

## Inferences

- Inference: Modern Western couples' heightened vulnerability to betrayal stems from having invested their entire identity in the romantic relationship.
