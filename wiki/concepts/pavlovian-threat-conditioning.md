# Threat Conditioning

## Summary

Pavlovian threat conditioning is a form of associative learning where a neutral stimulus (conditioned stimulus, CS) becomes capable of eliciting defensive responses after being paired with an aversive unconditioned stimulus (US). In this process, information about the CS and US converges onto neurons in the lateral amygdala, leading to strengthened synaptic connections. Donald Hebb's hypothesis that co-activation of neurons by weak and strong stimuli modifies those neurons' chemistry was confirmed optogenetically in LeDoux's lab. The conditioned CS can later activate LA neurons on its own, triggering downstream defensive responses via the central amygdala.

## Key facts

- In threat conditioning, a neutral conditioned stimulus (CS) becomes threatening when paired with an aversive unconditioned stimulus (US). [raw/books/neuroscience/Anxious - Joseph LeDoux.pdf#p143]
- Hebbian learning occurs when weak and strong inputs converge onto the same neurons, enabling the weak input to activate neurons more strongly. [raw/books/neuroscience/Anxious - Joseph LeDoux.pdf#p143]
- Optogenetic studies confirmed that direct activation of lateral amygdala neurons paired with a tone CS is sufficient to produce freezing behavior. [raw/books/neuroscience/Anxious - Joseph LeDoux.pdf#p151]
- Electron microscopy revealed that threat conditioning results in structural changes in amygdala synapses, including movement of spine apparatus into spines and increased protein synthesis machinery. [raw/books/neuroscience/Anxious - Joseph LeDoux.pdf#p152]

## Inferences

- Inference: Physical structural changes in brain architecture enable threat memories to persist over time.
- Inference: The molecular changes underlying threat conditioning involve numerous molecules that contribute to both learning induction and memory storage stabilization.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The complete molecular cascade of threat memory formation is not yet fully characterized.
