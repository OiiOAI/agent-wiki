# Self-Pity and Self-Serving Bias

## Summary

Munger warns that self-pity is always counterproductive and can approach paranoia. He describes a friend's practice of handing out cards saying 'Your story has touched my heart. Never have I heard of anyone with as many misfortunes as you.' He also warns against self-serving bias, which makes people think what's good for them is good for civilization, requiring deliberate countermeasures.

## Key facts

- Munger states: 'Self-pity is always counterproductive. It's the wrong way to think. And when you avoid it, you get a great advantage over everybody else.'. [raw/books/strategy/Poor Charlie's Almanack_ The Wit and Wisdo - Charles T. Munger, Peter D. Kaufman, Ed We.pdf#p441]
- Munger uses Mozart as an example of a man with a self-serving bias about spending, noting Mozart 'always overspent his income' and was 'utterly miserable most of the time.'. [raw/books/strategy/Poor Charlie's Almanack_ The Wit and Wisdo - Charles T. Munger, Peter D. Kaufman, Ed We.pdf#p441]
- Munger states: 'If you don't allow for self-serving bias in the conduct of others, you are, again, a fool.'. [raw/books/strategy/Poor Charlie's Almanack_ The Wit and Wisdo - Charles T. Munger, Peter D. Kaufman, Ed We.pdf#p442]
- Envy, resentment, revenge, and self-pity are 'disastrous modes of thought,' with self-pity being 'one of the very hardest things to reverse.'. [raw/books/strategy/Poor Charlie's Almanack_ The Wit and Wisdo - Charles T. Munger, Peter D. Kaufman, Ed We.pdf#p440]

## Inferences

- Inference: Self-pity training works through recognition that the narrative is universal—all humans have misfortunes—and that dwelling on them does not change outcomes but does impair functioning.
