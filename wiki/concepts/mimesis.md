# Mimesis

## Summary

Mimesis (human imitation) is argued to be the ultimately most important human skill and a critical development in the evolution of the human brain. Unlike mechanical copying, mimesis is an imaginative inhabiting of the other, grounded in empathy and the body. It is through mimesis that humans bridge the gap between selves and share in what another feels and does. Mirror neurones support this: when we imitate or even imagine something, some of the same neurones involved in direct perception are brought into play.

## Key facts

- Human imitation is not slavish or mechanical; it introduces variety and uniqueness to the 'copy', which remains alive because it becomes instantiated in the context of a different, unique human individual. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p345]
- Mimesis is founded on empathy and grounded in the body; it is a marker of empathy, with more empathic people mimicking facial expressions more than others. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p346]
- Babies as little as forty-five minutes old can imitate facial gestures, showing the process is fundamental and hard-wired. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p347]
- Mirror neurones mean that imagining something, watching someone else do something, and doing it ourselves share important neural foundations. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p349]

## Inferences

- Inference: The gene for imitation would gain selective advantage when crucial behaviours are partially imitable (neither wholly imitable nor wholly inimitable), explaining the rapid expansion of the human brain in early hominids.
- Inference: From the 'selfish gene' could arise a skill (imitation) that enables evolution to occur not only more rapidly but in a direction of our own choosing—through empathy and co-operation.
