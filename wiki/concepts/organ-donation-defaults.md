# Organ Donation Defaults

## Summary

Default options dramatically affect organ donation rates: countries where the default is consent (opt-out) have near-100% donation rates (Austria), while countries requiring active opt-in have rates as low as 4% (Denmark). This is explained by System 1's preference for defaults as normal, and System 2's reluctance to engage with forms requiring decisions.

## Key facts

- Austria has near-100% organ donation rates while Germany has only 12%, despite similar cultures and neighboring geography—due entirely to whether the default is consent (Austria) or refusal (Germany). [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p481]
- Sweden has an 86% donation rate while Denmark has only 4%, demonstrating that the same cultural background produces radically different outcomes based solely on the default option. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p481]
- The organ donation effect is best explained by System 2 laziness: people check a box if they have already decided what to do, but if they are not prepared to face the question, deciding requires effort they avoid. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p481]
- If the goal is to increase the supply of donated organs, the choice of default is not neutral: defaults producing nearly 100% donations versus 4% represent a policy decision with massive real-world consequences. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p482]

## Inferences

- Inference: The rational-agent model would predict that which default is chosen should not affect preferences on important matters—this finding is a decisive refutation of that prediction and demonstrates that choice architecture is inherently consequential.
