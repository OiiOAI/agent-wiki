# Regression to the Mean

## Summary

A statistical phenomenon discovered by Francis Galton where extreme values tend to be followed by less extreme values. Tall fathers have sons shorter than themselves on average, and short fathers have sons taller than themselves. This does not require a causal explanation—it follows from the mathematics of correlation and the fact that extreme measurements include larger error components that will not reoccur.

## Key facts

- Galton discovered regression to the mean in 1877 while studying inheritance of stature, noting that sons of very tall or very short fathers tend toward average height. [raw/books/strategy/The_Book_of_Why.epub#L470-L490]
- The slope of the regression line cannot exceed 1, otherwise the distribution would become wider over generations, eventually producing extreme heights not observed in nature. [raw/books/strategy/The_Book_of_Why.epub#L500-L515]
- Regression to the mean requires no causal explanation; it follows from the mathematics of correlation and will occur even when there is no causal relationship between variables. [raw/books/strategy/The_Book_of_Why.epub#L485-L500]
- The sophomore slump in baseball (Rookie of the Year players declining) is often wrongly attributed to overconfidence when regression to the mean alone can explain it. [raw/books/strategy/The_Book_of_Why.epub#L485-L500]
- Kahneman observed Israeli flight instructors praising good maneuvers and noting worse subsequent performance, then punishing poor ones and noting improvement—mistakenly attributing regression to the effectiveness of their feedback. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p227]
- Galton discovered regression toward mediocrity in 1886, showing that tall parents' children are taller than average but shorter than the parents, because the correlation between parent and child height is imperfect. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p232]
- Whenever correlation between two measures is imperfect (less than 1.0), regression to the mean necessarily occurs. The lower the correlation, the greater the regression. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p234]
- The 'Sports Illustrated curse'—the belief that appearing on the cover causes subsequent poor performance—is better explained by regression: athletes on the cover performed above average, which partly reflects luck, and luck does not persist. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p231]
- Depressed children given an energy drink who improved after three months did so due to regression toward the mean, not the drink—demonstrating why control groups are necessary to distinguish treatment effects from regression. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p237]

## Inferences

- Inference: Galton's original causal explanation using chutes in his quincunx was mistaken because luck (unlike genes) does not transmit across generations—the correct explanation awaited Mendelian genetics and Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium.
- Inference: Regression to the mean was discovered two centuries after gravity and calculus, suggesting that the human mind has a strong bias toward causal explanations and finds purely statistical phenomena deeply counterintuitive.
