# Shared Intentionality

## Summary

Michael Tomasello's concept describing the uniquely human ability to share mental representations of tasks pursued together. Unlike chimpanzees who cannot coordinate actions toward shared goals (Tomasello states 'it is inconceivable that you would ever see two chimpanzees carrying a log together'), humans developed the ability to form joint intentions and shared attention. This capacity enabled collaboration, division of labor, and the development of shared norms—the beginning of moral matrices. Shared intentionality may have been the 'Rubicon crossing' that made cumulative culture and ultrasociality possible.

## Key facts

- Tomasello's research showed chimpanzees solve physical problems as well as two-year-old humans, but fail at social collaboration tasks requiring understanding of shared intentions. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p226]
- When early humans began sharing intentions, their ability to hunt, gather, raise children, and raid neighbors increased exponentially. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p227]
- Language became possible only after ancestors developed shared intentionality, as words require shared conventions among people with joint representations. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p228]
- The ability to share intentions likely scaled up from groups of two or three to groups of hundreds or thousands through cultural evolution. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p228]
- Tomasello's research shows humans develop shared intentionality in early childhood, allowing collaborative planning and coordinated action toward joint goals, which chimps cannot do. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p396]
- Humans can recruit collaborators to help achieve goals requiring teamwork, but chimpanzees, while able to collaborate when necessary, do not appear to share intentions or truly coordinate with collaborators. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p396]
- Haidt suggests Homo heidelbergensis may have been the first species to develop shared intentionality, based on Tomasello's speculation when asked. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p398]
- Shared intentionality may have emerged as a mutualism: early cooperation for shared goals created selection for the cognitive and emotional mechanisms enabling 'we' experiences. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p396]

## Inferences

- Inference: Shared intentionality may explain why humans alone developed cumulative culture, complex moral systems, and large-scale cooperation—capabilities that required not just individual intelligence but the ability to coordinate thinking with others.
- Inference: Shared intentionality may be the key cognitive adaptation that enabled the cultural evolution and cumulative culture unique to humans.
