# Classical View of Emotion

## Summary

The classical view of emotion holds that emotions are built-in from birth, universal across cultures, and have distinct physical fingerprints in the face, body, and brain. This view, traceable to Plato, Aristotle, Descartes, and Darwin, proposes that emotions are like reflexes triggered by events in the world, causing characteristic facial expressions, bodily changes, and activity in specific brain regions such as the amygdala for fear. The book presents extensive evidence that this view cannot be true, as research has failed to find consistent fingerprints for any single emotion.

## Key facts

- The classical view proposes that emotions have distinct, recognizable 'fingerprints' in the brain, body, and face that uniquely identify each emotion. [raw/books/psychology/How Emotions Are Made_ The Secret Life of - Lisa Feldman Barrett.pdf#p8]
- According to the classical view, emotions are artifacts of evolution, universal across all cultures, and shared with hominin ancestors. [raw/books/psychology/How Emotions Are Made_ The Secret Life of - Lisa Feldman Barrett.pdf#p8]
- Hundreds of experiments support the classical view, but hundreds more cast that evidence into doubt. [raw/books/psychology/How Emotions Are Made_ The Secret Life of - Lisa Feldman Barrett.pdf#p10]
- The classical view considers particular brain areas to have dedicated psychological functions ('one to one'), but this has been contradicted by research showing core systems serve multiple purposes. [raw/books/psychology/How Emotions Are Made_ The Secret Life of - Lisa Feldman Barrett.pdf#p32]
