# Gambler's Fallacy

## Summary

The Gambler's Fallacy is the belief that if something happens more frequently than normal during some period, it will happen less frequently in the future (or vice versa), even when events are independent and have no memory. The classic example is expecting red on a roulette wheel after a long streak of black.

## Key facts

- In Monte Carlo in 1913, the ball landed on black twenty times in a row at a roulette table; players bet millions on red believing it 'had to change,' losing everything until the 27th spin when red finally appeared. [raw/books/strategy/The Art of Thinking Clearly _ Better thinking, Better - Rolf Dobelli; Nicky Griffin.pdf#p74]
- With independent events like coin flips or roulette, there is no balancing force at work; a ball cannot remember how many times it has landed on black. [raw/books/strategy/The Art of Thinking Clearly _ Better thinking, Better - Rolf Dobelli; Nicky Griffin.pdf#p75]
- 'What goes around, comes around' simply does not exist for independent events; pure independent events only exist at casinos, in lotteries and in theory. [raw/books/strategy/The Art of Thinking Clearly _ Better thinking, Better - Rolf Dobelli; Nicky Griffin.pdf#p75]
