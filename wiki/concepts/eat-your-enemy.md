# Eat Your Enemy

## Summary

"Eat Your Enemy" is an innovation method used by Special Operators that involves consuming the best aspects of an adversary's thinking — their plans, methods, and worldview — and making them part of one's own behavior. The method requires exiting "computer-think" to recognize that enemies are individuals with unique exceptional characteristics, not opposites. Examples include Japanese industry after WWII (combining American innovation with Japanese quality culture) and the Lord Chamberlain's Men under Shakespeare (combining Marlowe's charismatic antiheroes with Shakespeare's ensemble casts).

## Key facts

- Eating your enemy is described as an ancient war ritual where Celtic ancestors believed that by consuming their enemy's body they absorbed his spirit, gaining his power. [raw/books/neuroscience/Primal Intelligence_ You Are Smarter Than - Angus Fletcher.pdf#p111]
- The method requires putting your adversary's exceptional character in tension with your own organization's exceptional feature, then holding those exceptions close together. [raw/books/neuroscience/Primal Intelligence_ You Are Smarter Than - Angus Fletcher.pdf#p112]
- Japanese industry identified America's exceptional feature as democratic individualism that nurtured innovation and its own as a neo-Bushido culture of rectitude and honor, combining them to produce innovative but reliable vehicles. [raw/books/neuroscience/Primal Intelligence_ You Are Smarter Than - Angus Fletcher.pdf#p112]
- The Lord Chamberlain's Men combined Marlowe's larger-than-life antiheroes with their own sprawling troupes of quirky characters to produce Shakespeare's Richard III. [raw/books/neuroscience/Primal Intelligence_ You Are Smarter Than - Angus Fletcher.pdf#p113]
- Clausewitz's students at the Prussian War Academy "ate" each other's plans by splitting into teams, trading plans, and identifying what was exceptional about the other's approach. [raw/books/neuroscience/Primal Intelligence_ You Are Smarter Than - Angus Fletcher.pdf#p117]
