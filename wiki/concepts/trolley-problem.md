# The Trolley Problem

## Summary

The trolley problem is a classic philosophical thought experiment about whether the ends (saving five lives) justify the means (killing one). Neuroscientist Joshua Greene used brain imaging to show that pulling a lever (utilitarian, ~70% approve) and pushing someone (personal force, ~30% approve) engage different neural circuits—dlPFC for detached reasoning versus vmPFC/amygdala for intuitive aversion.

## Key facts

- Pulling a lever to divert a trolley (killing one to save five) activates dlPFC and ~70% of people approve—the utilitarian response. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p503]
- Pushing someone in front of a trolley (same outcome) activates vmPFC and amygdala; only ~30% approve—the intuitive, personal force aversion. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p503]
- People are equally willing to divert a trolley if they must push someone as a side effect of lunging for a switch (unintentional) versus if they push someone directly—this is about intentionality and locality, not physical contact. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p503]
- Moral intuitions discount heavily over space and time—the same killing feels more wrong when immediate and personal than when distant and indirect. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p504]
- The trolley problem was invented by British philosopher Philippa Foot in 1967. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p1179]
- People with vmPFC damage are strongly and equally willing to pull the lever or push the person, showing reduced emotional response to utilitarian dilemmas. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p1180]
- Benzodiazepines like Valium make people more willing to push someone in trolley scenarios by calming the vmPFC and amygdala. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p1180]

## Inferences

- Inference: The trolley studies demonstrate that 'rational' moral decision-making is highly context-dependent—minor changes in framing (distance, intentionality) produce radically different decisions via different brain circuits.
