# Cochlear implant

## Summary

The cochlear implant is the most successful neural prosthesis, having helped hundreds of thousands of people with severe hearing loss. It converts sound to electrical signals that directly stimulate the auditory nerve through electrodes in the cochlea, bypassing damaged hair cells.

## Key facts

- The cochlear implant consists of an external microphone, processor worn on the belt, and internal processor with up to 22 electrodes corresponding to different audio frequencies. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p343]
- The process of converting sound to electrical signal and stimulating the auditory nerve takes four milliseconds. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p343]
- A child born deaf with implants placed at 18-24 months can learn to speak normally and have functional hearing. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p337]
- The cochlear implant represents the first successful neuroprosthetic in a human—a merging of silicon with carbon forming a cybernetic organism. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p345]
- Michael Chorost wrote that his cochlear implant made him more human, allowing greater social participation. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p345]

## Inferences

- Inference: The cochlear implant demonstrates that a mechanical device can successfully replace a brain function, raising questions about personal identity and the boundaries of human enhancement.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Whether cochlear implants represent therapy or enhancement, and what future superhuman hearing capabilities might mean ethically, remain open questions.
