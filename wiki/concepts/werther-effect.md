# Werther Effect

## Summary

The Werther effect refers to the phenomenon where highly publicized suicide stories trigger imitative suicides, particularly in geographical areas where the story has been widely publicized. Named after Goethe's novel 'The Sorrows of Young Werther' which sparked a wave of emulative suicides across Europe in the 18th century, this effect demonstrates the powerful influence of social proof on self-destructive behavior.

## Key facts

- David Phillips found that within two months after every front-page suicide story, an average of fifty-eight more people than usual killed themselves, occurring principally in areas where the first suicide was highly publicized. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p136]
- Phillips found that commercial airliner crashes averaged more than three times as many deaths when they occurred one week after a front-page suicide story compared to one week before. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p137]
- Analysis showed that single-victim suicides led to single-victim crashes, while multiple-victim suicide incidents led to multiple-victim crashes, suggesting covert suicide attempts disguised as accidents. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p135]
- Phillips found that when a newspaper detailed a young person's suicide, young drivers then died in single-car crashes; when an older person's suicide was news, older drivers died in such crashes. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p138]
