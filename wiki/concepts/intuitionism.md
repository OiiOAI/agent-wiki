# Intuitionism

## Summary

Rawls understands intuitionism broadly as the doctrine that there is an irreducible family of first principles which have to be weighed against one another by asking which balance, in our considered judgment, is the most just. Intuitionist theories have two features: they consist of a plurality of first principles which may conflict, and they include no explicit method or priority rules for weighing these principles. The intuitionist maintains that at a certain level of generality there exist no higher-order constructive criteria for determining the proper emphasis for competing principles of justice. Rawls argues that while some reliance on intuition is unavoidable, the aim should be to reduce the direct appeal to considered judgments.

## Key facts

- Intuitionism holds that there is an irreducible plurality of first principles which have to be weighed against one another by asking which balance is the most just. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p41]
- Intuitionist theories consist of a plurality of first principles which may conflict to give contrary directives in particular cases. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p41]
- Intuitionism includes no explicit method or priority rules for weighing principles: we are simply to strike a balance by intuition. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p41]
- Common sense intuitionism takes the form of groups of rather specific precepts, each group applying to a particular problem of justice. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p42]
- The aggregative-distributive dichotomy is intuitionist because no priority rule is provided for determining how the principle of utility and the principle of equality are to be balanced. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p43]
- The intuitionist does not deny that we can describe how we balance competing principles, but contends that there exists no expressible ethical conception which underlies these weights. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p45]
