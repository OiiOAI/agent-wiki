# Metacognition in animals

## Summary

Metacognition—awareness of one's own mental operations—has been investigated in animals through uncertainty-response paradigms. Studies with rhesus monkeys, dolphins, and rats demonstrate that animals can opt out of difficult discrimination tasks when uncertain, suggesting they can assess whether they will pass a test on a trial-by-trial basis.

## Key facts

- Rhesus monkeys and humans in a visual density discrimination task could choose an 'uncertain' response, which led to a guaranteed-win trial. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p326]
- A bottlenose dolphin in an auditory discrimination test used a third paddle when uncertain, swimming more slowly and wavering between paddles. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p326]
- Rats hearing short or long sounds could opt out of the test for a small reward, and the more difficult the discrimination, the more frequently they opted out. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p327]
- Apes (orangutans, gorillas, chimps, bonobos) and 2.5-year-old children sought additional information when given incomplete information about which tube contained a treat. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p327]
- The debate continues whether animals use hardwired rules or flexible rules based on knowledge accumulation. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p327]

## Inferences

- Inference: Philosopher Derek Browne suggests that it may not be until postexperimental questioning that human subjects apply psychological concepts to their own performances, raising questions about whether the 'uncertain' response in animals truly reflects metacognition.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The interpretation of uncertainty responses remains controversial—emotional responses to stimuli (approach-avoidance) may power choices rather than higher cognitive metacognitive processes.
