# Grass-is-Always-Greener Syndrome

## Summary

The grass-is-always-greener syndrome describes the human tendency to desire what we do not have, imagining that other circumstances or objects are better than our own. This psychological pattern has deep evolutionary roots and explains much of human covetousness, restlessness, and tendency toward disappointment.

## Key facts

- The earliest recorded example appears in the Old Testament story of the Exodus, where the Hebrews, once suffering in the desert, became nostalgic for Egypt despite their slavery. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p176]
- Three qualities of the human brain explain this syndrome: induction (contrasts intensify desire for the opposite), complacency bias (we are wired for alertness rather than contentment), and the similarity of imagination to reality in brain activity. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p178]
- fMRI experiments show that imagining something produces similar brain activity to actually experiencing it. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p178]
- The syndrome explains why we idealize childhood as it recedes—because distance allows us to forget the anxieties and focus on pleasures. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p177]
