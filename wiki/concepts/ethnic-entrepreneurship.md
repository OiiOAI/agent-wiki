# Ethnic Entrepreneurship

## Summary

Ethnic entrepreneurship refers to business development within ethnic communities, facilitated by ethnic networks, trust, and informal financial mechanisms. The text examines why some ethnic groups (particularly Asian Americans) have succeeded in creating robust business classes while others have not.

## Key facts

- The immigrant self-employment rate in the United States is 7.2 percent, compared to 7.0 percent for the native born; immigrants since 1980 have a rate of 8.4 percent. [raw/books/sociology/Trust - Francis Fukuyama.pdf#p445]
- Light and Bonacich documented Korean immigrant entrepreneurship in Los Angeles from 1965-1982. [raw/books/sociology/Trust - Francis Fukuyama.pdf#p445]
- Shin and Han analyzed Korean immigrant small business in Chicago, examining resource mobilization processes. [raw/books/sociology/Trust - Francis Fukuyama.pdf#p445]
- Chinese and Japanese Americans developed strong ethnic enclaves with internal labor markets. [raw/books/sociology/Trust - Francis Fukuyama.pdf#p446]
- African-American entrepreneurship has a strong but underestimated historical tradition, though it remains weak statistically relative to other groups. [raw/books/sociology/Trust - Francis Fukuyama.pdf#p445]

## Inferences

- Inference: Ethnic entrepreneurship success appears linked to the presence of ethnic social capital, rotating credit associations, and ethnic enclaves that provide capital, labor, and markets

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The relative importance of cultural factors versus structural opportunities in explaining ethnic entrepreneurship success remains debated
