# Civil Disobedience

## Summary

In Rawls' theory, civil disobedience is defined as a public, nonviolent, conscientious yet political act contrary to law but done to appeal to the sense of justice of the majority. It is one of the stabilizing devices of a constitutional system, used to maintain and strengthen just institutions within the limits of fidelity to law.

## Key facts

- Civil disobedience is a public, nonviolent, conscientious yet political act that is contrary to law and done to appeal to the sense of justice of the majority. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p345]
- By engaging in civil disobedience one intends to address the sense of justice of the majority and to serve fair notice that the conditions of free cooperation are being violated. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p345]
- Civil disobedience is one of the stabilizing devices of a constitutional system, though by definition an illegal one. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p346]
- The constitutional theory of civil disobedience rests solely upon a conception of justice; religious or pacifist conceptions are not essential. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p347]
- Civil disobedience is a political and not a religious act, relying upon common sense principles of justice rather than affirmations of religious faith. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p347]
- Justifiable civil disobedience is normally a reasonable and effective form of dissent only in a society regulated to some considerable degree by a sense of justice. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p349]
- An overlapping consensus on political judgments suffices for civil disobedience to be a reasonable form of political dissent; strict consensus is not necessary. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p349]
- Each citizen must decide for himself whether circumstances justify civil disobedience and is always accountable for his deeds. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p350]

## Inferences

- Inference: Rawls distinguishes civil disobedience from mere protest because it appeals to shared principles of justice rather than self-interest or private convictions.
- Inference: The stability provided by civil disobedience comes from its ability to correct injustices without breaking the bonds of civic community.
