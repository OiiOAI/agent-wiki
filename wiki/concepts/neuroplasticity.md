# Neuroplasticity

## Summary

Neuroplasticity is the brain's ability to change throughout life, including generating new neurons (neurogenesis). This discovery in the mid-1990s revolutionized understanding of brain health, showing that cognitive decline is not inevitable and that the brain can adapt, learn, and heal at any age.

## Key facts

- Neuroplasticity—the brain's ability to change up until death—was discovered in the mid-1990s, ending the era of 'neurological nihilism'. [raw/books/health/Genius Foods - Max Lugavere.pdf#p26]
- The creation of new brain cells (neurogenesis) was thought to occur only during development until the mid-1990s discovery that it continues throughout adult life. [raw/books/health/Genius Foods - Max Lugavere.pdf#p26]
- Inflammation can strongly negate neuroplasticity and even produce feelings of depression. [raw/books/health/Genius Foods - Max Lugavere.pdf#p37]
- The brain is described as flexible or 'plastic' in neuroscientist parlance, more Play-Doh than porcelain, adaptable and moldable by input. [raw/books/health/Spark _ The Revolutionary New Science of Exercise and the - John J_ Ratey; Eric Hagerman.pdf#p33]
- The concept of plasticity is fundamental to understanding how the brain works and how exercise optimizes brain function by fostering that quality. [raw/books/health/Spark _ The Revolutionary New Science of Exercise and the - John J_ Ratey; Eric Hagerman.pdf#p33]
- Donald Hebb described the phenomenon as 'use-dependent plasticity,' the theory that synapses rearrange themselves under stimulation of learning. [raw/books/health/Spark _ The Revolutionary New Science of Exercise and the - John J_ Ratey; Eric Hagerman.pdf#p41]
- William Greenough used electron microscopy to show that environmental enrichment made neurons sprout new dendrites with thicker myelin sheaths that fire signals more efficiently. [raw/books/health/Spark _ The Revolutionary New Science of Exercise and the - John J_ Ratey; Eric Hagerman.pdf#p43]
- Neurogenesis (growth of new neurons) was proven in a seminal 1998 paper using cancer patient brain studies. [raw/books/health/Spark _ The Revolutionary New Science of Exercise and the - John J_ Ratey; Eric Hagerman.pdf#p43]
- The author states: 'The more you use it, the stronger and more flexible it becomes,' referring to the brain. [raw/books/health/Spark _ The Revolutionary New Science of Exercise and the - John J_ Ratey; Eric Hagerman.pdf#p33]
- Neuroplasticity is the brain's fundamental property allowing it to change its structure with each different activity performed. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p10]
- The term combines 'neuro' (nerve cells in the brain and nervous system) with 'plastic' (changeable, malleable, modifiable). [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p10]
- Early researchers faced ridicule for using the term and were accused of promoting a fanciful notion. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p10]
- Neuroplasticity allows children to not always be stuck with the mental abilities they are born with. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p10]
- The damaged brain can often reorganize itself so that when one part fails, another can substitute. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p10]
- If brain cells die, they can at times be replaced through neuroplastic processes. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p10]
- Thinking, learning, and acting can turn genes on or off, shaping brain anatomy and behavior through neuroplasticity. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p10]
- Neuroplasticity has both positive effects (flexibility, healing) and negative effects (rigid behaviors, stubborn habits), termed 'the plastic paradox.'. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p11]
- Bach-y-Rita argued that 'a large body of evidence indicates that the brain demonstrates both motor and sensory plasticity.'. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p29]
- Bach-y-Rita discovered that the 'visual' part of a cat's brain was processing touch and sound as well, leading him to conceive of much of the brain as 'polysensory.'. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p28]
- The sensory cortex is plastic and adaptable, not rigidly specialized for one sense only. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p36]
- Neural pathways can be 'unmasked' or exposed, and with use, strengthened—one of the main ways the plastic brain reorganizes itself. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p20]
- The brain is a far more open system than previously imagined, able to survive in a changing world by changing itself. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p36]
- Mark Rosenzweig was one of the first scientists to demonstrate neuroplasticity by showing that activity could produce changes in the structure of the brain. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p44]
- Rats raised in stimulating environments had heavier brains with more neurotransmitters and better blood supply than rats from less stimulating environments. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p44]
- Animals raised in enriched environments learn better than genetically identical animals reared in impoverished environments. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p52]
- Mental training or life in enriched environments increases brain weight by 5 percent in the cerebral cortex and up to 9 percent in directly stimulated areas. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p52]
- Trained neurons develop 25 percent more branches and increase their size, connections, and blood supply. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p52]
- Postmortem examinations show that education increases the number of branches among neurons, driving neurons apart and increasing brain volume and thickness. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p52]
- The idea that the brain is like a muscle that grows with exercise is not just a metaphor. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p52]
- Brain changes from training and enrichment occur in all types of animals tested to date. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p52]
- Barbara Arrowsmith Young demonstrated that children with learning disabilities can correct underlying problems rather than just compensate for them. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p53]
- Hubel and Wiesel won the Nobel Prize for discovering plasticity in infancy, showing kittens' visual cortex maps failed to develop normally when deprived of visual input during a critical period. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p59]
- When one eye was sewn shut in kittens, the brain map area for that eye was taken over by the open eye, showing the brain won't waste 'cortical real estate.'. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p60]
- Merzenich's 1968 experiment showed that when a peripheral nerve was cut and regenerated with crossed axons, the brain map 'unshuffled' the signals, normalizing its structure. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p63]
- Language development has a critical period beginning in infancy and ending between age 8 and puberty; after this, ability to learn a second language without an accent is limited. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p60]
- In adults, the more one uses their native language, the more it dominates linguistic map space, making it hard to learn new languages. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p69]
- Paying close attention is essential for long-term plastic change; when animals performed tasks automatically without attention, map changes did not last. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p78]
- Pascual-Leone showed that when blocking TMS was applied to the visual cortex of Braille readers, they could not read Braille or feel with their Braille-reading finger, indicating the visual cortex had been recruited for touch processing. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p207]
- The better a person could read Braille, the more the visual cortex was involved, demonstrating experience-dependent plastic reorganization. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p207]
- Blindfold experiments showed it could take as few as two days for the visual cortex to begin processing tactile and auditory signals when sight was completely blocked. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p217]
- Pascual-Leone proposed that the brain is 'plastic, not elastic' — permanently altered by every encounter rather than returning to a previous state. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p215]
- The operator theory proposes that individual brain parts are not committed to processing particular senses but compete to process abstract information like spatial relationships, movement, and shapes. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p218]
- Brain injury such as stroke triggers neurogenesis, but whatever compensatory neurogenesis occurs is limited, since many neurological insults leave lasting damage. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p973]
- New neurons sometimes make things worse after brain injury, migrating where they shouldn't and making circuits seizure-prone—described as neuronal pathological altruism. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p973]
- The number of new neurons integrated into circuits reflects: number formed from stem cells, percentage differentiating into neurons versus glia, and rate at which new neurons survive and form functional synapses. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p974]
- Voluntary exercise with glucocorticoid secretion enhances neurogenesis, while stress-induced glucocorticoid secretion inhibits it—the contrast between good and bad stress. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p974]

## Inferences

- Inference: Bach-y-Rita's discovery that the visual cortex could process tactile input suggests that brain areas are plastic processors rather than rigidly specialized modules.
- Inference: brain reorganization can occur within days through unmasking of existing connections, this suggests potential for rapid therapeutic interventions using constraint-based therapies.
