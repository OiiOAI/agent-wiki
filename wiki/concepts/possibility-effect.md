# Possibility Effect

## Summary

The possibility effect is the tendency to overweight very small probabilities, giving them far more decision influence than their objective magnitude warrants. It explains the attractiveness of lottery tickets (where a tiny chance of a huge prize seems worth playing despite unfavorable expected value), excessive fear of rare but dramatic events like terrorism, and willingness to pay much more than actuarial value for insurance that eliminates a small probability of catastrophe.

## Key facts

- A 2% probability of winning receives a decision weight of approximately 8.1—more than four times its objective probability—illustrating the magnitude of the possibility effect. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p407]
- Lottery players pay far more than the expected value of the ticket because the mere existence of a chance creates a psychological possibility that dominates the calculation, and the vivid fantasy of winning adds hedonic value beyond the probability itself. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p410]
- The terror of suicide bombings in Israel (23 bombings killing 236 people over 2.75 years in a city of 1.3 million daily bus riders) led to systematic avoidance behavior disproportionate to the statistical risk, because the vivid imagery of the threat was highly available and the possibility of being in a bombing was emotionally overweighted. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p415]
- The possibility effect operates in tandem with the availability heuristic: when a rare event is highly publicized or emotionally vivid, it becomes highly available in memory, causing both its probability and its decision weight to be overestimated. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p416]

## Inferences

- Inference: The possibility effect explains why risk communication often fails: any risk communication strategy that makes a rare risk more concrete or vivid (e.g., '1 in 1,000 children' rather than '0.1%') will increase perceived risk even if it conveys the same statistical information.
