# Gate Control Theory of Pain

## Summary

The gate control theory of pain, developed by Ronald Melzack and Patrick Wall in 1965, revolutionized understanding of pain by proposing that the brain actively controls pain signals through a system of 'gates' in the spinal cord. Rather than passively receiving pain, the brain can open or close these gates based on context, mood, expectations, and other psychological factors.

## Key facts

- In 1965, Ronald Melzack and Patrick Wall wrote what the book calls 'the most important article in the history of pain,' introducing the gate control theory. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p196]
- The gate control theory proposed that pain messages travel through several 'gates,' starting in the spinal cord, before reaching the brain, and these gates open or close based on brain signals. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p197]
- The brain can close a gate and block pain signals by releasing endorphins, the body's natural narcotics for pain relief. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p197]
- During World War II, 70 percent of seriously wounded U.S. troops reported they were not in pain and did not want painkillers, supporting the gate theory. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p197]
- Placebo effects work because the brain turns down its own pain-responsive regions, demonstrating top-down pain control. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p197]
- Wall co-invented transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS), which uses electric current to stimulate neurons that inhibit pain and help close the gate. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p198]
- The gate theory made Western scientists less skeptical of acupuncture, which may reduce pain by stimulating neurons that inhibit pain and close gates. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p198]
- Pain maps in the spinal cord can change following injury, and chronic injury can make pain system cells fire more easily, a plastic alteration making people hypersensitive to pain. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p197]
