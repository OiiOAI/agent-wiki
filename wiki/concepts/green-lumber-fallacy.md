# The Green Lumber Fallacy

## Summary

The Green Lumber Fallacy is the error of mistaking the source of necessary knowledge for another less visible source. Named after Joe Siegel, a highly successful trader who traded 'green lumber' while believing it meant lumber painted green (rather than freshly cut lumber). Taleb uses this to illustrate that people who succeed in practice often possess knowledge that differs fundamentally from what outsiders assume is important.

## Key facts

- Joe Siegel, one of the most successful traders in green lumber, thought green lumber was lumber painted green rather than freshly cut lumber with high moisture content. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p264]
- The successful expert on lumber was ignorant of central matters like the designation 'green,' yet knew things about lumber that nonexperts think are unimportant. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p264]
- People who do things in the field are not subjected to a set exam and are selected in the most non-narrative manner—nice arguments don't make much difference. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p264]
- Evolution does not rely on narratives; humans do. Evolution does not need a word for the color blue. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p264]
- The green lumber fallacy is mistaking the source of important or even necessary knowledge—the greenness of lumber—for another, less visible and less tractable one. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p530]
- It illustrates how theoreticians impute wrong weights to what one should know in a certain business or how many things we call 'relevant knowledge' aren't so much so. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p530]
- Easterly reports that assuming prosperity can be planned for other societies is like thinking racehorses can be put in charge of building racetracks—a green lumber type error. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p584]

## Inferences

- Inference: Taleb's own experience with foreign exchange traders—successful operators who lacked formal education and couldn't place Switzerland on a map—demonstrates that practical success relies on knowledge different from academic knowledge.
- Inference: The fallacy shows why theoretical knowledge about a subject may be unrelated to the tacit knowledge required to succeed in it.
