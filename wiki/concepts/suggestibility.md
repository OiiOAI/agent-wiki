# Suggestibility

## Summary

Suggestibility refers to the creation of false memories through leading questions, social pressure, or suggestive interviewing techniques. Schacter examines its role in eyewitness testimony errors, the recovered memory controversy, and controversial daycare abuse cases. He discusses the McMartin preschool case and Fells Acres daycare case as examples where suggestive techniques may have produced false accusations.

## Key facts

- Elizabeth Loftus demonstrated that leading questions can create false memories, such as altering whether witnesses report a 'stop sign' versus a 'yield sign'. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p385]
- Children are particularly susceptible to suggestion, especially when questions are repeated or come from authority figures. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p385]
- The Fells Acres daycare case illustrated how suggestive interviewing techniques could lead children to report events that did not occur. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p385]
- False confessions can occur when individuals internalize suggestions that they committed crimes they did not actually commit. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p385]

## Inferences

- Inference: Suggestibility may reflect an adaptive social learning mechanism that prioritizes believing authoritative sources, which can be exploited in forensic contexts.
