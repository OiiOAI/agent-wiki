# Split-brain self-recognition

## Summary

Research on split-brain patients reveals that the left and right hemispheres process self-recognition differently. Studies with patient J.W. using morphed photographs demonstrated a double dissociation: the left hemisphere quickly detects partial self-images (linear response to percentage of self), while the right hemisphere requires nearly complete self-images (>80% self) before recognition occurs.

## Key facts

- Patient J.W. was shown morphed photographs ranging from 0% (M.G.) to 100% (J.W.) to test self vs. familiar-other recognition. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p316]
- In the left hemisphere, there was a linear relationship between amount of self in the image and probability of detecting self. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p317]
- The right hemisphere did not recognize the image as self until the image contained more than 80 percent self. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p317]
- The left hemisphere is quick to detect a partial self-image, while the right brain needs an essentially full and complete picture. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p317]
- Face recognition of familiar others is typically reliant on structures in the right cerebral hemisphere. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p316]

## Inferences

- Inference: The left hemisphere's 'interpreter' may be taking available information and making hypothesis-based judgments about self, while the right brain maximizes information without forming hypotheses.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The exact neural mechanisms underlying this double dissociation remain to be fully elucidated.
