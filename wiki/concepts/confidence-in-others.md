# Confidence in Others

## Summary

Confidence in others is distinguished from trust. Trust involves conditions and security (like bank loans requiring collateral), while confidence means believing in others without any set conditions whatsoever, even without sufficient objective grounds. This unconditional confidence is presented as essential to building deep interpersonal relationships. The philosopher argues that if one plants 'doubt' at the foundation of interpersonal relations, the other person will detect it instantly and no positive relationship can arise.

## Key facts

- Trust refers to something that comes with set conditions, such as bank loans requiring security. Confidence means believing unconditionally without concerning oneself with security. [raw/books/psychology/The courage to be disliked_ the Japanese phenomenon that - Ichiro Kishimi, and Fumitake Koga.pdf#p235]
- The opposite of confidence is doubt. If one lives doubting friends and family, the other person will detect the doubt in one's eyes and understand that 'this person does not have confidence in me.'. [raw/books/psychology/The courage to be disliked_ the Japanese phenomenon that - Ichiro Kishimi, and Fumitake Koga.pdf#p237]
- Whether to believe or doubt is a choice that should be clear—believing connects to seeing others as comrades. [raw/books/psychology/The courage to be disliked_ the Japanese phenomenon that - Ichiro Kishimi, and Fumitake Koga.pdf#p240]

## Inferences

- Inference: The philosopher connects confidence in others to the separation of tasks: one decides whether to have confidence, but the other person's response (taking advantage or not) is their task.
