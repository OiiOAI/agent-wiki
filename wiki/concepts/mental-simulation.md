# Mental Simulation

## Summary

Mental simulation is the cognitive ability to consciously imagine people and objects and transform them through several transitions to picture them in a different state. In decision making, mental simulation allows experts to project how a course of action might unfold, identify potential problems before they occur, and evaluate whether a plan will work without actually executing it.

## Key facts

- Mental simulation is the ability to imagine people and objects consciously and transform them through several transitions to picture them in a different way. [raw/books/strategy/Sources of Power How People Make Decisions by Gary A. Klein .epub.pdf#p73]
- Mental simulations generally move through approximately six transitions driven by around three causal factors. [raw/books/strategy/Sources of Power How People Make Decisions by Gary A. Klein .epub.pdf#p82]
- Mental simulation can be used to explain what happened in the past (building a story to account for events) or to project what will happen in the future (evaluating a course of action). [raw/books/strategy/Sources of Power How People Make Decisions by Gary A. Klein .epub.pdf#p89-93]
- When mental simulation is used to evaluate a course of action, decision makers may adjust the plan to overcome pitfalls, reject it, or carry it out. [raw/books/strategy/Sources of Power How People Make Decisions by Gary A. Klein .epub.pdf#p93]
- A danger of mental simulation is that decision makers can explain away contradictory evidence, called de minimus explanations. [raw/books/strategy/Sources of Power How People Make Decisions by Gary A. Klein .epub.pdf#p95]
- Mental simulation can be self-correcting through a process called snap-back, where accumulated strain from explaining away discrepancies eventually triggers reexamination of the simulation. [raw/books/strategy/Sources of Power How People Make Decisions by Gary A. Klein .epub.pdf#p99-100]
- Experts can chunk several transitions into one unit to overcome working memory limitations. [raw/books/strategy/Sources of Power How People Make Decisions by Gary A. Klein .epub.pdf#p82]
- Mental simulation lets people explain how events have moved from the past into the present and project how the present will move into the future. [raw/books/strategy/Sources of Power How People Make Decisions by Gary A. Klein .epub.pdf#p104]
- Because of memory limitations, people usually construct mental simulations using around three variables and around six transitions. [raw/books/strategy/Sources of Power How People Make Decisions by Gary A. Klein .epub.pdf#p104]
- It takes a fair amount of experience to construct a useful mental simulation. [raw/books/strategy/Sources of Power How People Make Decisions by Gary A. Klein .epub.pdf#p104]
- Mental simulations can be misleading when a person argues away evidence that challenges the interpretation. [raw/books/strategy/Sources of Power How People Make Decisions by Gary A. Klein .epub.pdf#p104]
- Mental simulation serves three functions in nonroutine decision making: explaining cues to diagnose problems, generating expectancies to verify situation awareness, and evaluating courses of action. [raw/books/strategy/Sources of Power How People Make Decisions by Gary A. Klein .epub.pdf#p120]
- The ability to see the past and the future rests on understanding primary causes in a domain and applying these causes to run mental simulations. [raw/books/strategy/Sources of Power How People Make Decisions by Gary A. Klein .epub.pdf#p196]
- Mental simulations are evaluated using criteria similar to good stories: plausibility, consistency, economy, and uniqueness. [raw/books/strategy/Sources of Power How People Make Decisions by Gary A. Klein .epub.pdf#p227]
- True experts can mentally simulate how a different future state from the one they predicted might evolve; pretenders lack this sense of dynamics. [raw/books/strategy/Sources of Power How People Make Decisions by Gary A. Klein .epub.pdf#p196]

## Inferences

- Inference: The limitations of mental simulation (overconfidence, difficulty with complex interacting variables, and de minimus explanations) suggest that training should help people track contrary evidence and know when to seek alternate explanations.
- Inference: The concept of mental simulation overlaps significantly with Tversky and Kahneman's simulation heuristic (1974), which is used for diagnosis and evaluation.
