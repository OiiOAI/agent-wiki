# Developmental Systems Theory

## Summary

Developmental Systems Theory (DST), primarily associated with Susan Oyama, defines evolution not as change in gene frequencies but as change in the distribution and constitution of developmental systems. The fundamental unit of evolution is the life cycle — a developmental process that reconstructs itself from one generation to the next. DST rejects dichotomous accounts structured by nature/nurture, genetic/environmental, and replication/interaction distinctions, instead reconceptualizing these as products and processes rather than opposed causal factors.

## Key facts

- Developmental Systems Theory defines evolution as change in the distribution and constitution of developmental systems, not as change in gene frequencies. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p185]
- A life cycle is a developmental process that reconstructs itself from one generation to the next, involving numerous interdependent causal elements rather than unfolding from a genetic blueprint. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p185]
- DST expands inheritance beyond genes: not only genes but many other developmental resources (cytoplasmic components, symbionts, social traditions) are inherited as part of the developmental system. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p187]
- The parity thesis holds that the only coherent definition of information in developmental biology is one equally applicable to genetic and nongenetic causal factors. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p187]
- Oyama reconceptualizes 'nature' and 'nurture' as product and process: natures are developing phenotypes, and nurture is the developmental interactions by which they come into being. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p188]
- Developmental systems theory rejects accounts of evolution and development structured by the innate-versus-acquired, nature-versus-nurture, and nature-versus-culture dichotomies. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p391]
- The developmental system of an organism is the matrix of resources necessary for its development, including genes, cytoplasmic components, symbiont organisms, social structures, and cultural practices. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p391]
- Genes are not distinctly informational causes of development different in kind from other developmental factors; rather, development reconstructs itself from generation to generation through myriad interdependent causal pathways. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p391]
- Evolution is change in the distribution and constitution of developmental (organism-environment) systems, not simply change in gene frequencies. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p391]
- Phenotypic traits are as much acquired as inherited because they must be developmentally constructed anew in each generation. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p391]

## Inferences

- Inference: DST expresses dissatisfaction with neo-Darwinism's gene-centered focus, neglect of active developing organisms, and notion of adaptation as solving preexisting environmental problems.
- Inference: There is natural kinship between DST and the theory of autopoiesis: both provide tools to describe biological organization and to give nondichotomous accounts of organism-environment coupling.
