# Language Organ Theory

## Summary

Language organ theory, most prominently associated with Noam Chomsky, posits that humans possess an innate, species-specific neural mechanism dedicated to language acquisition. This 'language organ' supposedly contains Universal Grammar—innate knowledge of the structural principles common to all human languages. Deacon critically examines this theory, arguing that while children's grammatical abilities are remarkable, the focus on innate grammar misses the more fundamental problem of symbolic reference that underlies language.

## Key facts

- Chomsky argued that children's ability to acquire grammar despite insufficient input and lack of explicit instruction can only be explained by innate grammatical knowledge already present in the brain. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p35]
- Deacon argues that language organ theory doesn't explain why nonhuman species, even with similar brain structures, cannot acquire any form of symbolic communication. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p35]
- Steven Pinker has argued that an innate language instinct could have evolved through natural selection, making the theory compatible with adaptationism. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p38]

## Inferences

- Inference: Language organ theory addresses grammar acquisition but fails to explain the symbolic threshold problem, making it an incomplete account of language origins. [Based on: pp.37-38]

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Whether innate grammatical knowledge (Universal Grammar) actually exists or whether the appearance of such innateness can be explained by other means of language acquisition.
