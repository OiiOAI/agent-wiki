# Strategic Theory

## Summary

Strategic theory concerns thoughts about making effective strategy and the proper use of force. Unlike 'proper' scientific theory, strategic theory must grapple with interactive opponents who react, learn, and anticipate. It connects diverse dimensions including people, politics, military operations, command, geography, friction, adversary characteristics, and time. The difficulty of strategic theory lies in its evolutionary and paradoxical nature—strategies work until learned from, then require new approaches.

## Key facts

- Strategy provides the conceptual link between action and effect and between instrument and objective; it is an idea—a plan of action to achieve some end. [raw/books/strategy/ScienceStrategyWar_Osinga.pdf#p24]
- Theory serves to codify what we already know, put things in a system, and make sense of otherwise inscrutable empirical findings. [raw/books/strategy/ScienceStrategyWar_Osinga.pdf#p29]
- Strategic theory is evolutionary because novel actors, technologies, and phenomena require new theoretical frameworks. [raw/books/strategy/ScienceStrategyWar_Osinga.pdf#p36]
- Strategic theory is paradoxical because strategies that work once will be learned from and countered, requiring continuous innovation. [raw/books/strategy/ScienceStrategyWar_Osinga.pdf#p37]
- The scope of strategic theory ranges from general theories (Clausewitz, Sun Tzu) to theories explaining specific military domains (Corbett on naval warfare, Mahan on maritime power). [raw/books/strategy/ScienceStrategyWar_Osinga.pdf#p31]

## Inferences

- Inference: Strategic theory development follows formative factors: nature of war, strategic circumstances of countries, personal experience of thinkers, and intellectual climate.
- Inference: The educational function of strategic theory—to provide a way of thinking rather than what to think—is as important as predictive capability.
