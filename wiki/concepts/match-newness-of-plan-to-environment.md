# Match the Newness of Your Plan to the Newness of Your Environment

## Summary

This commonsense decision-making rule states that when the fundamentals change, you must junk your most successful plans and develop new plans with boldness proportional to uncertainty. George Marshall discarded successful generals when situations changed; Thomas Paine advocated embracing "hints" of innovation; George Washington was bold when situations were uncertain and cautious when they were stable.

## Key facts

- Marshall junked the most successful plans by force-retiring hundreds of officers whose expertise "guaranteed victory in the previous fight and ruin in the next one". [raw/books/neuroscience/Primal Intelligence_ You Are Smarter Than - Angus Fletcher.pdf#p142]
- Paine wrote that peculiarity is the sign of the exceptional, advocating concentration of original qualities rather than deleting odd parts of new plans. [raw/books/neuroscience/Primal Intelligence_ You Are Smarter Than - Angus Fletcher.pdf#p144]
- Washington selected the least imaginative plan (a classic siege at Yorktown) when British were trapped, but green-lit an extraordinarily hazardous night attack across the Delaware when the Army was collapsing. [raw/books/neuroscience/Primal Intelligence_ You Are Smarter Than - Angus Fletcher.pdf#p144]
- Armstrong's decision-making was rooted in dual background as test pilot and engineer, respecting SOPs but willing to scrap them at a moment's notice, improvising solutions whose novelty matched the novelty of the situation. [raw/books/neuroscience/Primal Intelligence_ You Are Smarter Than - Angus Fletcher.pdf#p147]
