# Memory Distrust Syndrome

## Summary

Memory distrust syndrome, identified by Gisli Gudjonsson, describes a condition in which individuals in the face of coercive questioning begin to distrust their own memory and eventually disregard it entirely. This can lead people to abandon the distinctiveness heuristic—the expectation that they would remember distinctive details of important events—and come to believe they committed crimes they did not commit.

## Key facts

- Clinical psychologist Gisli Gudjonsson coined the term 'memory distrust syndrome' to describe how suspects can begin to distrust their own memory under coercive questioning. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p163]
- Peter Reilly, who was exonerated two years after confessing to murdering his mother, illustrated memory distrust syndrome—he became convinced he must have committed the crime despite immediately reporting finding the body. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p163]
- To abandon trust in memory for a horrific event, Reilly would have had to abandon the distinctiveness heuristic—expecting to remember distinct details of an experience. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p163]
- Memory distrust syndrome can develop when it is plausible that one might forget even a violent crime, such as when a person was intoxicated or believes they could have repressed a horrendous event. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p163]
- Paul Ingram confessed to bizarre cult activities including satanic rituals and murders under coercive questioning, eventually producing full-blown 'memories' he came to believe he had previously repressed. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p164]
