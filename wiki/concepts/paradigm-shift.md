# Paradigm Shift

## Summary

Thomas Kuhn's concept of paradigm shift describes how scientific progress occurs through discontinuous revolutionary breaks rather than gradual accumulation of knowledge. Kuhn defined a paradigm as a constellation of achievements shared by a scientific community defining legitimate problems and solutions. This concept heavily influenced Boyd's understanding of conceptual change and innovation.

## Key facts

- Kuhn defined a paradigm as 'a constellation of achievements – concepts, values, techniques, etc. – shared by a scientific community and used by that community to define legitimate problems and solutions'. [raw/books/strategy/ScienceStrategyWar_Osinga.pdf#p100]
- Kuhn noted that falsification was a flawed concept because if everyone constantly questioned fundamentals, significant progress was unlikely since principles would not remain unchallenged long enough for esoteric work. [raw/books/strategy/ScienceStrategyWar_Osinga.pdf#p100]
- Paradigm shifts occur in discontinuous, revolutionary breaks – science progresses not via a neat gradual accumulation of knowledge but through crises and revolutions. [raw/books/strategy/ScienceStrategyWar_Osinga.pdf#p101]
- Kuhn argued that rival paradigms are 'incommensurable' – different paradigms have no common standard for comparison and regard different kinds of questions as legitimate or meaningful. [raw/books/strategy/ScienceStrategyWar_Osinga.pdf#p102]
- Boyd directly referred to Kuhn's 1970 edition of The Structure of Scientific Revolutions when discussing how anomalies, ambiguities, uncertainties, or inconsistencies emerge in paradigms. [raw/books/strategy/ScienceStrategyWar_Osinga.pdf#p102]
- Boyd manifested 'Polanyiist/Kuhnian' inspiration in The Conceptual Spiral, stating that novelty is produced by a mental/physical feedback process of analyses and synthesis that permit interaction with the world. [raw/books/strategy/ScienceStrategyWar_Osinga.pdf#p103]

## Inferences

- Inference: Boyd applied Kuhn's paradigm shift concept to military doctrine and strategy, viewing them as systems that can become inadequate and require revolutionary replacement.
