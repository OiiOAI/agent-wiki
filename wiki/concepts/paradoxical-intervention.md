# Paradoxical Intervention

## Summary

Paradoxical intervention encompasses therapeutic techniques that introduce paradox to disrupt self-defeating patterns. This includes symptom prescription (prescribing the symptom), counter-paradox, and directives like 'Go slow!' or prescribing a relapse. The approach recognizes that resistance to change can be turned into a vehicle for change by reframing resistance as a precondition for or aspect of change.

## Key facts

- Symptom prescription—or second-order change through paradox—is the most powerful and elegant form of problem resolution, making the patient actively do the symptom rather than fight it. [raw/books/strategy/Change _ principles of problem formulation and problem - Paul Watzlawick, John H_ Weakland, Richard Fisch; foreword.epub#L112-115]
- The 'Be Spontaneous!' paradox occurs when a person tries to force themselves to perform a spontaneous act (like falling asleep), creating a bind where the harder they try, the more they fail. [raw/books/strategy/Change _ principles of problem formulation and problem - Paul Watzlawick, John H_ Weakland, Richard Fisch; foreword.epub#L63-66]
- The 'Go slow!' intervention is a paradoxical technique for incipient change: instead of praising progress, the therapist raises pessimistic objections to prevent the patient from rushing and potentially failing. [raw/books/strategy/Change _ principles of problem formulation and problem - Paul Watzlawick, John H_ Weakland, Richard Fisch; foreword.epub#L133-135]
- A phobic can be helped by counter-paradox: telling him to walk into the store and faint on purpose, or to stop one yard short of overwhelming anxiety—directing intervention at the attempted solution. [raw/books/strategy/Change _ principles of problem formulation and problem - Paul Watzlawick, John H_ Weakland, Richard Fisch; foreword.epub#L85-87]
- Prescribing a relapse is a paradoxical intervention where the patient is told a relapse is desirable and should be brought about deliberately; either outcome 'proves' control. [raw/books/strategy/Change _ principles of problem formulation and problem - Paul Watzlawick, John H_ Weakland, Richard Fisch; foreword.epub#L134-136]

## Inferences

- Inference: Paradox plays as important a role in problem resolution as it plays in problem formation—solving problems requires the same type of intervention that created them.
- Inference: The probation officer's statement 'You should never fully trust me or tell me everything' makes him trustworthy to the extent that he has declared himself untrustworthy, resolving an otherwise hopeless paradoxical state.
