# The Uncanny

## Summary

The uncanny (das Unheimliche) arose as a cultural phenomenon during the Enlightenment. McGilchrist links it to experiences characteristic of schizophrenia: doubles, automata, waxwork figures, detached body parts, and déjà vu. The uncanny represents phenomena of the right hemisphere appearing in the context of the left hemisphere's rational, mechanistic, certain, humanly-controlled world. Freud's definition—that the uncanny is what should have remained hidden but has been brought to light—reflects the exclusion of right hemisphere promptings from rational discourse.

## Key facts

- The classic loci of the uncanny include doubles, dancing dolls and automata, waxwork figures, alter egos, 'mirror selves', spectral emanations, detached body parts, and the ghastly fantasy of being buried alive. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p483]
- Freud emphasized that the uncanny effect does not proceed automatically from supernatural ideas; children imagining dolls to be alive or ghostly appearances in fairy tales are not uncanny because they exist in a context acknowledged to be removed from everyday reality. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p483]
- The uncanny represents the terrifying possibility, to the rational left-hemisphere mind, that phenomena beyond what we can understand and control may truly exist. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p484]

## Inferences

- Inference: The rise of the uncanny in the Enlightenment reflects the increasing separation between hemispheres as the left hemisphere attempted to achieve objective, scientific detachment independent of personal or intuitive elements.
