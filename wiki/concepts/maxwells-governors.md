# Maxwell's Governors

## Summary

James Clerk Maxwell's 1867 mathematical analysis of steam engine governors—contraptions meant to control speed by compensating for variations. Maxwell showed mathematically that tight control paradoxically causes instability: light control works but close control leads to overreaction and crashes. This demonstrates Taleb's argument that suppressing natural volatility creates hidden fragility.

## Key facts

- Maxwell mathematically modeled governors controlling steam engine speed and showed that tightly controlling speed leads to instability—light control works, close control causes machinery to break. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p137]
- Maxwell's derivation generalizes across domains: fixing prices or eliminating speculators creates pseudo-stability with periods of calm punctuated by large jumps, as players become unused to volatility. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p138]
- When a currency never varies, even a slight move makes people believe the world is ending; injecting confusion stabilizes the system. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p138]

## Inferences

- Inference: Maxwell's analysis provides scientific rigor to what Taleb calls the 'time bomb called stability'—mechanisms that appear to reduce volatility actually concentrate fragility for catastrophic release.
