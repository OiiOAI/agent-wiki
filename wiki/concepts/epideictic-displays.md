# Epideictic Displays

## Summary

Epideictic behavior is V.C. Wynne-Edwards's proposed mechanism for how animals might estimate their own population density to regulate birth rates. He suggested that aggregations like starling murmurations or dancing midge swarms serve as deliberate massing to facilitate population census, allowing individuals to adjust reproductive output based on perceived crowding. Dawkins evaluates this hypothesis through the lens of selfish gene theory.

## Key facts

- Wynne-Edwards coined the term 'epideictic behavior' to describe what he proposed was deliberate massing in crowds to facilitate population estimation, linking sensory perception of population density to reproductive systems through automatic nervous or hormonal mechanisms. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p148]
- Dawkins argues that epideictic displays can be explained in selfish gene terms: females who can estimate winter population density may predict spring food competition and adjust clutch size to their own selfish advantage. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p154]
- If epideictic displays work through individuals estimating population density to adjust clutch size, there would be selective pressure for individuals to exaggerate their numbers (the Beau Geste Effect) to induce rivals to lay fewer eggs, reducing competition for one's own offspring. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p155]
- Dawkins concludes that Wynne-Edwards may have been right about epideictic displays all along, but for the wrong (group selection) reasons—the Lack-type hypothesis in selfish gene terms can account for the same evidence. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p155]

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: There is no direct evidence that any animal aggregations are actually epideictic (performing population estimation)—this is a hypothetical phenomenon that could explain observed behaviors.
