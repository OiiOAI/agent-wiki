# Temporal Processing

## Summary

Temporal processing is the brain's ability to determine how long events last and distinguish the order of rapidly occurring sounds or sensations. Merzenich discovered this is plastic and trainable, and that training in temporal processing can improve general mental abilities including IQ, attention, and even visual processing. Language-impaired children often have neurons that fire too slowly to distinguish similar sounds or determine their order.

## Key facts

- Eighty percent of language-impaired children had neurons that needed about 90 milliseconds to rest between firings, compared to the normal 30 milliseconds, losing large amounts of language information. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p80]
- Language-impaired children had 'muddy in, muddy out' signals—their improper hearing led to weaknesses in vocabulary, comprehension, speech, reading, and writing. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p80]
- Merzenich and Jenkins trained monkeys to distinguish sounds in shorter spans of time; trained neurons fired more quickly, processed sounds in shorter time, and needed less rest between firings. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p78]
- When people were trained to distinguish 75-millisecond vibrations on their skin, they could also detect 75-millisecond sounds, suggesting temporal processing is a general ability that transfers across senses. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p84]
- Fast ForWord improved IQ as measured by the matrix test, a visual-based measurement, showing improvements were not just from reading verbal test questions but from general mental processing improvement. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p84]
