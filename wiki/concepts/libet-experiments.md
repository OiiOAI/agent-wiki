# Libet experiments

## Summary

The Libet experiments, conducted by Benjamin Libet and colleagues in the 1980s, measured brain activity (readiness potential) preceding voluntary movements. Subjects were asked to move their finger 'whenever they felt like it' while watching a clock. Libet found that EEG-detected brain activity began ramping up several hundred milliseconds before subjects reported conscious awareness of the intention to move. This was interpreted as suggesting the brain 'decides' before conscious awareness. However, reinterpretation by Aaron Schurger and colleagues suggests the readiness potential reflects ongoing neural noise that occasionally crosses a threshold, not advance planning.

## Key facts

- In Libet's setup, subjects lifted a finger whenever they felt like it ('on a whim'), while EEG recorded brain activity and subjects noted when they first felt conscious intention. [raw/books/neuroscience/Free Agents _ How Evolution Gave Us Free Will - Kevin J_ Mitchell.pdf#p199]
- Libet found that readiness potential began ramping up several hundred milliseconds before subjects reported conscious awareness of intention to move. [raw/books/neuroscience/Free Agents _ How Evolution Gave Us Free Will - Kevin J_ Mitchell.pdf#p201]
- Aaron Schurger reinterpretation: constant neural noise fluctuates up and down; movement occurs when noise crosses a threshold, not because the brain decided in advance. [raw/books/neuroscience/Free Agents _ How Evolution Gave Us Free Will - Kevin J_ Mitchell.pdf#p204]
- When decisions were consequential (choosing between charities affecting money), no readiness potential was observed - only in arbitrary, inconsequential choices. [raw/books/neuroscience/Free Agents _ How Evolution Gave Us Free Will - Kevin J_ Mitchell.pdf#p205]
- Subjects retained the power to consciously veto the movement urge up to tens of milliseconds before execution. [raw/books/neuroscience/Free Agents _ How Evolution Gave Us Free Will - Kevin J_ Mitchell.pdf#p211]

## Inferences

- Inference: Libet's experiments relate only to arbitrary, inconsequential decisions, not deliberative ones where subjects weigh actual outcomes.
- Inference: The experiments confirm that neural activity is not completely deterministic and that organisms can harness inherent randomness for arbitrary decisions.
