# Full Theory of the Good

## Summary

The full theory of the good takes the principles of justice as already secured and uses these principles in defining moral concepts in which the notion of goodness is involved. It applies to persons, acts, and societies, and is necessary for defining beneficent acts, supererogatory acts, and moral worth.

## Key facts

- The full theory takes the principles of justice as already secured and uses these principles in defining the other moral concepts. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p355]
- The definition of beneficent and supererogatory acts depends upon the full theory of the good. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p355]
- Rational plans of life which determine what things are good for human beings are themselves constrained by the principles of justice. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p355]
- The full theory enables distinguishing between beneficent acts (that advance another's good), benevolent actions (performed for the sake of another's good), and supererogatory acts (done at considerable sacrifice beyond duty). [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p390]

## Inferences

- Inference: The full theory can account for moral worth (the third main concept of ethics) once the principles of right are established through the original position.
