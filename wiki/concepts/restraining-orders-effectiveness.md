# Restraining Orders Effectiveness

## Summary

An analysis of the effectiveness of temporary restraining orders (TROs) in domestic violence and stalking cases. The book challenges the indiscriminate recommendation of restraining orders, noting that they may worsen some situations while helping others, and that their effectiveness varies based on the level of emotional investment and entitlement of the abuser.

## Key facts

- A study of 179 stalking cases found that about half of victims who had sought restraining orders felt their cases were worsened by them. [raw/books/psychology/The Gift of Fear - Gavin de Becker.pdf#p126]
- A Department of Justice study concluded that restraining orders were 'ineffective in stopping physical violence' but helpful in cases with no history of violent abuse. [raw/books/psychology/The Gift of Fear - Gavin de Becker.pdf#p126]
- More than a third of women had continuing problems after getting restraining orders; while 2.6 percent reported physical abuse immediately after, the rate more than tripled after six months. [raw/books/psychology/The Gift of Fear - Gavin de Becker.pdf#p127]
- Restraining orders are most effective on the reasonable person with limited emotional investment—in other words, least likely to be violent anyway. [raw/books/psychology/The Gift of Fear - Gavin de Becker.pdf#p127]
- The book cites cases including Shirley Lowery (stabbed 19 times waiting for her TRO hearing), Tammy Marie Davis (killed shortly after being served), and Connie Chaney (killed after obtaining four protective orders) as examples of TRO failures. [raw/books/psychology/The Gift of Fear - Gavin de Becker.pdf#p125]
- The book states the only good reason to get a restraining order is when the woman believes the man will honor it and leave her alone; using it to stop murder is applying the wrong strategy. [raw/books/psychology/The Gift of Fear - Gavin de Becker.pdf#p127]

## Inferences

- Inference: Restraining orders may create a false sense of security, causing women to remain 'available' to their pursuers when they should be making themselves unavailable.
- Inference: The criminal justice system's promotion of restraining orders may serve police and prosecutors more than victims.
