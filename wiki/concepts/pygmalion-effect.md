# Pygmalion Effect

## Summary

The Pygmalion effect, demonstrated by Rosenthal and Jacobson in 1968, showed that changing teachers' expectations for students led to exceptional performance from students with no prior reason to believe they were special. Students chosen at random and labeled as 'diamonds in the rough' significantly improved their IQ scores by year's end, demonstrating that the expectation of possibility changes outcomes.

## Key facts

- Rosenthal and Jacobson showed that changing teachers' expectations for students was shown to make exceptional students out of those for whom there was no reason to believe they were special. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p189]
- Elementary school students were chosen at random and their teachers were told they were 'diamonds in the rough' with hidden talents; by year's end, these students significantly improved their IQ scores. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p189]
- Langer states: 'Planting the idea of possibility changes outcomes, not unlike the way in which placebos work.'. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p190]

## Inferences

- Inference: Langer suggests that the Pygmalion effect demonstrates how expectations of possibility can change outcomes, similar to placebo effects in medicine.
