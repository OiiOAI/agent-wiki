# First-Order Theory of Consciousness

## Summary

First-order theory, championed by Ned Block, assumes that a perceptual representation is all that is needed for conscious experience of a stimulus. It stands in opposition to higher-order and global workspace theories by claiming that awareness occurs as part of stimulus processing itself, not requiring additional cognitive access mechanisms. Block distinguishes between access consciousness (what we can report on) and phenomenal consciousness (subjective experience that may exist without cognitive access).

## Key facts

- First-order theory assumes that the representation of a perceptual object is all that is needed for conscious experience of that stimulus. [raw/books/neuroscience/Anxious - Joseph LeDoux.pdf#p234]
- Ned Block distinguishes between access consciousness (cognitive access allowing reports) and phenomenal consciousness (subjective experience that may exist without access). [raw/books/neuroscience/Anxious - Joseph LeDoux.pdf#p235]
- According to Block, phenomenal experience can exist without the cognitive access that allows one to know one is experiencing it. [raw/books/neuroscience/Anxious - Joseph LeDoux.pdf#p235]

## Inferences

- Inference: First-order theory requires fewer processing steps than other theories but raises difficult questions about distinguishing unaccessed phenomenal consciousness from nonconscious states.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The idea of unaccessed phenomenal consciousness is difficult to test experimentally since awareness depends on access.
