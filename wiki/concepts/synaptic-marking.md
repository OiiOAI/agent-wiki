# Synaptic Marking and Specificity

## Summary

Synaptic marking is a hypothesis explaining how long-term memory changes can be localized to specific synapses despite gene expression occurring in the nucleus. The hypothesis proposes that transient modifications at a synapse 'mark' it for stabilization, allowing proteins synthesized in the cell body to be selectively captured and used at marked synapses.

## Key facts

- Goelet and Kandel outlined the synaptic marking hypothesis in their 1986 Nature review to explain how long-term changes are localized to specific synapses. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p279]
- Kelsey Martin found that five pulses of serotonin applied to one set of synapses produced long-lasting facilitation only at the stimulated synapses. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p279]
- Two things must occur at a marked synapse: activation of protein kinase A and activation of local protein synthesis machinery. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p281]
- Proteins synthesized in the cell body and shipped to terminals are sufficient to initiate synaptic growth, but local protein synthesis is needed to sustain that growth. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p281]
- Newly synthesized proteins are sent to all synapses, but only serotonin-stimulated synapses use them to initiate growth of new terminals. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p282]
