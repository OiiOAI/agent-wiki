# Reconstruction

## Summary

Reconstruction refers to Bartlett's insight that remembering is not replaying the past exactly, but using context and retrieved information as starting points to imagine how the past could have been. We construct stories on the fly based on personal and cultural experiences, tacking on details to flesh out narratives. This reconstructive nature allows us to fill in gaps but also introduces errors. The same processes that enable reconstruction also enable creativity—suggesting reconstruction is a feature rather than a bug of the memory system. Memory is compared to a painting: a mixture of faithful details, distorted embellishments, and inferences reflecting the creator's perspective.

## Key facts

- Frederic Bartlett concluded that 'remembering is an imaginative reconstruction,' not re-exciting fixed traces. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p77]
- Bartlett's famous 'War of the Ghosts' experiment showed people adapted story details to match their own cultural expectations (canoe became boat, seal hunting became fishing). [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p77]
- Memory is best compared to a painting: a mixture of faithful details, distorted embellishments, and inferences reflecting the creator's perspective. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p80]
