# Berkeley Admissions Paradox

## Summary

The Berkeley Admissions Paradox is a statistical phenomenon discovered in 1973 where overall acceptance rates favored men (44%) over women (35%), but when examined department by department, each department accepted women at higher rates than men. This is a form of Simpson's paradox. Analysis by Peter Bickel and Eugene Hammel concluded that women applied to more competitive departments, and no discrimination existed at the university level.

## Key facts

- In 1973, Berkeley's graduate school admitted 44% of male applicants and 35% of female applicants overall. [raw/books/strategy/The_Book_of_Why.epub#L2680-L2688]
- When examined department by department, each department accepted women at equal or higher rates than men. [raw/books/strategy/The_Book_of_Why.epub#L2688-L2695]
- The paradox arises because women applied in greater numbers to harder-to-enter departments. [raw/books/strategy/The_Book_of_Why.epub#L2705-L2715]
- Bickel distinguished 'bias' (association) from 'discrimination' (causal effect of sex on outcome). [raw/books/strategy/The_Book_of_Why.epub#L2720-L2730]
- William Kruskal showed that hidden confounders like State of Residence could reverse conclusions about discrimination. [raw/books/strategy/The_Book_of_Why.epub#L2740-L2755]
- The source paper for the Berkeley admissions paradox is Bickel, Hammel, and O'Connell (1975) published in Science. [raw/books/strategy/The_Book_of_Why.epub#L3901-L3902]
- The correspondence between Bickel and Kruskal is documented in Fairley and Mosteller (1977). [raw/books/strategy/The_Book_of_Why.epub#L3901-L3902]
