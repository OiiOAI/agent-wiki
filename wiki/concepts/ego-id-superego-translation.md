# Translation of Freud's Ich, Es, Überich as Ego, Id, Superego

## Summary

The rendition of Freud's German vernacular Ich and Es as Latin ego and id, and Überich as 'superego', has been widely criticized. Critics argue that Ich, as the German personal pronoun, has qualities of intimacy and immediacy—the ego is said to be intellectualized and transformed into 'lifeless jargon' by the Latin term. However, translators were confronted with Ich before psychoanalysis was born; by the Standard Edition's conception, 'the ego' was widely established in English philosophical, psychological, psychiatric, and neuroscientific literature. Crucially, Freud himself did not object to the translation of Ich as 'ego' according to available sources. The Stracheys initially opposed the Latin choice, and Jones turned the tide in its favor. Similar considerations apply to Es, where 'id' removes the concept from the realm of direct and personal experience.

## Key facts

- Brandt (1961) argued that Ich represents the speaking person, has the greatest intimacy and immediacy of any word in the German language, and evokes the developmental achievement of self-awareness.
