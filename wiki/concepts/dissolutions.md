# Dissolutions

## Summary

Dissolutions encompass the mythological theme of endings—end of the microcosm (individual) and end of the macrocosm (universe). As the created form of the individual must dissolve, so must the universe itself in the great cycles of creation and destruction described in Mayan, Viking, Buddhist, and Biblical traditions.

## Key facts

- The Egyptian Book of the Dead describes the soul's journey through the underworld where, like the Buddha, he enters the cessation of perception and sensation then rises through all the levels of cosmogonic emanation back to the source—declaring 'I am Yesterday, Today, and Tomorrow, and I have the power to be born a second time.'. [raw/books/philosophy/The Hero With a Thousand Faces (Paladin Bo - Joseph Campbell.pdf#p293]
- Mayan mythology describes the final destruction: the rain serpent stretching across the sky belching forth torrents of water, the old goddess of floods overturning the bowl of heavenly waters, and the black god stalking abroad with downward-pointed spear symbolic of universal destruction. [raw/books/philosophy/The Hero With a Thousand Faces (Paladin Bo - Joseph Campbell.pdf#p295]
- The Viking prophecy describes Fenris-Wolf running free with lower jaw against earth and upper against heaven, the world serpent rising in giant wrath, Naglfar (the ship of dead men's nails) loosed, and Surt casting fire over the earth until 'The sun turns black, earth sinks in the sea, the hot stars down from heaven are whirled.'. [raw/books/philosophy/The Hero With a Thousand Faces (Paladin Bo - Joseph Campbell.pdf#p296]
- Jesus described the signs of the end: nation rising against nation, kingdom against kingdom, famines, pestilences, earthquakes in divers places, false Christs and false prophets showing great signs to deceive if possible even the elect—when the sun shall be darkening and the moon shall not give her light. [raw/books/philosophy/The Hero With a Thousand Faces (Paladin Bo - Joseph Campbell.pdf#p297]

## Inferences

- Inference: The dissolution myths serve a therapeutic function—they teach acceptance of impermanence by showing that endings are built into the cosmic order and that destruction is followed by recreation, making change itself the永恒 principle.
