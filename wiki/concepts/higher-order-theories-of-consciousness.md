# Higher-Order Theories of Consciousness

## Summary

Higher-order theories propose that conscious experience requires at least two steps: a first-order representation (not consciously experienced) and a higher-order representation (which is consciously experienced). David Rosenthal argues that one must have the ability to reflect upon one's thoughts for consciousness to occur. Without higher-order representation, lower-order information remains in the cognitive unconscious. The theory distinguishes between nonconscious cognitive processes that give rise to conscious experiences and the higher-order cognitions that make us aware of those experiences.

## Key facts

- Higher-order theories assume that perceptual consciousness requires both a first-order representation (not consciously experienced) and a higher-order representation (which is consciously experienced). [raw/books/neuroscience/Anxious - Joseph LeDoux.pdf#p230]
- David Rosenthal argues that to be conscious, one must have the ability to reflect upon one's thoughts; unless a thought is the subject of another thought, the first thought is not conscious. [raw/books/neuroscience/Anxious - Joseph LeDoux.pdf#p231]
- Higher-order representations do not happen automatically but must be learned according to Axel Cleeremans's radical plasticity hypothesis. [raw/books/neuroscience/Anxious - Joseph LeDoux.pdf#p232]
- To be consciously aware of seeing an apple, one needs: (1) perceptual representation, (2) placement in working memory, (3) thought about the object ('That's an apple'), and possibly (4) thought about the thought ('I am seeing an apple'). [raw/books/neuroscience/Anxious - Joseph LeDoux.pdf#p231]

## Inferences

- Inference: Metacognition (thinking about thinking) measures higher-order thoughts but is not the same as consciousness itself.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The exact neural mechanisms implementing higher-order representations remain speculative.
