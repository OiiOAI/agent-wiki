# Innate Disposition to Hysteria

## Summary

Breuer argued against Janet's view that hysteria results from innate psychological weakness, proposing instead that the disposition to hysteria lies in an excess rather than a defect. Adolescents who later become hysterical are typically lively, gifted, and full of intellectual interests before falling ill. The disposition is characterized by a surplus of nervous excitation liberated even at rest, which needs to be utilized. Puberty intensifies this through the awakening of sexuality. The abnormal excitability allows excitation to flow into sensory nervous apparatuses normally accessible only to peripheral stimuli, producing hyperalgesia and conversion symptoms. However, an additional specific idiosyncrasy is required, as most lively and excitable people do not become hysterical.

## Inferences

- Inference: Breuer's emphasis on sexual factors as a major component of hysteria foreshadows Freud's later focus on sexuality in psychoanalytic theory.
- Inference: The distinction between general nervous excitability and the specific hysterical disposition suggests a multifactorial model of hysteria aetiology.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The exact nature of the 'specific idiosyncrasy' required for hysteria, beyond general nervous excitability, remains undefined.
