# Fear vs. Anxiety Distinction

## Summary

Fear and anxiety are distinct emotional states: fear is a response to a present, identifiable threat, while anxiety is a future-oriented, objectless state concerning uncertain threats that may never occur. This distinction, originally proposed by Kierkegaard and later developed by Freud, has significant implications for understanding brain mechanisms and treatment of anxiety disorders.

## Key facts

- Fear involves a present and identifiable threat, while anxiety relates to an uncertain, possible future threat with an unknown object or timing. [raw/books/neuroscience/Anxious - Joseph LeDoux.pdf#p28]
- Kierkegaard distinguished between fear (which has a specific object) and anxiety (an unfocused, objectless, future-oriented fear rooted in 'nothingness'). [raw/books/neuroscience/Anxious - Joseph LeDoux.pdf#p24]
- Freud distinguished primary anxiety (essentially fear, with an immediate object) from signal anxiety (essentially anxiety, objectless with diffuse harm expectation). [raw/books/neuroscience/Anxious - Joseph LeDoux.pdf#p23]
- Fear states occur when a threat is present or imminent; states of anxiety result when a threat is possible but its occurrence is uncertain. [raw/books/neuroscience/Anxious - Joseph LeDoux.pdf#p32]

## Inferences

- Inference: The distinction between fear and anxiety suggests different brain circuits may be engaged: specific threat detection circuits for fear versus more diffuse uncertainty-processing circuits for anxiety.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The exact neural substrates that differentiate fear from anxiety states remain to be fully elucidated.
