# Ionic Hypothesis

## Summary

The theory formulated by Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley explaining how action potentials are generated at the molecular level. The hypothesis states that voltage-gated sodium channels open first, allowing sodium ions to rush into the cell (producing the upstroke), followed by opening of voltage-gated potassium channels (producing the downstroke).

## Key facts

- Bernstein proposed in 1902 that the resting membrane potential (about -70 millivolts) results from potassium ions leaking out through non-gated channels. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p94]
- Hodgkin and Huxley discovered the action potential reaches +40 millivolts, not zero, contradicting Bernstein's prediction of 70 millivolts. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p97]
- Voltage-gated sodium channels open when membrane potential is reduced from -70mV to -55mV, allowing sodium ions to rush in and raise potential to +40mV. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p99]
- Sodium channels close after ~1 millisecond, and voltage-gated potassium channels open briefly, allowing potassium efflux that returns the cell to resting potential. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p99]
- A sodium-potassium pump restores the original concentration gradients after each action potential. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p100]
- Roderick MacKinnon received the 2003 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for determining the three-dimensional structure of potassium ion channels. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p101]
