# Free Will

## Summary

Crick's theory of Free Will, developed in correspondence with Luis Rinaldini and refined with Patricia Churchland, proposes that a machine (or brain) that plans future actions, is conscious of those plans but not their underlying computations, and can personify its behavior, will appear to itself to have Free Will. Damage to or near the anterior cingulate sulcus can eliminate this sense of Will, as demonstrated in a patient case described by Antonio Damasio.

## Key facts

- Crick's first assumption: part of the brain is concerned with making plans for future actions without necessarily carrying them out, and one can be conscious of such plans. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p281]
- Second assumption: one is not conscious of the computations done by this planning part of the brain but only of the decisions it makes. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p281]
- Third assumption: the decision to act on one plan or another is subject to the same limitations; one has immediate recall of what is decided but not of the computations that went into the decision. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p281]
- Such a machine will appear to itself to have Free Will, provided it can personify its behavior—it has an image of 'itself.'. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p281]
- The cause of a decision may be deterministic but chaotic—a very small perturbation may make a big difference to the end result, giving the appearance of the Will being 'free' since it would make the outcome essentially unpredictable. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p281]
- Antonio Damasio described a woman with damage in or near the anterior cingulate sulcus who appeared very unresponsive, lying quietly in bed with an alert expression, unable to speak spontaneously, and whose mind was 'empty.' She had lost her Will. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p282]
- The 'alien hand' syndrome, where a patient's hand makes movements the patient denies willing, is associated with damage in or near the anterior cingulate sulcus on the opposite side from the alien hand. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p283]
- The anterior cingulate region projects strongly to the corpus striatum on both sides of the brain, consistent with having a single Will that influences motor output bilaterally. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p283]

## Inferences

- Inference: Free Will may be located in or near the anterior cingulate sulcus, in a region that receives output from higher sensory regions and feeds into the planning levels of the motor system.
- Inference: Confabulation is likely a mechanism whereby the brain, given incomplete evidence, jumps to the simplest conclusion about why it made a certain choice.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Other areas in the front of the brain may also be involved in Free Will beyond the anterior cingulate.
- Uncertain: Whether the evidence from brain damage cases is sufficient to localize Free Will is uncertain; more experiments on animals and careful examination of more cases are needed.
