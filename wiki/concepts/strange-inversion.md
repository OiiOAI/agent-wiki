# Stranger Inversion

## Summary

The strange inversion is a concept introduced by Daniel Dennett and explored by Andy Clark to explain how we come to believe in qualia. The idea suggests that what we call subjective experiences like the taste of honey is not the cause of our responses but rather a label we apply to the web of responses that actually constitutes our behavior toward things.

## Key facts

- According to the strange inversion, the specific taste of honey is nothing but the subtle complex of responses it evokes in me—seeking it out, licking, spreading, pronouncing it tasty. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Experience Machine_ How Our Minds Pred - Andy Clark.pdf#p119]
- The web of behaviors and responses comes first, and the puzzling ineffable taste is really just a handy label for that web. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Experience Machine_ How Our Minds Pred - Andy Clark.pdf#p120]
- Advanced intelligences like us find ourselves able fluently to predict our own responses using simple shorthands that suffice for daily purposes. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Experience Machine_ How Our Minds Pred - Andy Clark.pdf#p120]

## Inferences

- Inference: The strange inversion suggests that our puzzlement about qualia may arise from self-opacity and simplified self-models that lead us to infer mysterious qualitative states that are actually just efficient predictive tools [raw/books/neuroscience/The Experience Machine_ How Our Minds Pred - Andy Clark.pdf#p126]

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The extent to which the strange inversion fully explains the hard problem of consciousness rather than merely reframing it is contested
