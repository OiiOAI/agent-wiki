# Biological Autonomy

## Summary

Biological autonomy refers to the self-determining nature of living systems, distinguishing them from heteronomous systems determined from outside. An autonomous system is defined by its endogenous, self-organizing and self-controlling dynamics, and determines its own domain of interaction with the environment. The paradigm case is autopoiesis: a self-producing network of molecular processes that produces the membrane boundary that contains it. Autonomous systems exhibit organizational and operational closure—they recursively depend on each other and constitute themselves as unities. Examples range from cells and nervous systems to immune networks and ecosystems.

## Key facts

- An autonomous system is defined by endogenous, self-organizing and self-controlling dynamics and does not have inputs and outputs in the usual sense. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p53]
- The constituent processes of an autonomous system recursively depend on each other, constitute the system as a unity, and determine a domain of possible interactions. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p53]
- Organizational closure refers to the self-referential network of relations defining the system; operational closure refers to the reentrant and recurrent dynamics. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p54]
- Autonomous systems must be thermodynamically far-from-equilibrium systems exchanging matter and energy with surroundings. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p54]
- The Closure Thesis states: 'Every autonomous system is organizationally closed.'. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p57]
- Autonomous systems bring forth a unity with self-producing identity and domain of interactions. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p72]
- Autonomy derives etymologically from being self-governing and means acting in accord with one's self—feeling free and volitional in one's actions. [raw/books/psychology/Why we do what we do _ understanding self - motivation - Edward L_ Deci, Richard Flaste.pdf#p12]
- When autonomous, people fully endorse their actions with a sense of interest and commitment; their actions emanate from their true sense of self. [raw/books/psychology/Why we do what we do _ understanding self - motivation - Edward L_ Deci, Richard Flaste.pdf#p12]
- Autonomy is distinct from independence or aloneness; people can be autonomous while also feeling deeply connected to others. [raw/books/psychology/Why we do what we do _ understanding self - motivation - Edward L_ Deci, Richard Flaste.pdf#p16]
- Providing choice enhances intrinsic motivation; subjects offered choices about which puzzles to work on spent more time playing with them and reported liking them more. [raw/books/psychology/Why we do what we do _ understanding self - motivation - Edward L_ Deci, Richard Flaste.pdf#p43]
- The concept of personal causation (from Richard deCharms) suggests people strive to feel like the 'origin' of their own actions rather than 'pawns' manipulated by external forces. [raw/books/psychology/Why we do what we do _ understanding self - motivation - Edward L_ Deci, Richard Flaste.pdf#p37]
