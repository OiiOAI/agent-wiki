# Mental Accounting

## Summary

Mental accounting is the system by which people organize, evaluate, and track financial activities, creating separate 'accounts' for different purposes. These accounts can be physical or purely mental, with a clear hierarchy of willingness to draw funds from each. Mental accounting explains the disposition effect (selling winners to close a positive account rather than holding the better-performing asset), reluctance to spend windfalls on their intended purpose, and willingness to drive through a snowstorm to use a free ticket.

## Key facts

- Mental accounting is a form of narrow framing: money is mentally allocated to specific purposes (groceries, savings, education, emergencies), and the hierarchy of willingness to draw from each account controls spending behavior in ways that an economically rational agent would not exhibit. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p442]
- People often pay for self-control devices: they maintain savings accounts that earn low interest while carrying high-interest credit card debt, using the mental account separation to control spending despite the economic irrationality. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p442]
- The disposition effect—the preference for selling stocks that have gone up in value over stocks that have declined—is explained by mental accounting: investors want to close each stock account with a positive balance, experiencing the sale of a losing stock as a loss realization rather than as a rebalancing decision. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p444]
- Tax considerations create a seasonal pattern: US investors sell more losing stocks in December to realize tax losses, but during the other 11 months the mental accounting bias overrides financial logic. Holding onto losing positions while selling winners costs an estimated 3.4% in foregone returns per year. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p445]
- In the theater ticket problem, 46% of people would not buy a replacement ticket after losing their original ticket, but 88% would still buy a ticket after losing equivalent cash—because the lost ticket is mentally charged to the show account (doubling the cost) while lost cash is charged to general wealth. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p478]
- In the calculator problem, 68% would drive 20 minutes to save $5 on a $15 calculator, but only 29% would do so to save $5 on a $125 calculator—the same absolute saving is evaluated relative to the purchase price in a topical mental account. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p585]
- The sunk cost fallacy: continuing an endeavor because of previously invested resources even when new evidence suggests it is no longer worthwhile, results from treating sunk costs as losses that must be recovered. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p478]
- The 'loss deadening' effect: people continue playing tennis in pain after paying membership fees because stopping would convert the fee into a realized loss, whereas continuing keeps it as an 'investment'—a form of the cost-loss differential. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p591]

## Inferences

- Inference: Mental accounting can be either beneficial or costly: beneficial when it supports self-control and budgeting goals, costly when it leads to the disposition effect, sunk cost escalation, or reluctance to allocate resources to their highest-value use across accounts.
- Inference: Mental accounting explains why people who would not buy a replacement ticket after losing it would buy a ticket after losing equivalent cash—both economically equivalent situations are psychologically different because the ticket loss is mentally attached to the entertainment account.
