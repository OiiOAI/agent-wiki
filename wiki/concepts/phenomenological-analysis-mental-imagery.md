# Phenomenological Analysis of Mental Imagery

## Summary

According to the phenomenological analysis of mental imagery presented in this work (following Husserl and Marbach), visual imagining is not the inspection of a mental picture but rather the activity of mentally representing an object by subjectively simulating or emulating a perceptual experience of that object. The visualized object is phenomenally absent and mentally evoked, unlike the perceptual object which is bodily present.

## Key facts

- In visual imaging or visualizing, we do not experience mental pictures; instead we visualize an object or scene by mentally enacting or entertaining a possible perceptual experience of that object or scene. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p275]
- To visualize X is to mentally represent X by subjectively simulating or emulating a neutralized perceptual experience of X, where neutralized means the belief in the actual (past or future) occurrence of the experience is suspended. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p286]
- Sartre argued that a mental image properly understood is not a content contained in consciousness; rather, it is a dynamic and relational act of consciousness: 'an image is nothing other than a relation.'. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p295]
- The phenomenological analysis supports descriptionalism's claim that visualizing is not the inspection of a mental image, but undermines analytical isomorphism—the assumption that imagery experience requires depictive neural structures corresponding to pictorial content. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p292]

## Inferences

- Inference: The phenomenological analysis does not favor descriptionalism over pictorialism regarding subpersonal neural representations; it only clarifies the personal-level experience and suggests that whether depictive or propositional structures mediate imagery at the neural level remains an empirical question.
