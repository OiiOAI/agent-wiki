# Familiarity

## Summary

Familiarity is a sense of knowing that something has been experienced before, without access to specific episodic details. It is supported by the perirhinal cortex rather than the hippocampus. Familiarity differs from episodic memory in that it provides a strong sense of prior encounter without the ability to mentally time travel to the original event.

## Key facts

- Andy Yonelinas proposed that episodic memory gives access to tangible memories from specific places and times, while familiarity can be strong or weak but doesn't provide anything specific to hold onto. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p114]
- Research by Ranganath and Yonelinas found that activity in the perirhinal cortex was associated with memories giving a sense of familiarity—the more activity when reading a word, the more familiar it seemed later. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p115]
- Activity in the perirhinal cortex was not related to people's ability to recollect context of studied words, unlike hippocampal activity. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p115]
- The beer-bet study provided evidence that memories are not just strong or weak; the brain has two different kinds of memory—episodic memory supported by the hippocampus and familiarity supported by the perirhinal cortex. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p115]
