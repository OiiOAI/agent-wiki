# Conjunction Fallacy

## Summary

The conjunction fallacy occurs when we judge a subset (Chris works for a major bank AND runs its Third World foundation) as more probable than the whole set (Chris works for a major bank). We are easy prey because we have an innate attraction to 'harmonious' or 'plausible' stories.

## Key facts

- The conjunction fallacy is at play when a subset seems larger than the entire set, which by definition cannot be the case. [raw/books/strategy/The Art of Thinking Clearly _ Better thinking, Better - Rolf Dobelli; Nicky Griffin.pdf#p99]
- Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky studied the conjunction fallacy extensively. [raw/books/strategy/The Art of Thinking Clearly _ Better thinking, Better - Rolf Dobelli; Nicky Griffin.pdf#p99]
- In 1982, at an international conference for future research, experts presented with 'Oil consumption will decrease by 30%' rated it less likely than 'A dramatic rise in oil prices will lead to a 30% reduction in oil consumption' — the additional detail made the scenario seem more plausible. [raw/books/strategy/The Art of Thinking Clearly _ Better thinking, Better - Rolf Dobelli; Nicky Griffin.pdf#p100]
- 85–90% of Stanford graduate business students and 89% of social science students violated the conjunction rule by rating 'bank teller and feminist' more probable than 'bank teller.'. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p205]
- The conjunction fallacy persists because more detailed descriptions are more 'representativeness' (coherent with stereotypes), and representativeness is what System 1 evaluates, not probability. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p204]
- When participants were forced to compare only the two critical options directly, 64% of statistically trained Stanford/Berkeley students correctly chose 'bank teller' as more probable—showing that evaluation together can sometimes overcome the fallacy. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p206]
- The dice problem (HMMHM vs. HMHMMM) replicated the conjunction fallacy using outcomes rather than descriptions: nearly two-thirds chose the longer, more representative sequence despite it being less probable. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p211]
- When the survey question was phrased 'How many of 100...' instead of 'What percentage...,' the conjunction fallacy dropped from 65% to 25% errors, because frequency formats make the subset relationship more salient. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p212]

## Inferences

- Inference: The Linda problem demonstrates that System 1 evaluates coherence and representativeness, not logical probability, and that System 2 often endorses these intuitive judgments without applying logical rules. The fallacy is an instance of WYSIATI (what you see is all there is): the coherent story feels true, so its probability is overestimated.
