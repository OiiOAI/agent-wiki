# Success to the Successful

## Summary

Success to the successful (or competitive exclusion) occurs when winners of a competition receive the means to compete more effectively in the future, creating a reinforcing loop where winners win more and losers are eliminated. In ecology, this is the competitive exclusion principle. In economics, Marx critiqued how firms competing in the same market would eventually concentrate. The trap perpetuates inequality: the rich get richer through multiple feedback loops (better education, access to credit, political influence). Solutions include diversification, antitrust laws, leveling the playing field, and periodic redistribution.

## Key facts

- Winners receive as part of their reward the means to compete more effectively in the future. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking in Systems - Donella H. Meadows.pdf#p157]
- In ecology this is called the 'competitive exclusion principle'—two species competing for the same niche will lead to extinction of the weaker competitor. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking in Systems - Donella H. Meadows.pdf#p157]
- Marx's critique: two competing firms will exhibit the same behavior, with one gaining advantage then reinvesting to produce more and earn more. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking in Systems - Donella H. Meadows.pdf#p157]
- The poor pay highest prices, receive least government expenditure, get ideas and technologies last, and face disease and pollution first. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking in Systems - Donella H. Meadows.pdf#p159]
- Solutions: diversification, antitrust laws, policies that level the playing field, and mechanisms to redistribute (progressive taxation, charity, labor unions). [raw/books/strategy/Thinking in Systems - Donella H. Meadows.pdf#p159]
- If the winners of a competition are systematically rewarded with the means to win again, a reinforcing feedback loop is created. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking in Systems - Donella H. Meadows.pdf#p224]
- If allowed to proceed uninhibited, winners eventually take all while losers are eliminated. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking in Systems - Donella H. Meadows.pdf#p224]
- The remedy includes diversification to allow losers to exit and enter different games; strict limitation on the fraction any one winner may capture (antitrust laws); policies that level the playing field; and policies that devise rewards not biased toward the next round of competition.
