# Parent-Offspring Conflict

## Summary

Parent-offspring conflict, analyzed by R.L. Trivers in 1974, describes the evolutionary conflict of interest between parents and their children. While parents are equally related to all offspring (50% relatedness), each child is 100% related to themselves but only 50% related to each sibling. This asymmetry creates genetic incentives for children to demand more parental investment than is optimal from the parent's perspective. The conflict manifests in disputes over weaning time, food allocation, and other resource-sharing decisions.

## Key facts

- Trivers's 1974 paper 'Parent-Offspring Conflict' analyzed how children may grab more than their 'fair share' of parental investment because a child is 100% related to itself but only 50% related to each sibling. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p162]
- Parent-offspring conflict over weaning occurs because the mother wants to stop suckling the present child to prepare for the next one, while the child wants to continue receiving the convenient, trouble-free milk supply. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p163]
- Both mother and child agree that the child should not continue suckling when the cost to future children exceeds double the benefit to the child, but they disagree during an intermediate period where the child gets more than its share from the mother's perspective while the cost to others is still less than double the benefit to him. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p163]
- Baby birds may cheat by screaming louder than their actual hunger level to attract more parental food, creating an evolutionary arms race between offspring deception and parental discrimination. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p164]
- R.D. Alexander argued that parents will always win the parent-offspring conflict because genes for juvenile selfishness would reduce the individual's fitness when it becomes an adult parent. Dawkins counters that the relatedness is symmetrical and both arguments are flawed by artificial asymmetry. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p171]
- There is no general answer to who wins the battle of the generations—the outcome is a compromise between what the child wants and what the parent wants, comparable to but less fierce than the conflict between cuckoos and foster parents. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p173]
- Robert Trivers' paper on parent-offspring conflict has stood the test of time; more detailed mathematical and genetic models have confirmed his largely verbal arguments follow from Darwinian theory. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p353]
- Alexander conceded he was wrong to argue parental victory follows inevitably from Darwinian assumptions; Charnov offered a different argument for parental manipulation. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p353]
- Charnov's point: a young female in a monogamous species is genetically indifferent between leaving to reproduce and staying to help rear full siblings, but her old mother values new children twice as much as new grandchildren. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p353]
- The conflict is a 'walk-over' for the parents because only the parents see it as a conflict; the daughter has no inducement to resist manipulation since both options are genetically equivalent to her. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p354]
- The analogy is a race where one runner gets paid only for winning while the opponent gets paid whether they win or lose; the first runner tries harder and will probably win. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p354]
- Conditions vary from species to species; while other things will usually not be equal, Charnov's reasoning supports parental manipulation theory alongside Alexander's practical arguments about parents being bigger and stronger. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p354]

## Inferences

- Inference: Runts may 'willingly' die when their expected benefit from continued parental investment falls below half the potential benefit the same resources could provide to siblings, because a gene for graceful self-sacrifice has 50% chance of being in each saved sibling but very low chance of surviving in the dying runt.
- Inference: Zahavi's proposed baby blackmail tactic (screaming to attract predators) is unlikely to evolve in ordinary species because the blackmailing baby would have too much to lose if a predator actually came, though it might work for brood parasites like cuckoos who have no genetic stake in nestmates.
- Inference: Under monogamy with guaranteed full siblings, parental manipulation succeeds structurally because offspring lack genetic motivation to resist.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Whether baby swallow fratricide (actively throwing eggs from the nest) actually occurs in nature—the hypothesis is suggested but Dawkins acknowledges it is difficult to believe no one would have seen such behavior if it were common.
- Uncertain: How much Charnov's abstract argument explains actual parent-offspring conflict in specific species requires empirical testing.
