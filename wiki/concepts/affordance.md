# Affordance

## Summary

Affordances are the motor senses or meanings that things in the environment have which elicit appropriate actions from a subject. Coined by James Gibson, affordances are relational properties that exist only in relation to the capabilities and motor skills of the perceiving-acting subject—things offer affordances only to beings that can perceive and exploit them.

## Key facts

- Things in my surroundings, such as teacups, computer keys, and door handles, have motor senses or meanings, what Gibson (1979) calls 'affordances,' which elicit appropriate actions. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p245]
- Things in the world bring forth suitable intentional actions and motor projects from the subject (the subject is a project of the world), but things in the world have specific motor senses or affordances only in relation to the motor skills of the subject (the world is projected by the subject). [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p245]
- A terrestrial surface that is nearly horizontal, nearly flat, sufficiently extended, and rigid relative to an animal affords support and is called a substratum, ground, or floor. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p151]
- If a surface of support with the four properties is also knee-high relative to a perceiver, it affords sitting on—a stool, bench, chair, or ledge. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p151]
- A detached object must be comparable in size to the animal to afford behavior; to be graspable, an object must have opposite surfaces separated by a distance less than the span of the hand. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p155]
- Affordances are invariant—they do not change as the need of the observer changes, though the observer may or may not perceive or attend to them. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p162]
- The niche for a species in ecology is a set of affordances. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p152]
- Affordances are either positive (beneficial, life-promoting) or negative (injurious, life-threatening). [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p129]
- The concept of affordance was derived from Koffka's 'demand character' and Lewin's 'valence' (Aufforderungscharakter), but with a crucial difference—affordances are invariant, not bestowed by observer needs. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p162]

## Inferences

- Inference: Affordances are the complementarity of animal and environment, implying that perception is fundamentally relational rather than either purely subjective or purely objective.
- Inference: Since affordances point both to the environment and to the observer, the information specifying affordances must likewise be relational—accompanying information about the observer's body along with information about environmental properties.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Whether all affordances are directly perceivable or some require learning. Gibson states basic affordances are usually perceivable directly, but learning is needed for things that 'lie' (e.g., poison ivy looking like harmless ivy).
