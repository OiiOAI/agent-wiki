# Archaic Heritage

## Summary

Freud's hypothesis that humans possess not only innate dispositions but also memory traces of experiences of earlier generations—inherited independently of direct communication or education. This concept bridges individual and group psychology by providing a mechanism for how forgotten primal events influence later generations.

## Key facts

- Freud asserts that the archaic heritage of human beings comprises not only dispositions but also memory traces of the experience of earlier generations. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p7264]
- Freud states that analytic research reveals reactions to early traumas that diverge from what the subject himself has really experienced in ways that fit better with phylogenetic events and can only be explained by such influence. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p7264]
- Freud acknowledges his position conflicts with biological science's refusal to accept inheritance of acquired characteristics, but maintains he cannot dispense with this factor. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p7264]

## Inferences

- Inference: By assuming survival of memory traces in the archaic heritage, Freud claims we can deal with peoples as we do with individual neurotics—this bridges individual and group psychology.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Freud admits the evidence for archaic heritage consists mainly of residual phenomena from analytic work requiring phylogenetic derivation, calling his position 'audacity which cannot be avoided.'
