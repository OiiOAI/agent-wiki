# Brain allometry

## Summary

Brain allometry is the study of scaling relationships between brain size and body size across species. The analysis involves specific statistical challenges, particularly when the structure being measured (brain) is a large component of the whole (body).

## Key facts

- Within-species brain size/body size scaling slopes are approximately 0.1 to 0.2, much shallower than the 0.6–0.8 slopes observed within whole orders of mammals. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p511]
- This within-species scaling relationship was first demonstrated by Lapicque in 1907 using dogs. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p511]
- A major analytical error in brain allometry is confounding a part with the whole when the part is a significant fraction of the whole, which artificially shifts trends and reduces variance. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p512]
- The cerebral cortex comprises as much as 70% of the whole brain in large primates (with underlying white matter), causing analyses that fail to correct for this to greatly underestimate variance among species. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p512]
- Part-whole confounding in brain analyses causes underestimation of human divergence from trends and biases assessments toward isometry. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p512]
- This statistical artifact can be avoided by restricting comparisons to nonoverlapping structures, or comparing part-whole relationships only where the part is a small fraction of the whole. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p512]
