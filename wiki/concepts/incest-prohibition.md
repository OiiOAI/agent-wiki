# Incest Prohibition

## Summary

The horror of incest refers to the intense psychological aversion to sexual relations between blood relatives found across human societies. Freud argued that this prohibition represents a universal feature of human psychology, rooted in unconscious childhood desires that are later repressed.

## Key facts

- Australian aborigines set before themselves with the most scrupulous care and painful severity the aim of avoiding incestuous sexual relations, with their whole social organization seeming to serve that purpose. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p4121]
- Freud emphasized that the horror of incest displayed by savages is essentially an infantile feature and reveals a striking agreement with the mental life of neurotic patients. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p4134]
- Psychoanalysis taught that a boy's earliest choice of objects for his love is incestuous, with those objects being his mother and his sister, and that he liberates himself from this attraction as he grows up. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p4133]
- A neurotic invariably exhibits some degree of psychical infantilism, with incestuous fixations continuing to play the principal part in his unconscious mental life. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p4134]
- The relation of a child to his parents, dominated by incestuous longings, is regarded by Freud as the nuclear complex of neurosis. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p4134]

## Inferences

- Inference: Freud's linking of the anthropological incest prohibition with the psychoanalytic concept of the Oedipus complex represents a central theoretical integration of anthropology and psychoanalysis.
