# Visual Ego

## Summary

The visual ego refers to the observer's awareness of their own body in visual perception. Gibson argues that the occluding boundary of the field of view constitutes 'here' while its content constitutes 'out there.' The occupied point of observation includes a solid angle filled by the observer's body, specifying the self. This challenges the traditional view that egocentric perception reflects childish subjectivity.

## Key facts

- The occluding boundary of the field of view constitutes 'here.' The content and details of the field are 'out there,' and smaller detail means greater distance. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p229]
- When a point of observation is occupied, the ambient optic array includes a solid angle filled by the observer, having a boundary unique to the observer's particular anatomy. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p229]
- Koffka recognized that phenomenal space extends behind as well as in front, with the Ego lying between—a segregated object like others in phenomenal space. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p228]
- The nose yields what retinal theory calls the maximum limit of crossed diplopia, serving as a theoretical zero for the dimension of distance from here. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p229]
- The theory that young children are necessarily egocentric because they start with perspective appearances is a myth—the earliest visual experiences suggest they are not confined to surfaces seen-now-from-here. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p223]

## Inferences

- Inference: Gibson's theory implies that perceiving the environment entails coperceiving where one is in the environment and being in the world at that place—this is neither subjective nor objective but a neglected fact.
