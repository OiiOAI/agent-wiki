# Hedonic Adaptation

## Summary

Hedonic adaptation (also called the 'hedonic treadmill') is the psychological phenomenon whereby human beings always return to a baseline level of emotional wellbeing regardless of positive or negative life changes. The book cites a famous study of lottery winners and accident victims (including paraplegics) showing that after initial adjustment periods, both groups returned to their pre-event happiness levels. This demonstrates that permanent extreme happiness or misery is impossible—circumstances change but the emotional set-point remains stable. The implication for acceptance is profound: whatever one feels right now will not last. The book uses this to argue against chasing permanent happiness and instead advocates acceptance of fluctuating emotional states.

## Key facts

- A study of 22 lottery winners showed their happiness levels returned to baseline after initial excitement wore off. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Geek_ Secular Meditation for S - Michael Taft.pdf#p127]
- Paraplegic accident victims, despite permanent loss of mobility, also returned to their pre-accident happiness levels over time. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Geek_ Secular Meditation for S - Michael Taft.pdf#p127]
- The return to baseline emotional state is called 'hedonic adaptation' or the 'hedonic treadmill'. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Geek_ Secular Meditation for S - Michael Taft.pdf#p126]
- The book uses hedonic adaptation to argue that permanent extreme happiness is impossible and acceptance of emotional fluctuation is more effective. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Geek_ Secular Meditation for S - Michael Taft.pdf#p126]

## Inferences

- Inference: motions always return to baseline, pursuing external conditions for permanent happiness is misguided—internal practices like meditation may be more effective than external changes.
