# The Lindy Effect

## Summary

The Lindy Effect states that for nonperishable things (technologies, ideas, books), every additional day of survival translates into a longer additional life expectancy. A technology that has survived 40 years can be expected to survive another 40 years, and if it survives another decade, it can be expected to survive another 50 years. This is the inverse of perishable things (humans), where additional life reduces remaining life expectancy.

## Key facts

- For the perishable, every additional day in life translates into a shorter additional life expectancy. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p400]
- For the nonperishable, every additional day may imply a longer life expectancy proportional to past life. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p400]
- A book in print for 40 years can be expected to be in print for another 40 years; surviving another decade suggests 50 more years. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p400]
- Richard Gott tested the Lindy effect by predicting Broadway shows on May 17, 1993 and was proven right with 95 percent accuracy. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p401]
- Technologies that were prone to aging are already dead; surviving technologies have found something that works. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p401]

## Inferences

- Inference: The Lindy Effect implies that the future is mostly in the past. Technologies and practices that have survived millennia (wine, chairs, silverware, walking) are more likely to persist than newly invented ones. This inverts the common tendency to over-technologize future projections.
