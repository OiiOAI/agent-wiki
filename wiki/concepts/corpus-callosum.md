# Corpus Callosum

## Summary

The major band of neural tissue connecting the two cerebral hemispheres, containing an estimated 300-800 million fibers. McGilchrist emphasizes that a significant portion of these connections serve inhibitory rather than excitatory functions, suggesting the corpus callosum may exist primarily to allow one hemisphere to inhibit the other.

## Key facts

- The corpus callosum contains an estimated 300-800 million fibres connecting topologically similar areas in either hemisphere, yet only 2 per cent of cortical neurones are connected by this tract. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p34]
- A large number of callosal connections serve inhibitory functions; stimulation of neurones in one hemisphere commonly results in an initial brief excitatory response followed by prolonged inhibitory arousal in the contralateral hemisphere. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p35]
- As brains evolve to become larger, interhemispheric connections actually decrease relative to brain size rather than increasing. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p35]
- Severing the corpus callosum (split-brain procedure) produces surprisingly little effect on everyday functioning, with patients appearing almost as if nothing had happened. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p36]

## Inferences

- Inference: The inhibitory nature of many callosal connections suggests that separation of hemispheric processing may be as important as integration.
