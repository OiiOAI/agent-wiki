# Cumulative culture

## Summary

Cumulative culture refers to the human capacity to build on previous cultural innovations over generations, leading to knowledge and technology that far exceeds what any individual could develop alone. This ratchet effect distinguishes human cultural evolution from animal traditions.

## Key facts

- Two major differences between cultural and genetic evolution: (1) cultural innovations spread laterally through observation and copying, while genetic innovations spread only vertically; (2) cultural innovations can be driven by intelligent designers trying to solve problems, while genetic innovation happens only by random mutation. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p399]
- Richerson and Boyd argue that cultural evolution requires gene-culture coevolution: genes evolved to make us learn from others and teach them, creating the conditions for cultural accumulation. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p399]
- Humans began accumulating culture during the late Pleistocene, with cultural artifacts showing increasing stability across time and space—unlike the flexibility of chimpanzee tool use. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p398]
- The transition to cumulative culture likely required shared intentionality, which allowed people to teach, imitate, and coordinate in ways that chimpanzees cannot. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p396]

## Inferences

- Inference: Cumulative culture enabled humans to adapt to environments faster than genetic evolution could track, explaining humanity's rapid spread across diverse ecological niches.
