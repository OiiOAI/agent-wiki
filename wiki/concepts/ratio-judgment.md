# Ratio Judgment

## Summary

Ratio judgment is a natural function of neuronal circuits arising from their multiplicative organization and log-normal dynamics. Every animal can effortlessly estimate ratios, which is sufficient for survival decisions like evaluating predator size or prey availability. This 'number sense' differs fundamentally from precise counting, which requires learned symbolic representations.

## Key facts

- Ratio judgment is a natural function of neuronal circuits because perception follows logarithmic Weber-Fechner scaling, making relative differences more salient than absolute ones. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain from Inside Out - György Buzsáki MD, PhD.pdf#p326]
- Quantity judgment differs from number-based counting; animals estimate ratios without understanding exact numerical differences. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain from Inside Out - György Buzsáki MD, PhD.pdf#p352]
- The good-enough brain can make fast and efficient judgments about quantities under most conditions even without any clues about numbers. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain from Inside Out - György Buzsáki MD, PhD.pdf#p352]

## Inferences

- Inference: The ability to precisely distinguish between 20 and 21 objects cannot be innately understood and requires learned symbolic representations, unlike ratio estimation.
- Inference: The distinction between innate ratio estimation and learned counting explains why mathematics as formal symbolic manipulation took millions of years to develop.
