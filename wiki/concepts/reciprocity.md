# Reciprocity

## Summary

The reciprocity rule obligates individuals to repay what they have received in kind, creating a sense of indebtedness that powerful enough to override other factors like dislike for the requester. This rule is universal across human cultures and serves as one of the most potent weapons of influence, enabling both fair exchanges and exploitative compliance tactics.

## Key facts

- The rule for reciprocation states that we should try to repay in kind what another person has provided us—a favor should be met with a favor. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p23]
- Sociologist Alvin Gouldner's intensive study found no human society exists that does not subscribe to the reciprocity rule. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p24]
- Archaeologist Richard Leakey credits the reciprocity system as the essence of what makes humans human, enabling food-sharing and skills in an honored network of obligation. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p24]
- In 1985, Ethiopia—despite being the most impoverished nation—sent $5,000 in earthquake aid to Mexico because Mexico had sent aid to Ethiopia in 1935 when invaded by Italy, demonstrating reciprocity's power transcending need and self-interest. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p25]
- Dennis Regan's experiment showed subjects who received an unsolicited Coke bought twice as many raffle tickets as those who did not, regardless of whether they liked Joe. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p26]
- The reciprocity rule overwhelmed the normal influence of liking—subjects who disliked Joe bought as many tickets as those who liked him when they felt indebted. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p27]
- The Hare Krishna Society shifted from direct solicitation to giving flowers before requesting donations, using the reciprocity rule to overcome public dislike of their appearance. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p28]
- An uninvited gift creates obligation even when unwanted—the reciprocity rule does not require that we have requested what we received to feel indebted to repay. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p33]
- Marcel Mauss described the social pressures surrounding gift-giving as including an obligation to give, an obligation to receive, and an obligation to repay. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p34]
- A small first favor can produce obligation to agree to a substantially larger return—a dime's worth of Coke in the 1960s produced an average fifty-cent return in raffle tickets. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p37]

## Inferences

- Inference: The obligation to receive reduces personal choice about whom to be indebted to, placing that power in the hands of whoever first provides an unsolicited favor.
- Inference: The psychological burden of indebtedness drives people to agree to larger returns than they received simply to relieve the discomfort of owing.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The exact mechanisms by which cultures initially established the reciprocity rule—whether through rational calculation or emergent social convention—remain unclear.
