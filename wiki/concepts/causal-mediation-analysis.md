# Causal Mediation Analysis

## Summary

Causal mediation analysis is a statistical framework for decomposing the total effect of a treatment into indirect effects operating through intermediate variables (mediators) and direct effects operating through other pathways. The field underwent a dramatic transition from the traditional Baron-Kenny (1986) approach based on regression coefficients to a modern counterfactual-based framework that properly addresses confounding and identification assumptions.

## Key facts

- The traditional approach to mediation analysis was established by Baron and Kenny (1986) using moderator-mediator variable distinctions in social psychology. [raw/books/strategy/The_Book_of_Why.epub#L3884-L3885]
- The modern counterfactual-based approach to causal mediation was developed as a replacement for the Baron-Kenny statistical framework. [raw/books/strategy/The_Book_of_Why.epub#L3884-L3885]
- Key references in causal mediation analysis include MacKinnon (2008), Imai, Keele, and Yamamoto (2010), and Muthén and Asparouhov (2015). [raw/books/strategy/The_Book_of_Why.epub#L3882-L3883]
- Shpitser (2013) provides a general criterion for estimating arbitrary path-specific effects in graphs. [raw/books/strategy/The_Book_of_Why.epub#L3892-L3893]
- De Stavola et al. (2015) addresses mediation analysis with intermediate confounding. [raw/books/strategy/The_Book_of_Why.epub#L3891-L3892]

## Inferences

- Inference: The transition from Baron-Kenny to counterfactual methods represents a fundamental shift in how mediation is conceptualized, moving from regression-based decomposition to causal inference principles
