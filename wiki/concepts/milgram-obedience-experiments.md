# Milgram Obedience Experiments

## Summary

Stanley Milgram's obedience experiments at Yale demonstrated that approximately 65 percent of participants were willing to deliver potentially lethal electrical shocks to a screaming victim when ordered to do so by an authority figure in a lab coat, revealing the powerful human tendency toward obedience to legitimate authority.

## Key facts

- About two-thirds of Milgram's subjects delivered all thirty shock levels up to 450 volts, continuing when the victim screamed, begged to be released, and eventually went silent. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p190]
- None of the forty initial subjects quit when the victim first demanded release, or when he began begging, or even when he screamed in agony at 300 volts. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p191]
- Psychiatrists predicted only about one person in a thousand would continue to the end; colleagues estimated 1-2 percent. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p191]
- Female subjects were just as likely as males to deliver all shocks to the victim. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p191]
- Even when the victim mentioned having a heart condition, 65 percent of subjects continued through maximum shock. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p191]
- When the researcher ordered the teacher to stop (while the victim insisted on continuing), 100 percent of subjects refused to deliver another shock. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p193]

## Inferences

- Inference: The experiments reveal that ordinary people, when placed in certain authority structures, will inflict considerable harm. This has profound implications for understanding behavior in hierarchical organizations and systems.
