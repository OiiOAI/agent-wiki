# Mindful Decision-Making

## Summary

Langer's decision-making theory rejects the standard rational-agent model (cost-benefit analysis, subjective expected utility). She argues that decisions are made in irreducible uncertainty, that every cost is also a benefit from a different perspective, and that the goal should be to 'make the decision right' rather than 'make the right decision.' She proposes that once a choice is made, one should commit fully, look for advantages, and reframe the outcome positively—analogous to how a purchased television is enjoyed regardless of which store or context influenced the choice.

## Key facts

- Langer argues against cost-benefit analysis for decision-making, noting that there is no natural endpoint to the amount of information that could be considered for any decision. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p67-68]
- She challenges Kahneman's System 1/System 2 framework, arguing that both can be mindless; System 2 is effortful but not inherently mindful. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p65-67]
- Langer's core decision principle is: 'Don't try to make the right decision, make the decision right,' meaning commit fully to any chosen option and find advantages in it. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p70-71]
- Research by Kiesler found that the more choices offered, the faster people chose; Schwartz and colleagues found that considering many options correlates with decreased happiness, self-esteem, and satisfaction and increased depression and regret. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p68-69]
- Danziger et al. found that favorable parole decisions dropped from 65 percent to zero before judges' food breaks and recovered to 65 percent after, showing that judicial decisions were most influenced by hunger rather than legal reasoning. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p74]
- Langer argues that 'risk-taking is really an observer's phenomenon'; people do things that make sense to them, and if others believed what they did, they would likely behave the same way. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p29]

## Inferences

- Inference: Langer's decision theory implies that decision stress is largely self-imposed through the false belief in a single 'correct' choice that can be identified through analysis.
