# Blitzkrieg

## Summary

Blitzkrieg (lightning war) was the German military strategy of 1940 that combined fast-moving armored divisions with air support to penetrate enemy lines and encircle forces, causing rapid collapse through psychological disruption rather than attrition. The May 1940 attack on France achieved in two weeks what had required years of bloody trench warfare in World War I. The strategy used time as its primary device, attacking the enemy's ability to make decisions by creating unexpected, abrupt, and disorienting changes that generated chaos, panic, and paralysis. Key attributes included Einheit, Fingerspitzengefühl, Auftragstaktik, and Schwerpunkt. German forces achieved victory despite having no numerical advantage and lagging in technology.

## Key facts

- During the night of May 9th and 10th, 1940, Germany attacked Belgium and Holland, beginning the Blitzkrieg against France. [raw/books/strategy/Certain to Win - The Strategy of John Boyd, Applied to - Richards, Chet, Richards, Chester W.pdf#p9]
- The French and British anticipated the attack through Belgium similar to World War I and rushed forward to meet them—it was a trap. [raw/books/strategy/Certain to Win - The Strategy of John Boyd, Applied to - Richards, Chet, Richards, Chester W.pdf#p9]
- Three corps of eight German armored divisions pushed through the Ardennes Forest and crossed the Meuse River at Sedan by May 14. [raw/books/strategy/Certain to Win - The Strategy of John Boyd, Applied to - Richards, Chet, Richards, Chester W.pdf#p13]
- At the start of the attack, Germany had no advantage in numbers and lagged in technology yet won easily through strategy. [raw/books/strategy/Certain to Win - The Strategy of John Boyd, Applied to - Richards, Chet, Richards, Chester W.pdf#p13]
- German tank technology was not superior—one for one, French tanks were state of the art; Germany had only 349 of its most modern tank design by May 1940. [raw/books/strategy/Certain to Win - The Strategy of John Boyd, Applied to - Richards, Chet, Richards, Chester W.pdf#p14]
- The purpose of Blitzkrieg was not to cope with chaos but to cause and exploit it—the cascading of panic and chaos accounts for the German's 'string of luck'. [raw/books/strategy/Certain to Win - The Strategy of John Boyd, Applied to - Richards, Chet, Richards, Chester W.pdf#p17]
- British General J.F.C. Fuller described Blitzkrieg as using 'mobility as a psychological weapon: not to kill but to move; not to move to kill but to move to terrify, to bewilder, to perplex, to cause consternation, doubt and confusion'. [raw/books/strategy/Certain to Win - The Strategy of John Boyd, Applied to - Richards, Chet, Richards, Chester W.pdf#p17]

## Inferences

- Inference: The German victory was not primarily technological but cultural—they evolved a way to cope with chaos that their opponents lacked, and they designed the strategy to create chaos.
- Inference: Liddell Hart's observation that 'French counter-movements were repeatedly thrown out of gear because their timing was too slow' confirms that decision tempo, not firepower, was the decisive factor.
