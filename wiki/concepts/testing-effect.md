# Testing Effect

## Summary

The testing effect is the finding that testing yourself on material leads to much better long-term retention than simply re-studying the same material. Students who repeatedly studied retained only half of what they learned after one week, while those who tested themselves retained over 85 percent. This benefit comes from error-driven learning that strengthens weak neural connections.

## Key facts

- Henry Roediger and Jeff Karpicke found that students who repeatedly studied retained only about half of what they initially learned after one week, while students who tested themselves retained over 85 percent. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p169]
- Testing exposes weaknesses—students who studied repeatedly were overconfident about retention because they were never challenged, while tested students had the humbling experience of struggling. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p169]
- Pre-testing—being asked questions before learning—also enhances subsequent learning by giving the brain a chance to struggle to come up with answers. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p171]
- The benefits of testing come from challenging yourself to pull up what you learned, which exposes weaknesses in cell assemblies so memories can be updated, strengthening useful connections and pruning interfering ones. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p171]
- Error-driven learning works when you eventually get close to the right answer or can rule out wrong answers—simply flailing without feedback does not produce learning benefits. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p172]
