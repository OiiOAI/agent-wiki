# Antithetic Ideas

## Summary

A mechanism Freud described in the generation of certain hysterical motor symptoms, particularly tics and involuntary movements. When a person forms an intention (e.g., to remain still), an antithetic idea (fear of making a noise) may arise and, in states of exhaustion or heightened suggestibility, become the stronger idea and actualize the very outcome the person sought to prevent. The resultant motor act becomes fixed as a somatic mnemic symbol of the whole scene.

## Key facts

- Freud described the mechanism of stammering and clacking as 'the putting into effect of antithetic ideas': an intention (to remain still) gives rise to an antithetic idea (fear of making a noise), which, in a state of exhaustion, proves stronger and actualizes the noise. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p621]
- Neurotics form greater numbers of antithetic ideas than normal people, or perceive them more easily, and regard them as of more importance. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p621]
- Freud assumed that her exhaustion was partial—it affected only her primary ego and did not result in a weakening of the antithetic idea. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p622]
- The discontinuous character of the tic was explained by the conflict between intention and antithetic idea (counterwill), which confined the antithetic idea to paths other than the habitual ones for innervating the muscular apparatus of speech. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p622]
