# Developmental Displacement

## Summary

Developmental displacement is a mechanism of brain evolution where changes in developmental timing or constraints cause neural circuits to be functionally repositioned. The blind mole rat provides a clear example where connections between visual and auditory brain centers show adaptive reorganization. More subtle variants of this process may be widespread across species. In the context of human evolution, Deacon suggests that upright posture shifted the brain stem and cerebellum more directly below the cerebrum, allowing posterior cortical areas (visual cortex) to fold more into the posterior midline, causing apparent lunate sulcus displacement.

## Key facts

- Developmental displacement approach to brain evolution was presented in 'Rethinking Mammalian Brain Evolution' (Deacon, 1990b). [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p514]
- Studies by Doron and Wollberg (1994) and Heil et al. (1991) on connections between visual and auditory centers in the blind mole rat provide easily recognizable examples of evolutionary displacement of neural circuits. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p514]
- More subtle variants of developmental displacement are likely very widespread, as suggested by studies on frog species in different habitats and miniaturization effects on amphibian brain structure. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p514]
- Deacon suggests that upright posture in australopithecines caused the brain stem and cerebellum to become oriented more directly below the cerebrum, allowing posterior cortical areas to fold more into the posterior midline.
