# Social Comparison

## Summary

Langer discusses social comparison—the tendency to evaluate oneself relative to others—as a source of stress and poor health. She argues that social comparisons are mindless because they assume a single correct perspective or criteria, and that much apparent 'memory loss' in the elderly is actually a difference in values (what one found interesting) rather than a memory deficit. Frequent social comparison is associated with envy, guilt, regret, defensiveness, unmet cravings, stress, and depression.

## Key facts

- Research by White, Yariv, Welch, and Langer found that frequent social comparing was related to envy, guilt, regret, defensiveness, lying, blaming, unmet cravings, stress, and depression. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p86]
- Langer challenges Festinger's theory that humans have an innate drive to make social comparisons, arguing there are many activities (e.g., brushing teeth) where people never wonder if they are better or worse than others. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p86-87]
- Much apparent memory loss attributed to aging may be a difference in values—what one found interesting—rather than a memory deficit, since older adults remember more about games from their youth than young adults. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p60]
- Langer argues that if people are doing the same behavior for different reasons (one person is flexible, another is inconsistent), social comparison between them makes no sense. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p88-89]
