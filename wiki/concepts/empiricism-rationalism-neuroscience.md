# Empiricism vs. Rationalism in Neuroscience

## Summary

The philosophical debate between empiricism (John Locke's view of the mind as a blank slate filled by experience) and rationalism (Immanuel Kant's view of innate templates of knowledge) found reconciliation in Kandel's studies of Aplysia. The anatomy of neural circuits represents Kantian a priori knowledge, while changes in synaptic strength reflect Lockean experience, with practice making perfect.

## Key facts

- Locke argued the mind is a blank slate eventually filled by experience, and the more often we encounter an idea, the more enduring its impact. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p214]
- Kant argued we are born with built-in templates of knowledge (a priori knowledge) that determine how sensory experience is received and interpreted. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p214]
- Kandel found in Aplysia's gill-withdrawal reflex that both views had merit: neural circuit anatomy is a priori, while synaptic strength changes reflect experience. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p214]
- The potential for many behaviors is built into the brain (genetic/developmental control), but environment and learning alter the effectiveness of preexisting pathways. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p213]

## Inferences

- Inference: Kandel's work demonstrated that neuroscience can experimentally address philosophical questions about the nature of mind that were previously intractable.
