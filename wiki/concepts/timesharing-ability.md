# Time-Sharing Ability

## Summary

Time-sharing (or multitasking) refers to an operator's ability to allocate attention among multiple subtasks in real-time tasks where the external environment changes continuously and some changes are beyond the operator's control. The research measured time-sharing ability through multilimb coordination tasks, which require monitoring multiple moving stimuli while coordinating hand and foot movements. Multilimb coordination predicted SA independently of working memory, suggesting time-sharing is a distinct ability related to attentional resource allocation.

## Key facts

- Time-sharing refers to a driver's ability to allocate attention among the four situation-assessment processes (navigation knowledge, local scene comprehension, spatial orientation, and vehicle status). [raw/books/strategy/Situation awareness _ analysis and measurement - edited by Mica R_ Endsley, Daniel J_ Garland.pdf#p262]
- Multilimb coordination tasks required time-sharing because participants had to view moving stimuli and coordinate movements of hand and feet. [raw/books/strategy/Situation awareness _ analysis and measurement - edited by Mica R_ Endsley, Daniel J_ Garland.pdf#p262]
- In the regression analysis, multilimb coordination made a significant unique contribution to explaining SA variance (part r = -.44), with g/WM not reaching significance (part r = .02). [raw/books/strategy/Situation awareness _ analysis and measurement - edited by Mica R_ Endsley, Daniel J_ Garland.pdf#p281]
- The substantial correlation between g/WM and multilimb coordination (r = -.52) suggests that multilimb coordination involves time-sharing as well, consistent with Baddeley's description of working memory as involving the management of cognitive resources. [raw/books/strategy/Situation awareness _ analysis and measurement - edited by Mica R_ Endsley, Daniel J_ Garland.pdf#p282]

## Inferences

- Inference: Multilimb coordination tests predicted SA because they tap time-sharing capacity rather than motor requirements, since SA tasks themselves require no motor control.
