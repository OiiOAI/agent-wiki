# Alarm Processing and SA

## Summary

Alarm systems often fail to enhance SA due to excessive alarm rates, high false alarm rates, and operators actively disabling alarms. The cry wolf syndrome develops when alarms frequently occur without real danger, causing operators to tune them out. Alarm processing involves integrating the alarm signal with other environmental information, interpreting meaning based on past experience and mental models, and deciding whether to respond. The relationship between workload (underload, normal, overload) and alarm effectiveness creates different trade-offs for false alarms versus missed alarms.

## Key facts

- During the Three-Mile Island accident, more than 500 alarm annunciators were activated in the first few minutes, making it nearly impossible to determine what was happening. [raw/books/strategy/Designing for Situation Awareness _ An Approach to - Jones, Debra G_; Endsley, Mica R.pdf#p147]
- A typical medical operating room may have 26 different audio alarms, less than half of which people can reliably identify. [raw/books/strategy/Designing for Situation Awareness _ An Approach to - Jones, Debra G_; Endsley, Mica R.pdf#p147]
- Pilots delay response to GPWS alarms as much as 73% of the time, seeking confirming evidence on displays or out the window. [raw/books/strategy/Designing for Situation Awareness _ An Approach to - Jones, Debra G_; Endsley, Mica R.pdf#p152]
- Under underload conditions, false alarms can increase vigilance and performance; under normal operations, false alarms should be minimized. [raw/books/strategy/Designing for Situation Awareness _ An Approach to - Jones, Debra G_; Endsley, Mica R.pdf#p155]
- The GPWS 'whoop, whoop, pull up' alert can be caused by at least seven different possible conditions, making correct attribution difficult without improved diagnosticity. [raw/books/strategy/Designing for Situation Awareness _ An Approach to - Jones, Debra G_; Endsley, Mica R.pdf#p154]
