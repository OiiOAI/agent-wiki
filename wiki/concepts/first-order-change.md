# First-Order Change

## Summary

First-order change refers to change that occurs within a system while the system itself remains unchanged. According to the authors, 'plus ça change, plus c'est la même chose' (the more things change, the more they stay the same) captures this phenomenon. First-order change involves changing from one state to another within the same frame of reference, like moving from one position to another within a dream. No matter what behaviors a person exhibits in a nightmare—running, hiding, screaming—none of these changes can end the nightmare itself.

## Key facts

- First-order change is change within a system where the system itself remains invariant. [raw/books/strategy/Change _ principles of problem formulation and problem - Paul Watzlawick, John H_ Weakland, Richard Fisch; foreword.epub#L107-L686]
- First-order change corresponds to Ashby's cybernetic concept of change from state to state (the machine's behavior). [raw/books/strategy/Change _ principles of problem formulation and problem - Paul Watzlawick, John H_ Weakland, Richard Fisch; foreword.epub#L107-L686]
- In first-order change, different behaviors are combined into different sequences but lead to identical outcomes, maintaining family homeostasis. [raw/books/strategy/Change _ principles of problem formulation and problem - Paul Watzlawick, John H_ Weakland, Richard Fisch; foreword.epub#L107-L686]
- The nine-dot problem exemplifies first-order change: attempting to solve the puzzle within the assumed square runs through all first-order possibilities without success. [raw/books/strategy/Change _ principles of problem formulation and problem - Paul Watzlawick, John H_ Weakland, Richard Fisch; foreword.epub#L107-L686]

## Inferences

- Inference: First-order change is the type of change that 'more of the same' solutions produce—they may change intensity or sequence but not the underlying system structure.
