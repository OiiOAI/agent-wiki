# Neuroeconomics

## Summary

Neuroeconomics is an interdisciplinary field that emerged in the late 1990s and early 2000s, combining approaches from economics, cognitive neuroscience, and psychology to study decision-making and the brain. The field arose from two convergent trends: behavioral economists seeking neural data to test theories of choice, and neuroscientists seeking theoretical frameworks (particularly from economics) to organize neural measurements of decision processes.

## Key facts

- Neuroeconomics has its origins in the neoclassical economic revolution of the 1930s and the birth of cognitive neuroscience during the 1990s. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p1]
- The first interdisciplinary meeting considered the founding moment of neuroeconomics was held at Princeton University in 2001, organized by Jonathan Cohen and Christina Paxson. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p7]
- Neuroeconomics offers a way to adjudicate between competing economic theories by forcing them to make predictions about underlying neural activity. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p4]
- Key early papers in neuroeconomics include Shizgal and Conover (1996) on neural computation of utility and Platt and Glimcher (1999) arguing for normative utility-based analysis of choice. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p8]
- Neuroeconomics emerged from the convergence of behavioral economics, which documented systematic violations of rational choice theory, and neuroscience, which identified neural substrates of value computation. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p209]
- Key neural structures in neuroeconomics include the ventromedial prefrontal cortex, amygdala, striatum (ventral and dorsal), anterior cingulate cortex, and insula. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p211]
- Neuroeconomics investigates how dopamine signals represent reward prediction errors in temporal difference learning models. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p172]
- The field studies how neural systems for 'theory of mind'—including anterior cingulate, medial frontal cortex, and temporal-parietal junction—are engaged in strategic games. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p199]

## Inferences

- Inference: Neuroeconomics fundamentally changes how economists and neuroscientists approach decision research by requiring theories to make commitments about neural mechanisms, not just behavioral outcomes.
- Inference: The convergence of economics and neuroscience represents a methodological shift where neither discipline's traditional tools alone could resolve debates about decision processes.
- Inference: Neuroeconomics provides a mechanistic foundation for behavioral economics findings by identifying how bounded rationality and social preferences arise from brain architecture.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Scholars within neuroeconomics still debate whether neuroscientific data will provide theory for economists or whether economic theory will provide structure for neuroscience.
