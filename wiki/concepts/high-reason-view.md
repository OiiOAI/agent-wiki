# High-Reason View of Decision Making

## Summary

The high-reason view, also called the common-sense view, assumes that optimal decision-making relies solely on formal logic without emotional interference. It posits that rational processing must be unencumbered by passion, with cost-benefit analysis of each scenario guided by subjective expected utility calculations. Damasio argues this view is cognitively overwhelming and fails to explain normal human decision-making.

## Key facts

- The high-reason view assumes that to obtain the best results, emotions must be kept out and rational processing must be unencumbered by passion. [raw/books/neuroscience/Descartes' error _ emotion, reason, and the human brain - Antonio R_ Damasio,pdf.pdf#p192]
- Purely rational calculation would be impractical because working memory has limited capacity and intermediate calculation steps would vanish before comparisons could be made. [raw/books/neuroscience/Descartes' error _ emotion, reason, and the human brain - Antonio R_ Damasio,pdf.pdf#p193]
- The flaws of the common-sense view include not only limited memory capacity but also weaknesses in reasoning strategies, including humans' defective use of probability theory and statistics. [raw/books/neuroscience/Descartes' error _ emotion, reason, and the human brain - Antonio R_ Damasio,pdf.pdf#p193]

## Inferences

- Inference: The high-reason view may describe how patients with prefrontal damage actually make decisions, rather than how normal individuals operate.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The extent to which paper-and-pencil aids fully compensate for cognitive limitations in pure rational reasoning is uncertain.
