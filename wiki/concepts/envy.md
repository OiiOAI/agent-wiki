# Envy

## Summary

Rawls defines envy as the propensity to view with hostility the greater good of others even though their being more fortunate than we are does not detract from our advantages. He distinguishes between general envy, particular envy, benign envy, and emulative envy.

## Key facts

- Envy proper is a form of rancor that tends to harm both its object and its subject, in contrast with benign envy which we freely express without ill will. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p471]
- Envy is not a moral feeling; no moral principle need be cited in its explanation. It is sufficient to say that the better situation of others catches our attention. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p471]
- Excusable envy is when the circumstances evoking envy are so compelling that given human beings as they are no one can reasonably be asked to overcome his rancorous feelings; when the lesser position as measured by the index of objective primary goods is so great as to wound self-respect. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p472]
- General envy is when the least advantaged envy those better situated for the kinds of goods and not for the particular objects they possess; particular envy is typical of rivalry and competition for the very same thing that others have won. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p470]
