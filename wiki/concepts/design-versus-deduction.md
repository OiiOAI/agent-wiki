# Design Versus Deduction in Strategy

## Summary

Strategy is not created through deduction from preset principles or frameworks but through design. Deduction, as formalized by Euclid, works when axioms and given alternatives exist—like calculating spacecraft trajectories. Design, by contrast, concerns how things ought to be to achieve human purposes. Herbert Simon distinguished between normal science (understanding the natural world) and design (creating things that don't yet exist). Strategy creation requires imagination, knowledge of other designs, and insight rather than algorithmic deduction.

## Key facts

- You cannot deduce a strategy from preset principles like Porter's Five Forces or Blue Ocean Strategy Canvas—they call attention to what might be important but cannot guide specific actions. [raw/books/strategy/The Crux - Richard Rumelt.pdf#p39]
- Herbert Simon won the 1978 Nobel Prize in Economics for observing that people don't maximize when making choices—their rationality is bounded. [raw/books/strategy/The Crux - Richard Rumelt.pdf#p40]
- Simon argued that design is concerned with how things ought to be to carry out human purposes, distinguishing it from deduction which derives conclusions from assumptions. [raw/books/strategy/The Crux - Richard Rumelt.pdf#p41]
- Strategy creation requires imagination and knowledge of other designs rather than algorithmic deduction from theory. [raw/books/strategy/The Crux - Richard Rumelt.pdf#p40]

## Inferences

- Inference: The inability to deduce strategy explains why frameworks and planning processes alone often fail to produce effective strategic responses.
