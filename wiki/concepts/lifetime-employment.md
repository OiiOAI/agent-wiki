# Lifetime Employment

## Summary

Lifetime employment (终身雇用制, shūshin koyō seido) is a Japanese HR practice where large companies commit to employing workers for their entire career, in exchange for worker loyalty. While often portrayed as rigid, the system contains significant flexibility through internal job rotations and retraining.

## Key facts

- One of the first Western observers to describe the Japanese postwar lifetime employment system was James C. Abegglen in The Japanese Factory (1958). [raw/books/sociology/Trust - Francis Fukuyama.pdf#p419]
- The Japanese labor market is more flexible than it might first seem: workers within a company are not rigidly tied to a particular job description. [raw/books/sociology/Trust - Francis Fukuyama.pdf#p419]
- Japanese engineers tend to take less pride in their engineering qualifications than in the particular company they work for. [raw/books/sociology/Trust - Francis Fukuyama.pdf#p419]
- Lifetime employment is a privilege only of large companies and is not nearly as widely practiced among small companies. [raw/books/sociology/Trust - Francis Fukuyama.pdf#p419]
- Companies have flexibility in moving workers around and taking responsibility for retraining, with layoff, retraining, and rehiring happening within the enterprise. [raw/books/sociology/Trust - Francis Fukuyama.pdf#p419]

## Inferences

- Inference: The concept originated in large Japanese manufacturing companies in the postwar period and gradually spread to other sectors.
- Inference: Professional identity in Japan is less tied to specific technical qualifications compared to Western countries, enabling easier job mobility within companies.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The exact scope of lifetime employment in the contemporary period versus the 1950s-1970s when Abegglen and Dore studied it.
