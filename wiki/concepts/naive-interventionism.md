# Naive Interventionism

## Summary

The urge to intervene and 'do something' without properly balancing benefits against iatrogenic harms. Naive interventionism fails to consider the break-even point between benefits and costs of action, instead assuming that intervention is inherently beneficial. This pattern manifests across medicine, economics, politics, and editing, where excessive correction produces worse outcomes than inaction.

## Key facts

- The tonsillectomy study of 1930s New York demonstrated naive interventionism: doctors recommended surgery to 174 of 389 children, then 99 of remaining 215, then 52 of remaining 116—with no consistent medical basis. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p150]
- Overintervention depletes mental and economic resources and is rarely available when truly needed, while underintervention characterizes emergencies where intervention is essential. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p159]
- Providing someone with a random numerical forecast increases their risk-taking even when they know the projections are random—demonstrating the iatrogenic harm of false precision. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p180]
- Non-naive interventionism advocates intervening to limit size, concentration, and speed—beneficial in reducing Black Swan risks—while avoiding intervention in systems that self-organize effectively. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p161]

## Inferences

- Inference: The failure to distinguish naive from non-naive interventionism leads to both overregulation of organic systems (like markets and ecology) and underregulation of dangerous concentrations (like 'too big to fail' institutions).
