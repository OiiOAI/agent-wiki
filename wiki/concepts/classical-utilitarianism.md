# Classical Utilitarianism

## Summary

Classical utilitarianism, as discussed by Rawls, is the doctrine that society is rightly ordered and just when its major institutions are arranged so as to achieve the greatest net balance of satisfaction summed over all individuals. Rawls contrasts this with justice as fairness, arguing that utilitarianism does not take seriously the distinction between persons. It extends the principle of rational choice for one man to society as a whole through the conception of an impartial spectator who organizes the desires of all persons into one coherent system. This allows inequalities imposed on some to be justified by greater aggregate satisfaction of others.

## Key facts

- Classical utilitarianism holds that society is rightly ordered and just when its major institutions are arranged so as to achieve the greatest net balance of satisfaction summed over all individuals. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p31]
- The principle of utility does not matter how the sum of satisfactions is distributed among individuals, just as it does not matter how one man distributes his satisfactions over time. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p34]
- Utilitarianism applies to society the principle of rational choice for one man through the impartial spectator who identifies with and experiences the desires of others as if they were his own. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p35]
- Utilitarianism does not take seriously the distinction between persons, as it conflates all systems of desires into one through the imaginative acts of the impartial sympathetic spectator. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p35]
- Utilitarianism allows that the violation of the liberty of a few might be made right by the greater good shared by many, since no distribution of satisfaction is better than another except for breaking ties. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p34]
- Rawls argues that persons in the original position would not agree to a principle that may require lesser life prospects for some simply for the sake of a greater sum of advantages enjoyed by others. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p24]
- The classical principle requires institutions be arranged to maximize the absolute weighted sum of the expectations of the relevant representative men, weighting each expectation by the number of persons in the corresponding position. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p156]
- The classical principle requires that social institutions should be arranged so that population grows indefinitely as long as average well-being falls slowly enough, since total utility increases with population. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p157]
- The surprising conclusion is that while the average principle is the ethic of a single rational individual with no aversion to risk, the classical doctrine is the ethic of perfect altruists. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p179]

## Inferences

- Inference: Rawls contends that utilitarianism is individualistic in holding that the good of society is constituted by advantages enjoyed by individuals, yet it is not individualistic in that it applies the principle of choice for one man to society.
- Inference: The difference in underlying assumptions between classical and average utilitarianism shows that despite similar practical consequences, these conceptions derive from markedly distinct motivational premises.
