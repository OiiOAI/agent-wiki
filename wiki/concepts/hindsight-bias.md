# Hindsight Bias

## Summary

Hindsight bias is the tendency to see past events as inevitable once their outcome is known. After learning an outcome, people feel they 'knew it all along' and reconstruct memories to fit this belief. This bias is especially pronounced when people can construct causal explanations making outcomes seem inevitable. Hindsight bias has important implications for medical diagnosis, legal proceedings, and learning from experience.

## Key facts

- Sports fans who were asked after Northwestern football games recalled giving the team a much greater chance of winning than fans who rated chances before the games. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p194]
- Students rated the likelihood of O.J. Simpson's conviction as lower after the jury returned a not-guilty verdict than they had two hours before. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p194]
- Hindsight bias was especially strong when experimenters provided a deterministic cause of an outcome but practically nonexistent when suggesting a chance cause. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p195]
- Hindsight bias occurs even when people are explicitly instructed to disregard the actual outcome of an event, as knowledge of the outcome becomes instantly integrated with other general knowledge. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p195]
- Mock jurors who heard inadmissible evidence were more likely to convict than those who didn't, even when explicitly instructed to disregard it. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p196]
- In Linda Carli's study, students who read a story ending in marriage falsely recognized expected precursors like 'Jack gave Barbara a ring,' while those who read the rape ending falsely recognized precursors like 'Jack was unpopular with women.'. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p197]

## Inferences

- Inference: Hindsight bias may reduce learning from experience—if we feel we always knew what would happen, we may be less inclined to profit from the lessons an event can teach us.
