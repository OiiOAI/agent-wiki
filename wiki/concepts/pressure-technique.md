# Pressure Technique

## Summary

A psychotherapeutic technique developed by Freud as an alternative to hypnotic somnambulism. Freud placed his hand on the patient's forehead and instructed them to report whatever came to mind (Einfall) at the moment pressure was relaxed. He found that this technique reliably evoked repressed memories and connections, and that patients who claimed 'nothing occurred to me' had actually perceived the material but rejected it as irrelevant or unwelcome. The technique was modeled on Bernheim's experiments demonstrating that memories seemingly forgotten in waking state could be revived by a mild word of command and a pressure.

## Key facts

- Freud developed the pressure technique after finding that his powers to induce somnambulism were severely limited and that the cathartic method could not be used without it. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p636]
- He placed his hand on the patient's forehead, said the patient would think of something under the pressure, and asked them to report what occurred to them at the moment of relaxing the pressure. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p637]
- Freud found that patients who said 'nothing occurred to me' had actually perceived the material but had rejected it on the ground that it was unserviceable and an irrelevant interruption. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p638]
- The technique was also used to recover forgotten numbers and dates, relying on the principle that recognizing something is a lighter task for memory than thinking of it spontaneously. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p639]
- Freud concluded that experiences which have played an important pathogenic part are accurately retained in the patient's memory even when they seem to be forgotten. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p639]
- Freud developed a technique where he applied pressure to the patient's forehead while instructing them to report whatever picture or idea occurred to them during the pressure. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p783]
- The advantage of the procedure lies in dissociating the patient's attention from conscious searching and reflecting, from everything on which he can employ his will. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p784]
- Freud stated he could no longer do without this procedure and that it invariably achieved its aim. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p783]
- What emerges under pressure is often not the actual pathogenic recollection but an intermediate link in the chain of associations between the starting point and the target memory. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p785]

## Inferences

- Inference: The pressure technique represented a significant technical innovation that allowed Freud to conduct psychical analysis without relying on deep hypnosis, making the cathartic method more widely applicable.
- Inference: The pressure technique represents a practical application of the theoretical insight that repressed material remains accessible but blocked by conscious will—the technique temporarily bypasses this blockage.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: There are slight inconsistencies in how Freud described the timing—when the patient should experience the idea (at pressure, at relaxation of pressure, or continuously during pressure)—suggesting the technique evolved through clinical use.
