# Semantic Priming in Sleep Research

## Summary

Semantic priming is a cognitive test that measures how quickly participants respond to target words when preceded by related or unrelated prime words. Bob Stickgold used this test to measure the brain's preference for associations during REM versus non-REM sleep. The studies showed that during wakefulness, strong primes produce three times as much priming as weak primes, but during REM sleep, this reverses dramatically—priming from strongly related words drops by 90 percent while that from weak primes increases more than twofold, demonstrating that the sleeping brain preferentially activates weak associations.

## Key facts

- In semantic priming tests during wakefulness, strong primes like 'right' produce three times as much priming as weak primes like 'thief'. [raw/books/neuroscience/When Brains Dream - Antonio Zadra.pdf#p114]
- During REM sleep, priming produced by strongly related words drops by 90 percent while that produced by weak primes increases more than twofold. [raw/books/neuroscience/When Brains Dream - Antonio Zadra.pdf#p116]
- When participants were awakened from REM sleep, their brains were activating weakly related words eight times more effectively than strongly related words. [raw/books/neuroscience/When Brains Dream - Antonio Zadra.pdf#p116]
- During non-REM sleep, weak primes had no effect at all, with strong primes once again producing the biggest effect. [raw/books/neuroscience/When Brains Dream - Antonio Zadra.pdf#p124]
- This difference between REM and non-REM sleep is likely due to noradrenaline being shut off during REM sleep, which focuses attention and prevents wandering among weak associations. [raw/books/neuroscience/When Brains Dream - Antonio Zadra.pdf#p124]
