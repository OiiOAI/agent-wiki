# Confirmation Bias

## Summary

Confirmation bias is the tendency to seek out and interpret evidence in ways that confirm pre-existing beliefs while neglecting or dismissing contradictory evidence. Haidt presents research showing that this bias underlies much of human reasoning, including moral reasoning. Studies by Perkins found that IQ predicted the number of arguments people generated for their own side but not for the opposing side. Haidt argues this confirms the Glauconian view that reasoning evolved to serve social and strategic goals (appearing right, winning arguments) rather than epistemic goals (discovering truth).

## Key facts

- Wason's 2-4-6 problem demonstrated that people rarely test hypotheses by proposing triplets that would disconfirm their theory, even though this would be the logical approach to finding the rule. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p95-96]
- Deanna Kuhn found that both children and adults interpreting food-sickness evidence usually concluded the evidence proved their initial hunch correct, ignoring stronger evidence that contradicted their theory. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p96]
- Perkins found that IQ was the biggest predictor of number of my-side arguments generated, but did not predict ability to find reasons on the other side, leading him to conclude 'people invest their IQ in buttressing their own case rather than exploring the entire issue more fully.'. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p97]
- Tetlock's research found that accountability increased exploratory thought only when three conditions applied: decision makers learned they'd be accountable before forming opinions, the audience's views were unknown, and the audience was believed to value accuracy. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p92]
- Confirmation bias causes people to happen to be surrounded by people who think alike, read publications that confirm prior beliefs, and fail to notice dis-confirming facts. [raw/books/productivity/How to Take Smart Notes_ One Simple Technique to Boost - Sönke Ahrens.pdf#p76]
- Raymond Nickerson stated that if one had to identify a single problematic aspect of human reasoning, confirmation bias 'would have to be among the candidates for consideration.'. [raw/books/productivity/How to Take Smart Notes_ One Simple Technique to Boost - Sönke Ahrens.pdf#p76]
- Charles Darwin used the technique of writing down dis-confirming facts immediately in a dedicated record, elaborating on them to prevent them from escaping memory. [raw/books/productivity/How to Take Smart Notes_ One Simple Technique to Boost - Sönke Ahrens.pdf#p76]
- The linear writing model (decide hypothesis first, then research) structurally amplifies confirmation bias by priming perception in one direction before collecting evidence. [raw/books/productivity/How to Take Smart Notes_ One Simple Technique to Boost - Sönke Ahrens.pdf#p77]
- In the slip-box method, dis-confirming data becomes attractive because it opens more possible connections and discussions, reversing the incentive away from confirmation. [raw/books/productivity/How to Take Smart Notes_ One Simple Technique to Boost - Sönke Ahrens.pdf#p78]
- If insight becomes a threat to academic or writing success, the workflow is designed incorrectly. [raw/books/productivity/How to Take Smart Notes_ One Simple Technique to Boost - Sönke Ahrens.pdf#p77]

## Inferences

- Inference: The prevalence of confirmation bias supports Glaucon's view that appearance and reputation matter more than truth, and that the function of reasoning is persuasion rather than discovery.
