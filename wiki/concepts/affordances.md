# Affordances

## Summary

Affordances are the possibilities for action that the environment offers to an animal. They are relational properties that exist at the interface between the environment and the animal, being neither purely objective (physical) nor purely subjective (mental). For example, a surface affords support to an animal of appropriate size and locomotion capability; water affords swimming to some animals but not to humans. The concept was developed through multiple iterations and became Gibson's most widely cited contribution.

## Key facts

- Affordances are neither objective nor subjective; they point both ways to the objective environment and to the subject animal. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p26]
- The surface of water affords locomotion to spiders of the dolomedes genus but not to humans. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p27]
- Chapter 8 'The Theory of Affordances' went through multiple drafts including 'purple perils' and a 1977 conference paper. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p19]
- The concept of affordance has 'gone viral' largely through Donald Norman's promotion in 'The Psychology of Everyday Things' (1988). [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p27]

## Inferences

- Inference: Affordances are real, well-defined properties that animals can directly perceive, unlike abstract spatial relationships.
- Inference: William H. Warren's research on stair climbing demonstrated that affordances are scaled relative to individual perceivers—maximum step height judged climbable corresponded to the same ratio of leg length to step riser height for both tall and short people.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The extent to which Norman's interpretation of affordances aligns with Gibson's original meaning remains debated.
