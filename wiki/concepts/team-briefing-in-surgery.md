# Team Briefing in Surgery

## Summary

Preoperative team huddles in which surgical team members discuss the case, expected duration, blood loss risks, and patient-specific concerns.

## Key facts

- The team briefing component of surgical checklists requires team members to stop and take a moment to talk with one another before proceeding—about expected operation duration, anticipated blood loss, patient risks, and concerns the team should know about. [raw/books/strategy/The checklist manifesto _ how to get things right - Atul Gawande.pdf#p77]
- The Johns Hopkins checklist explicitly required staff to introduce themselves by name and role before starting operations with new teams. [raw/books/strategy/The checklist manifesto _ how to get things right - Atul Gawande.pdf#p81]
- Psychology studies showed that people who do not know one another's names do not work together nearly as well as those who do; in operating rooms, about half the time staff did not know one another's names. [raw/books/strategy/The checklist manifesto _ how to get things right - Atul Gawande.pdf#p81]
- Researchers observed that when nurses were given a chance to say their names and mention concerns at the beginning of a case, they were more likely to note problems and offer solutions—this was termed the 'activation phenomenon.'. [raw/books/strategy/The checklist manifesto _ how to get things right - Atul Gawande.pdf#p81]
- At Johns Hopkins, after three months of using the checklist, the number of team members reporting that they 'functioned as a well-coordinated team' leapt from 68% to 92%. [raw/books/strategy/The checklist manifesto _ how to get things right - Atul Gawande.pdf#p109]

## Inferences

- Inference: The activation phenomenon suggests that simply creating a structured opportunity to speak at the start of a case increases subsequent engagement and willingness to raise concerns.
