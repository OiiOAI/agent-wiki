# Stiglitz Syndrome

## Summary

The Stiglitz syndrome describes a combination of remarkable analytical skills, blindness to fragility, selective memory, and absence of skin in the game that allows experts to contribute to a problem's cause while convincing themselves (and sometimes others) that they predicted and warned against it. Named after Joseph Stiglitz, who co-authored assurances that Fannie Mae risks were 'effectively zero' before its collapse cost taxpayers hundreds of billions, then later claimed to have predicted the 2008 crisis. Taleb identifies this as a form of cherry-picking so severe that the perpetrator is not aware of what he is doing—a combination of fragilista behavior with ex post rationalization that makes experts personally antifragile at society's expense.

## Key facts

- Stiglitz and colleagues assessed that 'the risk to the government from a potential default on GSE debt is effectively zero' and that default probability was 'so small that it is difficult to detect.'. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p480]
- The Stiglitz syndrome = fragilista (with good intentions) + ex post cherry-picking. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p481]
- Postdictors are antifragile: the more volatility and uncertainty, the higher the illusion of their intelligence, as they cherry-pick past statements showing they 'predicted' events while discarding mispredictions. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p478]
- Taleb states: 'Had Stiglitz been a businessman with his own money on the line, he would have blown up, terminated. Or had he been in nature, his genes would have been made extinct.'. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p480]
- Peter Orszag, a co-author of the Fannie Mae report, later obtained a position in the Obama administration, then became vice chairman of Citibank. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p500]

## Inferences

- Inference: The Stiglitz syndrome explains why economists continue to contribute to systemic fragility—they face no evolutionary or financial pressure to correct their misunderstanding of rare events.
- Inference: This pattern is more dangerous than outright fraud because the perpetrator genuinely believes they predicted the problem, making self-correction impossible.
