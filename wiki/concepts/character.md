# Character

## Summary

Character refers to morally evaluated traits—virtues like honesty, fairness, courage, and patience, or their opposite vices—that distinguish it from personality traits, which denote differences between individuals that are more or less neutral. Character traits are not simply given but must be actively developed through practice, reinforcement, and choice. The development of character is rooted in our social nature as an obligately cooperative species, where virtues emerged as prosocial traits that benefit everyone.

## Key facts

- Character traits are defined as morally better or worse, categorized as virtues or vices, including honesty, fairness, courage, humility, generosity, steadfastness, loyalty, integrity, prudence, patience, temperance, and selflessness. [raw/books/neuroscience/Free Agents _ How Evolution Gave Us Free Will - Kevin J_ Mitchell.pdf#p258]
- Virtues are essentially prosocial traits—they are good or bad for everyone, not just good or bad for the individual in absolute terms. [raw/books/neuroscience/Free Agents _ How Evolution Gave Us Free Will - Kevin J_ Mitchell.pdf#p258]
- Humans survived and thrived by working together, requiring cooperation, concerted action, resource sharing, and division of labor, which shaped the evolution of virtues. [raw/books/neuroscience/Free Agents _ How Evolution Gave Us Free Will - Kevin J_ Mitchell.pdf#p258]
- Character traits represent mature habits and skills and practices more than just predispositions, and must be actively developed. [raw/books/neuroscience/Free Agents _ How Evolution Gave Us Free Will - Kevin J_ Mitchell.pdf#p260]
- Cicero identified four sources of character: human nature generally, individual natures, events and experiences, and the accumulating effects of our own choices. [raw/books/neuroscience/Free Agents _ How Evolution Gave Us Free Will - Kevin J_ Mitchell.pdf#p260]

## Inferences

- Inference: The moral evaluation inherent in character traits arose because cooperation created opportunities for freeloading, leading to cognitive systems that monitor and enforce prosocial norms.
- Inference: Praise and blame exist because character emerges over time—if personality were simply fixed, there would be no point in praising or blaming people.
