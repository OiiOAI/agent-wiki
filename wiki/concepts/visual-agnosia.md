# Visual Agnosia

## Summary

Visual agnosia is a loss in the ability to recognize objects despite intact visual perception. There are two major categories: apperceptive agnosia (inability to construct sensory representations of visual stimuli) and associative agnosia (inability to associate sensory representations with meaning, function, or utility). Apperceptive agnosia results from damage to the posterior inferior temporal cortex, while associative agnosia results from damage to the anterior inferior temporal cortex.

## Key facts

- Visual agnosia is a condition in which patients cannot recognize visual objects despite relatively normal visual perception. [raw/books/neuroscience/Eric Kandel PRINCIPLES OF NEURAL SCIENCE Sixth Edition - Eric R Kandel; John Koester; Sarah Mack; Steven Siegelbaum.pdf#p614]
- Apperceptive agnosia results from disruption of the first stage of object recognition: integration of visual features into sensory representations. [raw/books/neuroscience/Eric Kandel PRINCIPLES OF NEURAL SCIENCE Sixth Edition - Eric R Kandel; John Koester; Sarah Mack; Steven Siegelbaum.pdf#p614]
- Associative agnosia results from disruption of the second stage of object recognition: association of the sensory representation with knowledge of the object's meaning or function. [raw/books/neuroscience/Eric Kandel PRINCIPLES OF NEURAL SCIENCE Sixth Edition - Eric R Kandel; John Koester; Sarah Mack; Steven Siegelbaum.pdf#p614]
- Apperceptive agnosia is most common following damage to the posterior inferior temporal cortex. [raw/books/neuroscience/Eric Kandel PRINCIPLES OF NEURAL SCIENCE Sixth Edition - Eric R Kandel; John Koester; Sarah Mack; Steven Siegelbaum.pdf#p614]
- Associative agnosia is more common following damage to the anterior inferior temporal cortex. [raw/books/neuroscience/Eric Kandel PRINCIPLES OF NEURAL SCIENCE Sixth Edition - Eric R Kandel; John Koester; Sarah Mack; Steven Siegelbaum.pdf#p614]
- Visual agnosia was first described in the late 19th century by Sanger Brown and Edward Schäfer, who found that lesions of the temporal lobe in primates resulted in loss of object recognition. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p675]
- The term visual agnosia was coined by Sigmund Freud to replace the earlier term psychic blindness. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p675]
- With apperceptive agnosia, the ability to match or copy complex visual shapes or objects is impaired due to disruption of integration of visual features into sensory representations. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p675]
- With associative agnosia, patients can match or copy complex objects but cannot identify them, due to disruption of associating sensory representations with meaning. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p675]
- Apperceptive agnosia most commonly follows damage to the posterior inferior temporal cortex, whereas associative agnosia follows damage to the anterior inferior temporal cortex. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p676]
- Prosopagnosia patients can identify a face as a face and recognize its parts, but cannot identify a particular face as belonging to a specific person. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p676]
- Other category-specific agnosias have been reported for living things, fruits, vegetables, tools, or animals, in addition to faces. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p676]

## Inferences

- Inference: The two-stage model of object recognition (integration of features followed by association with meaning) is supported by the distinct behavioral profiles of apperceptive and associative agnosia.
