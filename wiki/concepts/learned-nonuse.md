# Learned Nonuse

## Summary

Learned nonuse is a phenomenon identified by Edward Taub where stroke patients stop using their affected limbs even when recovery is possible. It develops during the period of 'cortical shock' following a stroke, when attempts to move the affected limb fail repeatedly. Without positive reinforcement, patients learn to rely on their good limbs and the motor programs for the affected limb weaken and atrophy. Taub believed that even years after a stroke, motor programs for movement remain present in the nervous system, masked by learned nonuse.

## Key facts

- Taub proposed that the reason a monkey didn't use its arm after a single limb was deafferented was because it had learned not to use it in the period right after the operation when the spinal cord was still in 'spinal shock.'. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p149]
- Spinal shock can last from two to six months, during which an animal tries to move its affected arm and fails many times. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p149]
- Without positive reinforcement during spinal shock, the animal gives up and uses its good arm, and the motor map for the affected arm begins to weaken and atrophy. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p149]
- Taub reasoned that stroke patients with extensive brain damage might be suffering from learned nonuse, masking their ability to recover. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p150]
- Taub believed that even after a stroke, motor programs for movement were present in the nervous system, and overcoming learned nonuse would unmask this motor capacity. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p150]

## Inferences

- Inference: Learned nonuse explains why some stroke patients show limited recovery with conventional therapy—they are not attempting to use affected limbs due to prior failed attempts.
