# Efference Copy

## Summary

An efference copy is an internal signal representing a copy of the motor command sent to effectors. It is used by the nervous system for state estimation, forward internal models, and predicting the sensory consequences of movement. Efference copies allow the brain to distinguish self-generated sensory signals from externally generated signals and to make predictive corrections during movement.

## Key facts

- State estimation uses forward internal models with efference copies of motor commands combined with sensory feedback to estimate current body and environment state. [raw/books/neuroscience/Eric Kandel PRINCIPLES OF NEURAL SCIENCE Sixth Edition - Eric R Kandel; John Koester; Sarah Mack; Steven Siegelbaum.pdf#p865]
- Premovement activity in parietal cortex neurons may be driven by efference copies of motor commands transmitted via reciprocal cortico-cortical connections. [raw/books/neuroscience/Eric Kandel PRINCIPLES OF NEURAL SCIENCE Sixth Edition - Eric R Kandel; John Koester; Sarah Mack; Steven Siegelbaum.pdf#p872]
- Efference copies enable animals to distinguish reafference (sensory input caused by own actions) from exafference (sensory input from external sources). [raw/books/neuroscience/Other Minds_ The Octopus, the Sea, and the - Peter Godfrey - Smith.pdf#p122]
- Efference copies allow comparison between intended and actual action outcomes, enabling error detection in motor control. [raw/books/neuroscience/Other Minds_ The Octopus, the Sea, and the - Peter Godfrey - Smith.pdf#p116]
- In speech, efference copies enable comparison of spoken words to inner images, allowing detection of speech errors. [raw/books/neuroscience/Other Minds_ The Octopus, the Sea, and the - Peter Godfrey - Smith.pdf#p116]
- Efference copies in human speech gave rise to inner speech through a process where quasi-heard sentences were composed without external expression. [raw/books/neuroscience/Other Minds_ The Octopus, the Sea, and the - Peter Godfrey - Smith.pdf#p116]

## Inferences

- Inference: Cephalopod skin displays are unlikely to involve efference copies in the same way human speech does, as cephalopods cannot perceive their own skin patterns visually.
