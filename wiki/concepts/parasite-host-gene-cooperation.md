# Parasite-Host Gene Cooperation

## Summary

A parasite's genes will cooperate with host genes to the extent that they share the same exit route to future generations. If parasite genes pass via the host's eggs or sperm, they will help the host survive and reproduce. If not, they will tend to harm the host. This explains the spectrum from parasitism to mutualism observed in nature, as with algae in hydra species.

## Key facts

- The most important question to ask about any parasite is: Are its genes transmitted to future generations via the same vehicles as the host's genes? If they are not, the parasite will damage the host; if they are, the parasite will help the host. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p284]
- In Hydra vulgaris and Hydra attenuata, algae are real parasites making them ill, but in Chlorohydra viridissima the algae are never absent and provide oxygen—the algae transmit themselves via the hydra's egg in the cooperative case but not in the parasitic ones. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p285]
- Ambrosia beetles are parasitized by bacteria that use the host's eggs as transport to new hosts; the bacteria 'prick the unfertilized eggs into action' to develop into male beetles, demonstrating cooperation because both share the same genetic destiny. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p285]

## Inferences

- Inference: Mitochondria and chloroplasts may have evolved from parasites whose genes eventually shared the same germ-line exit as host genes.
