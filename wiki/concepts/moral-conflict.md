# Moral Conflict

## Summary

Moral conflict is one of three categories of conflict Boyd identified, dealing with psychological factors that determine cohesion and fighting will. Boyd derived insights from Hermann Balck on leadership creating moral strength and from Cyril Falls on how moral effects were achieved through air attacks in WWI. Moral conflict addresses how to generate fear, anxiety, and alienation in adversaries while building moral bonds within one's own forces.

## Key facts

- Moral strength represents mental capacity to overcome menace, uncertainty, and mistrust. [raw/books/strategy/ScienceStrategyWar_Osinga.pdf#p225]
- Moral victory is the triumph of courage, confidence, and esprit over fear, anxiety, and alienation when confronted by menace, uncertainty, and mistrust. [raw/books/strategy/ScienceStrategyWar_Osinga.pdf#p225]
- The essence of moral conflict involves creating menace (impressions of danger), uncertainty (ambiguous events), and mistrust (atmosphere of doubt) to surface fear, anxiety, and alienation in adversaries. [raw/books/strategy/ScienceStrategyWar_Osinga.pdf#p226]
- Counterweights to negative moral factors are initiative (internal drive to act), adaptability (power to adjust), and harmony (interaction of disconnected events). [raw/books/strategy/ScienceStrategyWar_Osinga.pdf#p226]
- Balck emphasized that cohesion during combat relied more on moral superiority than material superiority, requiring leaders to create bonds based on trust and lead by example. [raw/books/strategy/ScienceStrategyWar_Osinga.pdf#p224]
- Cyril Falls documented that German dive-bomber successes in Poland, Belgium, and France were won for the most part by moral rather than material effect. [raw/books/strategy/ScienceStrategyWar_Osinga.pdf#p225]

## Inferences

- Inference: Boyd's moral conflict framework synthesizes Balck's practical military leadership insights with Falls' historical analysis to create a theoretical structure for understanding and manipulating the psychological dimensions of warfare.
