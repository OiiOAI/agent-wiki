# Anterograde Amnesia

## Summary

Anterograde amnesia is the inability to form new declarative memories following brain injury or surgery, as demonstrated in Henry Molaison's case after his 1953 bilateral medial temporal lobe operation. Patients with this condition cannot consciously remember new facts or events while typically retaining memories from before the injury.

## Key facts

- Henry Molaison's anterograde amnesia prevented him from forming new declarative memories for 55 years after his surgery. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p100]
- Anterograde amnesia affects the conscious, declarative memory system while often leaving nondeclarative memory abilities intact. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p117]
- Henry could not learn new vocabulary words, the name of the current president, or the faces of people he encountered at the CRC. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p117]
- The condition results from damage to medial temporal-lobe structures including the hippocampus and surrounding cortices. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p81]

## Inferences

- Inference: Anterograde amnesia selectively impairs the hippocampus-dependent memory system while sparing other brain circuits.
