# Self-Analysis

## Summary

Freud undertook self-analysis to understand his own neurosis and to illuminate the general problem of neuroses. Through analysing his dreams, he discovered key elements of his own childhood including his nurse's influence, his rivalry with his younger brother, and ultimately the Oedipus complex. He concluded that genuine self-analysis is impossible without knowledge obtained objectively from others.

## Key facts

- Freud's self-analysis proceeded in dreams and presented him with the most valuable inferences and clues about the origins of his own neurosis. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p288]
- Through self-analysis, Freud traced his libidinal development including an early stirring towards his mother during a journey from Leipzig to Vienna. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p288]
- Freud concluded that genuine self-analysis is impossible; otherwise there would be no neurotic illness, since one can only analyse oneself with the help of knowledge obtained objectively. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p297]
- The old nurse who told Freud about God Almighty and Hell was identified by Freud as the prime originator of his troubles, later confirmed through enquiry of his mother. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p288]

## Inferences

- Inference: Freud's self-analysis, though incomplete, provided the empirical foundation for his theoretical formulations about childhood sexuality and the Oedipus complex.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Freud noted that his self-analysis was interrupted and that he could only proceed with difficulty because patients holding up his work also held up his self-analysis.
