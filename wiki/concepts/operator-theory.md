# Operator Theory

## Summary

The operator theory, developed by Alvaro Pascual-Leone based on Roy Hamilton's collaboration, proposes that the brain is organized not by sensory modality but by specific operators that process abstract information. Unlike traditional localizationist views where the 'visual cortex' processes only vision, operators process information like spatial relationships, movement, and shapes that can be derived from multiple senses. Selection of operators follows a Darwinian competition similar to Gerald Edelman's neuronal group selection, where the most able group of neurons is recruited for any given task.

## Key facts

- Operator theory proposes that the brain is organized by specific operators processing abstract information rather than systems dedicated to single sensory modalities. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p218]
- Spatial relationships, movement, and shapes are processed by several senses, allowing operators to handle input from multiple sources. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p218]
- The theory draws on Gerald Edelman's 1987 theory of neuronal group selection, proposing neural Darwinism where operators compete to most effectively process signals. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p218]
- The rapid brain reorganization seen within days is possible because operators are not committed to specific senses but compete for processing tasks. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p217]

## Inferences

- Inference: The operator theory provides a framework for understanding how learning a new skill can recruit operators from other domains, potentially explaining why blind people can use visual cortex for Braille reading.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The exact mechanism of how operators are selected and compete remains theoretical.
