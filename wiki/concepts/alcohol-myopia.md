# Alcohol Myopia

## Summary

Alcohol myopia theory, developed by psychologists Claude Steele and Robert Josephs, proposes that alcohol's principal effect is to narrow mental fields of vision, making immediate aspects of experience disproportionately influential while fading longer-term considerations. Gladwell applies this to sexual assault cases like Brock Turner/Emily Doe, arguing that alcohol transforms rather than reveals the self, and that drunk encounters between strangers cannot constitute meaningful consent.

## Key facts

- Alcohol myopia creates 'a state of shortsightedness in which superficially understood, immediate aspects of experience have a disproportionate influence on behavior'. [raw/books/psychology/Talking to Strangers - Gladwell, Malcolm.pdf#p120]
- The Camba of Bolivia drank 180-proof rum at weekend parties with no social pathology—no arguments, disputes, or aggression. [raw/books/psychology/Talking to Strangers - Gladwell, Malcolm.pdf#p119]
- Alcohol affects the frontal lobes (planning), reward centers (euphoria), amygdala (threat detection), and cerebellum (coordination). [raw/books/psychology/Talking to Strangers - Gladwell, Malcolm.pdf#p124]
- At blood-alcohol levels around 0.08, the hippocampus begins to struggle, causing memory gaps; at 0.15, complete blackouts occur. [raw/books/psychology/Talking to Strangers - Gladwell, Malcolm.pdf#p125]
- Emily Doe's blood-alcohol was 0.249—three times the legal limit; Brock Turner's was 0.171. [raw/books/psychology/Talking to Strangers - Gladwell, Malcolm.pdf#p132]
- At Duke University, 50% of drinkers had suffered a blackout at some point, 40% in the past year. [raw/books/psychology/Talking to Strangers - Gladwell, Malcolm.pdf#p127]

## Inferences

- Inference: Sexual assault at parties is facilitated by alcohol myopia because both parties lose the capacity to properly assess consent—immediate gratification overwhelms longer-term judgment
