# Present-Past Experience Dichotomy

## Summary

Gibson argues that the traditional distinction between present experience and past experience is a false dichotomy, arising from linguistic categories (present tense vs. past tense verbs) rather than from perceptual reality. He contends there is no dividing line between perceiving and remembering, and that a perception does not become a memory after a certain length of time. This critique underlies his argument that memory is unnecessary as a theoretical construct in perceptual theory.

## Key facts

- The stream of experience does not consist of an instantaneous present and a linear past receding into the distance; it is not a 'traveling razor's edge' dividing the past from the future. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p274]
- A special sense impression clearly ceases when the sensory excitation ends, but a perception does not. A perception does not become a memory after a certain length of time. A perception, in fact, does not have an end. Perceiving goes on. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p274]
- Gibson identifies what he calls the 'muddle of memory'—assumptions that memory is images or pictures of the past, that memories accumulate and are stored, that memory is physiological consisting of engrams or traces, and that any effect of the past on the present is memory. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p274]

## Inferences

- Inference: Gibson's rejection of the present/past dichotomy implies that traditional memory research paradigms may be fundamentally misframed, since the phenomena they study may not exist as traditionally conceived.
