# Altruistic punishment

## Summary

Altruistic punishment refers to costly punishment of norm violators that benefits others but not the punisher. In humans, this activates reward circuitries. Research in non-human primates shows limited evidence for altruistic punishment, though chimpanzees have been shown to engage in retaliation against direct harm and may punish those who violate social expectations.

## Key facts

- In humans, altruistic punishment activates reward circuitries usually engaged in processing primary rewards, which may explain why people are motivated to engage in altruistic punishment even when costly. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p252]
- Jensen et al. (2007a) found that chimpanzees were most likely to collapse a food platform when another chimpanzee, rather than an experimenter, took food from them, indicating victim-specific retaliation. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p273]
- In a reduced ultimatum game, Jensen et al. (2007b) found that chimpanzees in the responder role rarely rejected any non-zero offers, showing little evidence of altruistic punishment. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p273]
- Singer et al. (2006) found that men (but not women) showed increased activation in reward areas (nucleus accumbens) correlated with desire for revenge when seeing an unfair player in pain. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p252]
- In a Trust Game, investors could punish nonreciprocators at a ratio of 2:1, and activation in the caudate nucleus was greater when punishment was real versus symbolic. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroscience of Decision Making - Mandel, David R.,Vartanian, Oshin.pdf#p325]
- Some people find it rewarding to see unfair people punished, implicating the nucleus accumbens and left ventral striatum, but this does not entail that people will find punishing in unrelated third-party transactions rewarding. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroscience of Decision Making - Mandel, David R.,Vartanian, Oshin.pdf#p343]
- Third-party punishment is sensitive to social context and whether the third-party punisher is observed. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroscience of Decision Making - Mandel, David R.,Vartanian, Oshin.pdf#p344]

## Inferences

- Inference: The distinction between retaliation (individual benefit) and altruistic punishment (benefits group) is evolutionarily important, as only the latter requires multi-level selection.
- Inference: The absence of clear altruistic punishment in non-human primates may explain why human cooperation can scale to larger groups.
- Inference: The caudate nucleus activation during altruistic punishment suggests the brain's reward system is involved in motivating costly punishment behavior.
- Inference: The evidence that punishment observed is motivated by altruism is weak; it may be plausibly attributed to desire for revenge at being treated unfairly.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The extent to which chimpanzee victim-specific retaliation represents true altruistic punishment versus self-interested retaliation requires clarification.
- Uncertain: Whether third-party punishment in unrelated transactions is motivated by altruism or revenge remains an open question.
