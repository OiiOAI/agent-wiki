# The Reid Technique

## Summary

The Reid Technique is a widely-used method for police interrogation developed by Fred E. Inbau and John E. Reid in the 1940s–1950s. It teaches interrogators to watch for specific nonverbal cues—eye contact avoidance, facial expression changes, hand movements, and foot bouncing—as signs of deception. Gladwell, drawing on research by criminologist Richard R. Johnson, demonstrates that these cues have no empirical support and that the technique's assumptions are 'nonsense.'

## Key facts

- The Reid Technique teaches that people who are lying look away, and that truthful suspects maintain eye contact. [raw/books/psychology/Talking to Strangers - Gladwell, Malcolm.pdf#p230]
- The technique instructs that 'variation of expressions may be suggestive of untruthfulness, where the lack of such a variation may be suggestive of truthfulness.'. [raw/books/psychology/Talking to Strangers - Gladwell, Malcolm.pdf#p231]
- Richard R. Johnson's analysis of 452 segments from the TV show 'Cops' found that black people who are perfectly innocent are actually less likely to look police in the eye than black people who are suspected of a crime. [raw/books/psychology/Talking to Strangers - Gladwell, Malcolm.pdf#p231]
- The Cops data showed that the people who smile the most are innocent African Americans, while the people who smile the least are Hispanic suspects—contrary to what the Reid Technique teaches. [raw/books/psychology/Talking to Strangers - Gladwell, Malcolm.pdf#p231]
- African American suspects speak more fluidly than innocent Hispanics, who hem and haw nervously—again opposite to what Reid interprets as signs of deception. [raw/books/psychology/Talking to Strangers - Gladwell, Malcolm.pdf#p231]
- Research by Saul Kassin and Christina Fong found that training in Reid-based verbal and nonverbal cues did not improve judgment accuracy and that trained participants were less accurate than naive participants, yet more confident in their often erroneous judgments. [raw/books/psychology/Talking to Strangers - Gladwell, Malcolm.pdf#p233]

## Inferences

- Inference: The Reid Technique may lead to racial bias in law enforcement because innocent Black individuals display behaviors the technique interprets as deceptive (less eye contact).
- Inference: The technique creates overconfidence in interrogators despite poor accuracy, potentially leading to wrongful convictions.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The extent to which police departments continue to use the Reid Technique despite scientific evidence against it.
