# Haystack Searches

## Summary

A 'haystack search' refers to looking for a rare event or item among a large population where the base rate of the target is very low. Gladwell uses mammography as an example: less than 0.5% of women who get mammograms actually have cancer, yet radiologists must flag thousands of cases to find the actual cancers. The problem is that more thorough searching (being more 'suspicious') generates more false positives. This concept applies to terrorism screening, police searches, and other domains where the sought-after behavior is rare.

## Key facts

- In mammography, less than 0.5% of women who get screened actually have breast cancer, yet finding those cancers requires flagging thousands of false positives. [raw/books/psychology/Talking to Strangers - Gladwell, Malcolm.pdf#p235]
- Epidemiologist Joann Elmore calculated that radiologists reading 100,000 mammograms would correctly identify 398 of 480 cancers but also generate 8,957 false positives. [raw/books/psychology/Talking to Strangers - Gladwell, Malcolm.pdf#p235]
- Highly trained, suspicious radiologists correctly identified 422 cancers but generated 10,947 false positives—showing that more suspicion yields more false positives, not better accuracy. [raw/books/psychology/Talking to Strangers - Gladwell, Malcolm.pdf#p235]
- TSA haystack searches demonstrate the dilemma of looking for rare items: catching a rare gun requires flagging many non-gun items. [raw/books/psychology/Talking to Strangers - Gladwell, Malcolm.pdf#p229]

## Inferences

- Inference: The haystack search problem explains why police 'stop and frisk' and terrorism screening programs generate large numbers of false positives while missing many actual targets.
