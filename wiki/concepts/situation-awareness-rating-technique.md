# Situation Awareness Rating Technique

## Summary

SART (Situation Awareness Rating Technique) is a self-administered test of SA developed by Taylor (1990) consisting of three subscales combined in an equation to produce an overall SA estimate. Pew critiques SART for including attentional demand as one of its subscales, arguing this confounds SA with workload. According to Pew, while SA and workload may be correlated in practice, they should be viewed as different constructs, similar to how speed and accuracy relate—they can be positively correlated across individuals and within individuals across practice, but only sometimes trade off.

## Key facts

- SART was developed by Taylor in 1990 as a self-administered test of SA. [raw/books/strategy/Situation awareness _ analysis and measurement - edited by Mica R_ Endsley, Daniel J_ Garland.pdf#p61]
- SART consists of three subscales combined in an equation to produce an overall estimate of SA. [raw/books/strategy/Situation awareness _ analysis and measurement - edited by Mica R_ Endsley, Daniel J_ Garland.pdf#p61]
- SART includes attentional resource demand as one of its subscales, which Pew argues confounds SA with workload. [raw/books/strategy/Situation awareness _ analysis and measurement - edited by Mica R_ Endsley, Daniel J_ Garland.pdf#p61]
- Pew argues workload and SA should be viewed as different but correlated constructs, analogous to the relationship between speed and accuracy of performance. [raw/books/strategy/Situation awareness _ analysis and measurement - edited by Mica R_ Endsley, Daniel J_ Garland.pdf#p61]
