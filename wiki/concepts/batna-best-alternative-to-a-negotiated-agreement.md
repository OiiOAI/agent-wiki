# BATNA

## Summary

BATNA (Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement) is the standard against which any proposed agreement should be measured. It represents the most attractive option a party will have if no agreement is reached, and knowing it protects negotiators both from accepting terms that are too unfavorable and from rejecting terms it would be in their interest to accept. Developed by Roger Fisher and William Ury in 'Getting to Yes', BATNA is contrasted with the common but flawed practice of establishing a 'bottom line' before negotiations begin.

## Key facts

- The reason you negotiate is to produce something better than the results you can obtain without negotiating. [raw/books/strategy/Getting_to_Yes_Fisher_Ury.pdf#p51]
- Your BATNA is the standard against which any proposed agreement should be measured. [raw/books/strategy/Getting_to_Yes_Fisher_Ury.pdf#p51]
- The relative negotiating power of two parties depends primarily upon how attractive to each is the option of not reaching agreement. [raw/books/strategy/Getting_to_Yes_Fisher_Ury.pdf#p52]
- Developing your BATNA requires three distinct operations: inventing a list of actions you might take if no agreement is reached, improving promising ideas into practical alternatives, and selecting the best option. [raw/books/strategy/Getting_to_Yes_Fisher_Ury.pdf#p53]
- The better your BATNA, the greater your ability to improve the terms of any negotiated agreement. [raw/books/strategy/Getting_to_Yes_Fisher_Ury.pdf#p53]
- Developing your BATNA is perhaps the most effective course of action you can take in dealing with a seemingly more powerful negotiator. [raw/books/strategy/Getting_to_Yes_Fisher_Ury.pdf#p54]

## Inferences

- Inference: BATNA is fundamentally different from a 'bottom line' because it is based on realistic alternatives rather than arbitrary figures, and it remains flexible enough to evaluate creative solutions rather than ruling them out.
- Inference: The tourist and Bombay vendor example illustrates that wealth does not equal negotiating power; power derives from knowing alternatives, not from resources themselves.
