# Coma and Persistent Vegetative State

## Summary

Neurological conditions characterized by severely disrupted or absent consciousness. Coma involves a state of unarousable unresponsiveness, while persistent vegetative state involves wakefulness without behavioral evidence of awareness. Both are caused by damage to specific brain structures, particularly the brainstem tegmentum and/or thalamus.

## Key facts

- Coma and persistent vegetative state are caused, most often, by structural brain damage as opposed to metabolic changes, commonly from cerebral vascular disease leading to stroke or head injury. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Feeling of What Happens_ Body and Emot - Antonio R. Damasio.pdf#p357]
- Coma from structural damage involves damage to the upper half of the brainstem tegmentum at high pontine level and/or midbrain level, with hypothalamus often also damaged. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Feeling of What Happens_ Body and Emot - Antonio R. Damasio.pdf#p357]
- Coma can also be caused by damage to particular nuclei in the thalamus, namely the intralaminar nuclei, which are part of the long upward pathway originating in brain stem and disseminated throughout the cerebral cortex. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Feeling of What Happens_ Body and Emot - Antonio R. Damasio.pdf#p357]
- In all instances of structural damage causing coma, it is necessary to damage both the left and right sides of the structure; unilateral damage does not alter consciousness. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Feeling of What Happens_ Body and Emot - Antonio R. Damasio.pdf#p357]

## Inferences

- Inference: The bilateral nature of damage required for coma suggests that consciousness requires redundant systems operating on both sides of the brain.
- Inference: The distinction between coma and persistent vegetative state appears to hinge on whether wakefulness mechanisms are preserved while awareness is lost.
