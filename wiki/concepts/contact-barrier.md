# Contact Barrier

## Summary

The contact barrier is Freud's hypothetical construct in the 1895 'Project for a Scientific Psychology' representing the resistance at synapses between neurons. Freud proposed that these barriers explain how neurons can both be permanently altered by excitation (enabling memory) while remaining capable of receiving new stimuli. The concept anticipates the modern understanding of synapses, which were not formally named until 1897 by Sherrington.

## Key facts

- Freud introduced contact barriers to explain how neurons can be 'both influenced and also unaltered, unprejudiced' regarding new excitations. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p370]
- The theory states there are permeable neurons (offering no resistance and retaining nothing), which serve for perception, and impermeable ones (loaded with resistance, and holding back Qη), which are the vehicles of memory. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p371]
- Freud theorized that memory is represented by differences in the facilitations between the ψ neurons. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p371]
- Facilitation depends on the Qη which passes through the neuron in the excitatory process and on the number of repetitions of the process. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p372]

## Inferences

- Inference: Contact barriers correspond to modern synapses; Freud's 'facilitation' anticipates synaptic plasticity.
- Inference: The permeable/impermeable distinction foreshadows later neurobiological understanding of sensory relay versus associative cortex.
