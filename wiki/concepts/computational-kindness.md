# Computational Kindness

## Summary

Computational kindness is a principle introduced in this book suggesting that when interacting with others, one should frame issues in ways that minimize the computational burden on others. The key insight is that polite conventions like 'I'm flexible' or 'What do you want to do?' actually impose heavy cognitive costs by forcing others to infer preferences through recursive simulation. Being explicit about preferences ('Personally, I'm inclined toward X') or offering constrained choices ('Option A or B?') reduces the search and verification problems others face, lowering friction in social interactions. This principle applies to design—architects, planners, and interface designers can structure environments to minimize the computational problems users must solve.

## Key facts

- Polite withholding of preferences ('I'm flexible') passes the cognitive buck and invites recursive simulation of those preferences from others. [raw/books/strategy/Algorithms_to_Live_By.epub#L3150-L3153]
- Computationally kind behavior includes stating preferences explicitly or reducing options to two or three concrete choices. [raw/books/strategy/Algorithms_to_Live_By.epub#L3160-L3162]
- Computation is bad—the underlying directive of good algorithms is to minimize the labor of thought, suggesting this should apply to human interactions as well. [raw/books/strategy/Algorithms_to_Live_By.epub#L3145-L3147]

## Inferences

- Inference: Computational kindness provides a framework for understanding why certain social conventions feel considerate or inconsiderate at a cognitive level—the burden of inference may outweigh the apparent flexibility offered.
