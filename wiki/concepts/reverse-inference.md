# Reverse Inference

## Summary

Reverse inference in neuroimaging refers to the practice of reasoning from the presence of brain activation to the engagement of a particular mental function. While widely used, it is logically problematic because activation of a brain region does not necessarily imply engagement of all functions associated with that region. Poldrack (2006) identified factors that can enhance or diminish confidence in reverse inferences: selectivity of the brain region's response, presence of a connected network, and size of the region of interest.

## Key facts

- Reverse inference in brain imaging refers to reasoning from brain activation back to the engagement of a particular mental function, and is logically a fallacy because activation does not imply the function is uniquely engaged. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p245]
- Confidence in reverse inference is higher when the activated brain region shows selectivity for the hypothesized function rather than involvement in many diverse functions. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p245]
- The amygdala is implicated in fear learning, memory modulation, attention to threat, face perception, trust, other-race faces, biological motion, oddball detection, sharp contours, and framing effects, showing little selectivity. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p246]
- The insular cortex is implicated in somatic awareness, pain, disgust, empathy, gustatory processing, conditioned taste aversion, working memory interference, object recognition, and pitch perception, showing little selectivity. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p246]

## Inferences

- Inference: Neuroeconomic studies claiming to identify 'emotion' or 'social cognition' from activation in regions like insula or amygdala should be interpreted cautiously given the lack of selectivity of these regions
- Inference: Supporting reverse inferences with additional behavioral or physiological measures (like SCR or self-report) can strengthen interpretations when direct contrasts are unavailable

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The degree to which subregions within large areas like the insula show greater selectivity remains an active area of research
