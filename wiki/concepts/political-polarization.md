# Political Polarization

## Summary

Haidt examines the psychology of political polarization in American politics, arguing that 'morality binds and blinds.' He documents how Americans have become more polarized since 2000, with fewer calling themselves centrists or moderates. The analysis shows that once people join political teams, they get ensnared in moral matrices, see confirmation of their grand narratives everywhere, and find it difficult to understand those who think differently. Haidt suggests that structural changes to political institutions may be needed to reduce polarization.

## Key facts

- There has been a decline in the number of people calling themselves centrists or moderates (from 40 percent in 2000 down to 36 percent in 2011), a rise in the number of conservatives (from 38 percent to 41 percent), and a rise in the number of liberals (from 19 percent to 21 percent). [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p296]
- Friendships and social contacts across party lines were discouraged after new rules in the 1990s—once human connections were weakened, it became easier to treat members of the other party as the permanent enemy. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p297]
- Morality binds and blinds—this is not just something that happens to people on the other side; we all get sucked into tribal moral communities. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p336]
