# Cognitive Reframing

## Summary

Cognitive reframing (also called reappraisal) is the most intellectually engaged emotion regulation method, based on the principle that 'there is nothing either good or bad, but thinking makes it so' (Hamlet). It involves consciously choosing to view a situation differently to generate less negative emotion, or taking the perspective of the person who triggered you and assuming best intention. The book notes that reframing can sometimes enable denial or avoidance of real problems, and warns that people should ask whether they are using it to justify avoiding difficult conversations. Harvard research found that reframing pretest anxiety as beneficial improved exam performance, and reframing anxiety as excitement improved negotiating and public-speaking skills.

## Key facts

- Cognitive reframing (reappraisal) involves consciously choosing to view a situation in a way that generates less negative emotion or taking the perspective of the person who activated you and assuming best intention. - Harvard research found that students who reframed pretest anxiety as beneficial performed better on exams than a control group, and reframing anxiety as excitement improved negotiating and public-speaking skills. [raw/books/psychology/Permission to feel _ unlocking the power of emotions to help - Marc Ph D Brackett; OverDrive, Inc.pdf#p141]
- Functional fMRI studies show that reappraisal significantly dampens activity in the amygdala while activating lateral temporal cortical areas that help modulate emotional responses. - The book cautions that reframing can enable living in denial about real problems—if someone repeatedly humiliates you at parties, reframing that they 'didn't mean it' may avoid rather than address a genuine relationship issue.
