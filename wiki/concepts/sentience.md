# Sentience

## Summary

Sentience is the capacity to have subjective experiences—the basic ability to feel pain, pleasure, thirst, and other bodily states. The author argues sentience comes before consciousness and may be more widespread in the animal kingdom than consciousness alone. Evidence from fish, chickens, and crabs choosing environments with painkillers suggests basic sentience exists in animals with very different nervous systems from humans.

## Key facts

- Sentience comes before consciousness; if it feels like something to be a squid or octopus, then these are sentient beings. [raw/books/neuroscience/Other Minds_ The Octopus, the Sea, and the - Peter Godfrey - Smith.pdf#p65]
- Zebrafish injected with pain-causing chemicals preferred environments containing painkillers, demonstrating flexible pain-avoidance behavior. [raw/books/neuroscience/Other Minds_ The Octopus, the Sea, and the - Peter Godfrey - Smith.pdf#p77]
- Chickens with damaged legs chose less preferred food containing painkillers over more preferred food without them. [raw/books/neuroscience/Other Minds_ The Octopus, the Sea, and the - Peter Godfrey - Smith.pdf#p77]
- Hermit crabs required stronger shocks to leave higher-quality shells and were more reluctant to abandon shells when predator odor was present, demonstrating cost-benefit calculation. [raw/books/neuroscience/Other Minds_ The Octopus, the Sea, and the - Peter Godfrey - Smith.pdf#p77]

## Inferences

- Inference: Crabs and some shrimp engage in grooming injured body areas, unlike insects, suggesting sentience may be present in some arthropods but not others.
