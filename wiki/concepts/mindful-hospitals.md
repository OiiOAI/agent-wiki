# Mindful Hospitals

## Summary

Langer envisions mindful hospitals as places designed to reduce stress rather than increase it. Key features would include: colorful, spa-like environments with gardens; involvement of patients' families in all aspects of care; formation of support groups among patients; and staff whose mission is to encourage patients to add more life to their years, not just more years to their life.

## Key facts

- Hospitals ostensibly are for healing, yet as people enter they are likely to get sicker because of overwhelming fear, sterile environments, and serious-faced medical personnel. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p184]
- Roger Ulrich found that people assigned to hospital rooms with windows overlooking gardens healed faster and needed fewer painkillers than people whose rooms faced a brick wall. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p186]
- In a mindful hospital, patients' families and significant others would be involved in all aspects of their care. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p185]
- Mindful hospitals would encourage social support through gentle yoga, meditation, mindfulness exercises, card games, and discussion groups. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p185]
- The mission of the mindful hospital would be to stretch the possibilities of health and healing dynamically and continuously, with the goal of encouraging patients to add more life to one's years, not just more years to one's life. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p186]

## Inferences

- Inference: Langer's concept of a spa-like hospital environment challenges the assumption that medical seriousness requires sterile, stressful surroundings.
