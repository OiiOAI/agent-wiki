# NETtalk

## Summary

NETtalk was a neural network produced by Terry Sejnowski and Charles Rosenberg in 1987 that learned to produce spoken English from written text by training on samples with phonetic transcriptions. The network had a window of seven input letters, 80 hidden units, and output units for articulatory features. After training, NETtalk could 'read' English aloud, sounding similar to a small child learning to speak.

## Key facts

- NETtalk, produced by Sejnowski and Rosenberg in 1987, was trained to produce spoken English from written text without being explicitly given pronunciation rules. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p207]
- The network used a window of seven letters as input and had 80 hidden units and output units for 21 articulatory features plus 5 units for syllable boundaries and stresses. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p207]
- After about ten passes through the training set, NETtalk's words became intelligible and sounded very similar to a small child talking. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p208]
- NETtalk could generalize from the training set to novel words it had not encountered before. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p208]
- The rules of English pronunciation are not laid out explicitly in the network but are embedded implicitly in the learned pattern of weights. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p209]
- NETtalk has less than 500 units and 20,000 connections, tiny compared to the brain where a quarter-millimeter patch of cortex contains about 5,000 neurons. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p210]
