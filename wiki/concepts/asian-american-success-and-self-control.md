# Asian-American Success and Self-Control

## Summary

Asian-American success appears linked to cultural traditions that emphasize self-control and delayed gratification from early ages. Chinese parents expect toilet training and impulse control at earlier ages than American parents. Asian-American children show superior performance on self-control tests compared to American children of the same age. Asian-Americans make up 4% of the U.S. population but 25% of elite university student bodies, and earn 25% above the American norm. James Flynn's research found that Chinese-Americans achieve the same elite jobs as Americans with higher IQs (103 vs 110 threshold), suggesting self-control compensates for modest IQ differences.

## Key facts

- Asian-Americans comprise 4% of the U.S. population but 25% of student bodies at elite universities like Stanford, Columbia, and Cornell. [raw/books/psychology/Willpower _ Rediscovering the Greatest Human Strength - Roy F_ Baumeister, John Tierney.pdf#p179]
- Chinese-Americans achieve the same elite jobs with an IQ of 103 that white Americans need an IQ of 110 to achieve. [raw/books/psychology/Willpower _ Rediscovering the Greatest Human Strength - Roy F_ Baumeister, John Tierney.pdf#p179]
- Chinese mothers in one study most frequently mentioned setting high goals, enforcing tough standards, and requiring extra homework—concepts of little interest to American mothers focused on self-esteem. [raw/books/psychology/Willpower _ Rediscovering the Greatest Human Strength - Roy F_ Baumeister, John Tierney.pdf#p180]
- Chinese-American parents follow Confucian concepts of chiao shun ('to train') and guan ('to govern' and 'to love'), which represent a form of devotion rather than oppression. [raw/books/psychology/Willpower _ Rediscovering the Greatest Human Strength - Roy F_ Baumeister, John Tierney.pdf#p180]
