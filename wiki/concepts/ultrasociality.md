# Ultrasociality

## Summary

Ultrasociality refers to the extreme form of social cooperation found in humans, eusocial insects, and a few other species. Haidt uses the term to describe the uniquely human capacity for large-scale cooperation with non-kin, achieved through cultural evolution and shared intentionality.

## Key facts

- Haidt distinguishes ultrasociality from eusociality: eusociality requires that nearly all group members are effectively sterile (as in bees and ants), while ultrasociality encompasses both eusocial insects and humans who achieve massive cooperation without sterile castes. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p395]
- Humans became ultrasocial through a 'mutualism hypothesis': early cooperation for shared goals created selection pressures for shared intentionality and group-level adaptations. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p396]
- Humans and their domesticated servants now account for an estimated 98 percent of all mammalian life by weight. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p396]
- Tomasello's research shows that humans uniquely develop 'shared intentionality'—the ability to coordinate with others toward common goals—which distinguishes human cooperation from chimpanzee collaboration. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p396]

## Inferences

- Inference: Ultrasociality likely required both genetic evolution during the Pleistocene and cultural evolution, with some genetic changes continuing into the Holocene.
