# Background Institutions for Distributive Justice

## Summary

Rawls describes four branches of government necessary for distributive justice: the allocation branch (maintaining competitive markets and correcting externalities), the stabilization branch (achieving reasonably full employment), the transfer branch (guaranteeing a social minimum addressing needs), and the distribution branch (preserving approximate justice through taxation and property rights adjustments). These institutions work together to ensure the price system yields just distributive shares.

## Key facts

- The allocation branch keeps the price system workably competitive, prevents formation of unreasonable market power, and corrects departures from efficiency caused by prices not measuring social benefits and costs. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p253]
- The stabilization branch strives to bring about reasonably full employment supporting free choice of occupation and deployment of finance. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p253]
- The transfer branch guarantees a social minimum taking needs into account—competitive markets give no consideration to needs and cannot be the sole device of distribution. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p253]
- The distribution branch imposes inheritance and gift taxes and restrictions on bequest to gradually correct the distribution of wealth and prevent concentrations of power detrimental to fair equality of opportunity. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p254]
- Inheritance is permissible provided resulting inequalities are to the advantage of the least fortunate and compatible with liberty and fair equality of opportunity. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p255]

## Inferences

- Inference: The theory of justice does not prejudge whether a private-property economy or socialism is more just—either can satisfy the two principles of justice depending on the traditions, institutions, and historical circumstances of each society.
