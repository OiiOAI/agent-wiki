# Chunking

## Summary

Chunking is a memory strategy that compresses large amounts of information into manageable units. George Miller's 1956 paper established that humans can only keep 3-4 items in mind at once, but there is no set definition for what constitutes 'one piece' of information. By grouping information into meaningful chunks—like the 3-2-4 pattern of Social Security numbers—memory load is dramatically reduced. Chess grand masters and memory athletes exploit chunking by recognizing familiar patterns from years of experience, allowing them to memorize vast amounts of information.

## Key facts

- George Miller's 1956 paper established that the human brain can only keep about 3-4 pieces of information in mind at once. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p58]
- Social Security numbers are easy to remember because they follow a predictable 3-2-4 chunk pattern, reducing information load by two-thirds. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p58]
- Chess grand masters could perfectly recall piece positions in normal game configurations but performed at amateur levels when pieces were placed randomly. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p59]
- Memory athletes do not test higher than average subjects in natural memory abilities but exploit chunking and other mnemonic strategies. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p56]
