# The Work Ethic and Sex

## Summary

Perel critiques the American tendency to apply the Protestant work ethic—goal-orientation, efficiency, planning, and measurable results—to sexual relationships. Eroticism is inherently inefficient, resists optimization, and requires surrender of control rather than more effort.

## Key facts

- Americans apply the can-do attitude to relationships, viewing dwindling desire as a scheduling or communication problem that can be fixed with better prioritizing and organizational skills. [raw/books/psychology/Mating in Captivity_ Unlocking Erotic Intelligence - Esther Perel.pdf#p9]
- Eroticism is inefficient—it loves to squander time and resources. As Adam Phillips notes, 'in our erotic life work does not work... trying is always trying too hard.'. [raw/books/psychology/Mating in Captivity_ Unlocking Erotic Intelligence - Esther Perel.pdf#p76]
- The sexual performance perfection industry generates its own inhibitions and anxieties; sensuality doesn't lend itself to the rigors of scorekeeping. [raw/books/psychology/Mating in Captivity_ Unlocking Erotic Intelligence - Esther Perel.pdf#p74]
- Perel reframes the challenge from 'work' to 'freedom' and 'play,' suggesting that rigidifying desire comes from confinement, not from lack of effort. [raw/books/psychology/Mating in Captivity_ Unlocking Erotic Intelligence - Esther Perel.pdf#p77]

## Inferences

- Inference: The case of Ryan and Christine demonstrates that desire renewal came not from planned effort but from spontaneous moments of honesty that reintroduced mystery and freedom into their relationship.
