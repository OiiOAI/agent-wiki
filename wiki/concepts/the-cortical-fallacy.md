# The Cortical Fallacy

## Summary

The cortical fallacy is the assumption that consciousness is generated by the cerebral cortex. This idea dominated neuroscience from the 19th century onwards, based on the empiricist philosophy that the mind consists entirely of memory images derived from sensory experience, and the observation that the cortex is the seat of perceptual and cognitive functions. Solms argues this is a fallacy: consciousness persists in the absence of cortex, as demonstrated by hydranencephalic children who show emotional responsiveness and intentional behaviour, and by decorticate animals that are more active and emotional than normal. The fallacy arose from 19th-century neurologists including Meynert and Munk, who equated the 'mind' with learned representations, thereby excluding subcortical structures from consciousness. Solms argues that consciousness arises from ancient brainstem structures.

## Key facts

- The cortical fallacy is the assumption that consciousness is generated by the cerebral cortex — Solms argues it is a fallacy because consciousness persists in the absence of cortex. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p56]
- Hydranencephalic children born without a cortex show emotional responses, orienting reactions, preferences for certain situations, initiative, and even absence seizures — none of which is consistent with a vegetative state. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p54]
- Decorticate mammals — including dogs, cats, and rats — show no gross behavioural abnormalities that would allow a casual observer to identify them as impaired and are often more active and emotional than normal animals. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p57]
- The cortical theory arose from 19th-century empiricist philosophy and was promoted by neurologists including Theodor Meynert and Hermann Munk, who equated the 'mind' with memory images. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p63]
- Freud accepted the cortical theory — believing consciousness (including feelings of pleasure and unpleasure) was located in the cortex — but Solms argues Freud got the relationship between the 'id' (brainstem) and 'ego' (cortex) the wrong way round. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p51]
- Solms argues that consciousness should not be confused with intelligence — it is possible to feel pain or hunger without any intellectual comprehension of what the feeling is about. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p13]
- Antonio Damasio studied Patient B, whose insular cortex was entirely destroyed by herpes simplex encephalitis, and found that the patient continued to report feelings of hunger, thirst, desire to void, pleasure, sadness, and self-awareness — contradicting the theory that the insula generates the feeling self. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p70]

## Inferences

- Inference: The cortical fallacy persisted because the cortex does perform many mental functions (perception, memory, language) — but these functions are largely unconscious. The cortex generates the contents of consciousness (qualia) but not the sentience that experiences them.
- Inference: The cortical theory creates ethical problems: doctors may assume hydranencephalic children are incapable of feeling pain and therefore perform operations without anaesthetic — a potentially appalling medical error.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Whether hydranencephalic children have the same kind of phenomenal consciousness as typical humans cannot be definitively established, given the problem of other minds and their inability to provide verbal reports.
