# Automatic Postural Responses

## Summary

Automatic postural responses are fast, involuntary reactions to sudden disturbances that maintain the body's center of mass within the base of support. These responses are not simple stretch reflexes but involve synergistic activation of muscle groups in characteristic temporal and spatial patterns. They adapt to changing biomechanical conditions through implicit learning and involve multiple sensory inputs including somatosensory, vestibular, and visual information.

## Key facts

- Automatic postural responses are not simple stretch reflexes; recruitment of muscles serves the requirements of equilibrium and is not determined by muscle length changes from the disturbance. [raw/books/neuroscience/Eric Kandel PRINCIPLES OF NEURAL SCIENCE Sixth Edition - Eric R Kandel; John Koester; Sarah Mack; Steven Siegelbaum.pdf#p887]
- Automatic postural responses have latencies of 40-60 ms in cats and 90-120 ms in humans, longer than monosynaptic stretch reflexes but shorter than voluntary reaction times. [raw/books/neuroscience/Eric Kandel PRINCIPLES OF NEURAL SCIENCE Sixth Edition - Eric R Kandel; John Koester; Sarah Mack; Steven Siegelbaum.pdf#p888]
- The set of muscles recruited in a postural response depends on the body's initial stance; the same disturbance elicits different responses when standing unaided versus holding a support. [raw/books/neuroscience/Eric Kandel PRINCIPLES OF NEURAL SCIENCE Sixth Edition - Eric R Kandel; John Koester; Sarah Mack; Steven Siegelbaum.pdf#p889]
- Postural responses use stereotyped strategies: ankle strategy (rotating about ankles), hip strategy (bending at hips), and stepping or grasping strategies that enlarge the base of support. [raw/books/neuroscience/Eric Kandel PRINCIPLES OF NEURAL SCIENCE Sixth Edition - Eric R Kandel; John Koester; Sarah Mack; Steven Siegelbaum.pdf#p889]
- Postural strategies adapt gradually to new biomechanical conditions; switching from a wide platform to a narrow beam requires several trials to shift from ankle to hip strategy. [raw/books/neuroscience/Eric Kandel PRINCIPLES OF NEURAL SCIENCE Sixth Edition - Eric R Kandel; John Koester; Sarah Mack; Steven Siegelbaum.pdf#p891]
- Large-diameter somatosensory fibers (group I) are essential for normal response latencies; destruction of these fibers delays EMG responses and impairs balance recovery. [raw/books/neuroscience/Eric Kandel PRINCIPLES OF NEURAL SCIENCE Sixth Edition - Eric R Kandel; John Koester; Sarah Mack; Steven Siegelbaum.pdf#p895]
