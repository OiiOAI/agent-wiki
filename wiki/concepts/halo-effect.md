# Halo Effect

## Summary

The Halo Effect occurs when a single outstanding quality (positive or negative) dazzles observers and affects how they view the entire person, company, or product. The author illustrates this with Cisco's stock crash where journalists reframed all company attributes as positive before and negative after.

## Key facts

- The halo effect occurs when a single aspect dazzles us and affects how we see the full picture; we take a remarkable fact and extrapolate conclusions that are harder to nail down. [raw/books/strategy/The Art of Thinking Clearly _ Better thinking, Better - Rolf Dobelli; Nicky Griffin.pdf#p93]
- When Cisco's stock plummeted 80% in 2001, journalists reframed all competitive advantages as destructive shortcomings even though neither strategy nor CEO had changed. [raw/books/strategy/The Art of Thinking Clearly _ Better thinking, Better - Rolf Dobelli; Nicky Griffin.pdf#p93]
- Dozens of studies show we automatically regard good-looking people as more pleasant, honest and intelligent, with this effect detectable even in schools where teachers unconsciously give attractive students better grades. [raw/books/strategy/The Art of Thinking Clearly _ Better thinking, Better - Rolf Dobelli; Nicky Griffin.pdf#p93]
- The halo effect is the tendency to like (or dislike) everything about a person based on a single impression. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p84]
- In Solomon Asch's classic study, the same traits were rated more positively when listed after positive traits and more negatively when listed after negative traits. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p85]
- The halo effect extends to consumer behavior: people prefer products with easy-to-pronounce names and give higher ratings to identical arguments written in clear fonts. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p85]
- The halo effect causes observers to assume that successful companies have better leadership practices, and that leaders of successful companies are more flexible, orderly, and decisive, while the same leaders appear rigid and confused when their companies struggle. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p267]
- Philip Rosenzweig documented the halo effect in The Halo Effect, showing how business writers continuously overstate the impact of leadership on outcomes while ignoring the role of luck and regression to the mean. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p267]
- The halo effect reverses causal inference: we conclude that companies fail because their CEOs are rigid, when actually the CEOs appear rigid because the companies failed. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p267]
- The halo effect also operates in personality judgments, where finding one person 'good' leads to assuming they are also kind, intelligent, and capable, maintaining narrative coherence. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p258]

## Inferences

- Inference: The halo effect demonstrates how System 1 constructs coherent narratives by filling information gaps with emotionally consistent assumptions.
- Inference: The halo effect explains why retrospective business success books are popular: knowing outcomes, observers construct coherent causal narratives that attribute success to good leadership and underestimate the contribution of luck.
