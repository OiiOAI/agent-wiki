# Action-Shaping View

## Summary

The action-shaping view holds that the first nervous systems existed to solve the problem of coordinating the micro-actions of body parts into coherent macro-actions of the whole organism. This view, developed by Chris Pantin in the 1950s and revived by Fred Keijzer, emphasizes that generating action in multicellular organisms is not trivial—it requires shaping multitudes of micro-contractions into unified behavior.

## Key facts

- The action-shaping view holds that early nervous systems were for shaping movements, maintaining rhythms, crawling and swimming rather than primarily for sensory-motor coordination. [raw/books/neuroscience/Other Minds_ The Octopus, the Sea, and the - Peter Godfrey - Smith.pdf#p29]
- Chris Pantin developed the action-shaping view in the 1950s and it was revived recently by Fred Keijzer. [raw/books/neuroscience/Other Minds_ The Octopus, the Sea, and the - Peter Godfrey - Smith.pdf#p24]
- The author suggests the demands of pure coordination may have been responsible for much of the multiplication of neurons in the octopus; those neurons are needed just to make the body controllable. [raw/books/neuroscience/Other Minds_ The Octopus, the Sea, and the - Peter Godfrey - Smith.pdf#p59]

## Inferences

- Inference: The octopus's soft, multi-armed body without joints posed an extreme coordination challenge, potentially driving the evolution of its large nervous system.
- Inference: Large nervous systems may have evolved first for coordination, with intelligence arising as a byproduct of that neural complexity.
