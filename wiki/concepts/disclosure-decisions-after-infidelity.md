# Disclosure Decisions After Infidelity

## Summary

This chunk examines the complex ethical and practical dilemmas surrounding whether to disclose an affair. It presents Perel's 'open-secrets policy' for therapy, distinguishes between 'detective questions' (seeking sordid details) versus 'investigative questions' (exploring meaning and motives), and explores cultural differences in attitudes toward secrecy, privacy, and disclosure in matters of infidelity.

## Key facts

- Perel's colleague Lisa Spiegel proposes a three-part test for truth-telling: Is it honest, is it helpful, and is it kind?. [raw/books/psychology/The State of Affairs - Esther Perel.pdf#p113]
- Perel recounts advising a terminally ill patient that while confession might bring him 'peace,' his wife 'will live in turmoil' and 'toss and turn, sleepless for months.'. [raw/books/psychology/The State of Affairs - Esther Perel.pdf#p114]
- Perel adopts what Janis Abrahms Spring calls an 'open-secrets policy,' where individual therapy sessions remain confidential and the decision to reveal or not reveal is considered part of the therapy itself. [raw/books/psychology/The State of Affairs - Esther Perel.pdf#p115]
- Michele Scheinkman argues that 'a no-secrets policy holds the therapist hostage' and limits ability to help in critical relationship moments. [raw/books/psychology/The State of Affairs - Esther Perel.pdf#p116]
- Perel distinguishes privacy (a functional boundary we agree on) from secrecy (matters we deliberately mislead about). [raw/books/psychology/The State of Affairs - Esther Perel.pdf#p117]
- Journalist Pamela Druckerman notes that 'discretion seems to be the cornerstone of adultery in France,' with many preferring not to tell and not to know. [raw/books/psychology/The State of Affairs - Esther Perel.pdf#p117]
- Perel distinguishes 'detective questions' (how many times, did you do it in our bed) from 'investigative questions' (why now, what did you experience that you don't have with me). [raw/books/psychology/The State of Affairs - Esther Perel.pdf#p125]
- Psychologist Marty Klein argues that monitoring a partner's digital activity 'thwarts' rather than enhances trust, because cheaters who want to be unfaithful 'all the monitoring in the world won't stop them.'. [raw/books/psychology/The State of Affairs - Esther Perel.pdf#p127]

## Inferences

- Inference: Perel's distinction between detective and investigative questions implies that excessive focus on factual details may impede rather than facilitate reconciliation, while questions about meaning create bridges toward renewed intimacy.
