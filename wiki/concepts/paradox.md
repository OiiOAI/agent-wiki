# Paradox in Human Communication

## Summary

Paradoxes in communication arise when messages demand behaviors that by their nature cannot be commanded. The classic example is 'Be spontaneous!'—spontaneity cannot be willed into existence. These paradoxes violate the Theory of Logical Types by applying to themselves the rules they establish. The authors identify 'Be spontaneous!' paradoxes in family dynamics (wanting a child to want to do homework), insomnia (trying to force sleep), depression (demanding one change one's mood), sexual difficulties (planning spontaneity), totalitarian systems (requiring belief rather than compliance), and education (making fun mandatory).

## Key facts

- Paradox arises when messages demand behaviors that by their nature cannot be commanded or willed. [raw/books/strategy/Change _ principles of problem formulation and problem - Paul Watzlawick, John H_ Weakland, Richard Fisch; foreword.epub#L107-L686]
- 'Be spontaneous!' paradoxes violate the Theory of Logical Types by imposing the rule that behavior should not be rule-governed but spontaneous. [raw/books/strategy/Change _ principles of problem formulation and problem - Paul Watzlawick, John H_ Weakland, Richard Fisch; foreword.epub#L107-L686]
- The structure of 'Be spontaneous!' paradoxes is analogous to Epimenides' paradox ('All Cretans are liars')—both confuse member and class levels. [raw/books/strategy/Change _ principles of problem formulation and problem - Paul Watzlawick, John H_ Weakland, Richard Fisch; foreword.epub#L107-L686]
- Double-bind theory, developed by Gregory Bateson's research group, originally applied to schizophrenia but applies more broadly to disturbed communication patterns. - Dictatorships create paradoxes by demanding not just compliance but belief, requiring citizens to want what they are coerced into doing. [raw/books/strategy/Change _ principles of problem formulation and problem - Paul Watzlawick, John H_ Weakland, Richard Fisch; foreword.epub#L107-L686]
