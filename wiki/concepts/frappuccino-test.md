# Frappuccino Test

## Summary

The Frappuccino Test is a self-assessment for metabolic flexibility inspired by the medical glucose challenge test. By consuming a high-sugar drink (like a 16-oz Starbucks Frappuccino with 45 grams of sugar) on an empty stomach and monitoring how the body responds over four hours, one can gauge whether blood sugar is being managed effectively. Adverse reactions indicate metabolic inflexibility and potential insulin resistance.

## Key facts

- The Frappuccino Test is related to the medical glucose challenge test used by doctors. [raw/books/health/Built_to_Move.epub#L1574-L1575]
- In a glucose challenge test, consuming a sugary liquid causes blood glucose to rise, signaling the pancreas to send insulin to clear glucose from the blood. [raw/books/health/Built_to_Move.epub#L1575-L1578]
- A regular 16-ounce Frappuccino has 45 grams of sugar; the 24-ounce has 59 grams. [raw/books/health/Built_to_Move.epub#L1590-L1592]
- The test should be run on an empty stomach, at least four hours out from the last meal, with no eating for another four hours afterward. [raw/books/health/Built_to_Move.epub#L1592-L1594]
- If you felt irritable, nauseous, jittery, spacey, or had diarrhea after the Frappuccino, this indicates metabolic inflexibility. [raw/books/health/Built_to_Move.epub#L1603-L1605]
- If you were able to continue your day without incident after the Frappuccino, you are most likely metabolically flexible. [raw/books/health/Built_to_Move.epub#L1605-L1607]
- The book recommends retaking the test after two weeks of following the Vital Sign 6 physical practice to see if metabolic flexibility has improved. [raw/books/health/Built_to_Move.epub#L1618-L1621]

## Inferences

- Inference: The book suggests that people who regularly consume high-sugar foods may not have adverse reactions to the Frappuccino but that this adaptation is not necessarily healthy.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The test is not a medical diagnostic tool and cannot determine whether someone has insulin resistance.
