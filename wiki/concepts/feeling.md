# Feeling

## Summary

A feeling is the private, mental experience of an emotion, as opposed to emotion itself which has publicly observable components. Feelings are poised at the threshold separating being from knowing, having a privileged connection to consciousness. An organism may represent a feeling nonconsciously without ever knowing it is occurring.

## Key facts

- The term feeling should be reserved for the private, mental experience of an emotion, while emotion designates the collection of publicly observable responses. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Feeling of What Happens_ Body and Emot - Antonio R. Damasio.pdf#p43]
- You cannot observe a feeling in someone else although you can observe a feeling in yourself when you perceive your own emotional states. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Feeling of What Happens_ Body and Emot - Antonio R. Damasio.pdf#p43]
- The basic mechanisms underlying emotion do not require consciousness; even the occurrence of a feeling is conceivable without the organism knowing of its occurrence. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Feeling of What Happens_ Body and Emot - Antonio R. Damasio.pdf#p43]
- Feelings are poised at the very threshold that separates being from knowing and thus have a privileged connection to consciousness. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Feeling of What Happens_ Body and Emot - Antonio R. Damasio.pdf#p43]
- We often realize suddenly that we feel anxious or uncomfortable, pleased or relaxed, but the particular state of feeling has begun before the moment of knowing. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Feeling of What Happens_ Body and Emot - Antonio R. Damasio.pdf#p37]
- Consciousness must be present if feelings are to influence the subject having them beyond the immediate here and now. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Feeling of What Happens_ Body and Emot - Antonio R. Damasio.pdf#p37]
- Solms argues that, alone among mental functions, feeling is necessarily conscious — 'Who ever heard of a feeling that has no subjective quality?'. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p85]
- Freud accepted that feelings must be conscious: 'It is surely of the essence of an emotion that we should be aware of it, i.e., that it should become known to consciousness.'. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p86]
- Solms argues that while perception and memory are not inherently conscious brain functions, feelings are — they are the wellspring of sentient being. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p89]
- The brainstem and hypothalamus generate what Freud called 'drives' and what Panksepp calls 'homeostatic affects' — bodily needs that generate the 'psychical energy' of mental life. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p50]
- Freud described the forebrain as a 'sympathetic ganglion' monitoring and regulating the needs of the body, with bodily needs providing 'the mainspring of the psychical mechanism'. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p40]
- Consciousness, in Solms's view, arose from organisms whose survival prospects were affected by their ambient surrounds — the physiological values registering their experiences were the precursors of conscious feeling. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p13]
- Solms argues that consciousness exists to help organisms do better — feeling is about how well or badly you are doing in life. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p13]

## Inferences

- Inference: Feelings serve as the bridge between nonconscious emotional processes and conscious self-awareness, making subjective experience possible.
- Inference: ling is necessarily conscious and feeling originates in the brainstem, then consciousness also originates in the brainstem — not the cortex. This resolves the apparent contradiction in Freud, who located the 'id' (drives/feelings) in unconscious processes while locating consciousness in the ego (cortex).

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Solms acknowledges that some psychoanalysts disagree that all feelings are conscious, claiming unconscious emotions exist — he defers this debate to later in the book.
