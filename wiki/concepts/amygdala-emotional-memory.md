# Amygdala and Emotional Memory

## Summary

The amygdala is a small almond-shaped brain structure critical for emotional memory formation. Damage to the amygdala impairs fear conditioning and the emotional enhancement of memory without causing general memory deficits. The amygdala enables the brain to learn fear responses and encodes traumatic memories with particular vividness and persistence. The hippocampus, adjacent to the amygdala, handles the contextual details of memories.

## Key facts

- Damage to the hippocampus and surrounding cortical areas causes general impairment in forming and retrieving new episodic memories, but damage to the amygdala does not result in this global memory deficit. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p235]
- Patients with amygdala damage can remember recent experiences with little difficulty, but their memories do not benefit from the emotions that normally accompany arousing experiences. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p235]
- Abnormal fear responses are a hallmark of amygdala damage: patients have great difficulty learning to fear situations that would normally scare others. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p235]
- Healthy people remember emotionally arousing events better than mundane ones, but patients with amygdala damage remember mundane events normally but show no improved memory for emotionally arousing events. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p235]
