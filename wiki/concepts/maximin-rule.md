# Maximin Rule

## Summary

A decision rule for choice under uncertainty that instructs one to rank alternatives by their worst possible outcomes and adopt the alternative whose worst outcome is superior to the worst outcomes of others. Rawls uses this as a heuristic device to understand why the parties in the original position would choose the two principles of justice, arguing that the original position manifests features making the maximin rule appropriate.

## Key facts

- The maximin rule tells us to rank alternatives by their worst possible outcomes: we are to adopt the alternative the worst outcome of which is superior to the worst outcomes of the others. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p148]
- Three chief features suggest the maximin rule: (1) sharply discounting estimates of probabilities, (2) the person cares very little for what he might gain above the minimum he can be sure of, and (3) the rejected alternatives have outcomes that one can hardly accept. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p148]
- The original position has been defined so that it is a situation in which the maximin rule applies: the veil of ignorance excludes knowledge of likelihoods, and the choice of principles is a fundamental one. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p149]
- The two principles of justice are the maximin solution to the problem of social justice — the principles a person would choose for the design of a society in which his enemy is to assign him his place. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p147]
- The maximin rule is not in general a suitable guide for choices under uncertainty but is attractive in situations marked by certain special features that the original position manifests. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p148]

## Inferences

- Inference: Rawls does not claim the parties actually use the maximin rule mechanically, but uses it as an analogical device to clarify the structure of the choice problem in the original position.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Rawls acknowledges the maximin rule is not self-evident and only sketches the features that make it plausible; detailed arguments for the principles appear in subsequent parts of the book.
