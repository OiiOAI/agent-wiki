# Neural Networks

## Summary

Neural networks are assemblies of variously interconnected units, where each unit has the properties of a much simplified neuron. They are used to simulate nervous system processes, produce useful commercial devices, and test general theories of how brains work. The field has evolved from McCulloch-Pitts networks (1943) through perceptrons, Hopfield networks, to the PDP approach and backpropagation algorithms.

## Key facts

- Neural networks are assemblies of variously interconnected units, each with properties of a much simplified neuron. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p192]
- Neural networks are used to simulate what goes on in parts of the nervous system, to produce useful commercial devices, and to test general theories of how brains work. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p192]
- McCulloch and Pitts showed in 1943 that networks made of very simple units connected together can, in principle, compute any logical or arithmetical function. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p196]
- Frank Rosenblatt invented the Perceptron, which could alter its connections using a simple rule so that it could be taught to perform certain simple tasks. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p196]
- Minsky and Papert proved that the Perceptron architecture could not execute the 'exclusive OR,' which killed interest in Perceptrons for several years. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p196]
- John Hopfield proposed a network in 1982 (Hopfield network) that was a simple network that fed back on itself and would quickly reach a stable state. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p197]
- The PDP book by Rumelhart and McClelland appeared in 1986 and rapidly became influential, introducing backpropagation as a key learning algorithm. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p201]
- Backpropagation requires units to have a graded output between 0 and 1, and the network has input, hidden, and output layers with only forward connections. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p204]
- The Hopfield network uses a form of Hebb's rule to adjust connection strengths, where units encourage their 'friends' and discourage their 'enemies.'. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p198]
- If too many memories are added to a Hopfield network it can easily become confused and produce nonsense output. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p200]

## Inferences

- Inference: The brain is highly nonlinear, meaning doubling the input does not give twice the output—this makes predicting behavior of interacting neurons difficult.
- Inference: The brain is massively parallel while computers are largely serial, which compensates for the relatively slow behavior of neurons.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The exact biological validity of backpropagation as a learning mechanism in the brain is questionable since it requires teaching information sent back along the same axons.
