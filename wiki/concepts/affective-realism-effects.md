# Affective Realism Effects

## Summary

Affective realism effects describe how a person's own inner bodily sensations may be taken by the predictive brain as additional evidence for active threat, leading them to perceptually misidentify innocent items. This demonstrates that we sometimes perceive what we feel, with profound implications for understanding phenomena like shooter bias.

## Key facts

- A police officer's own inner bodily sensations such as heightened heart rate, sweaty palms, and clenched stomach may be taken as evidence for active threat, leading to perceptual misidentification of innocent items as weapons. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Experience Machine_ How Our Minds Pred - Andy Clark.pdf#p128]
- Between 2007 and 2014, 49 percent of officer-involved shootings of unarmed victims were linked to threat perception failures. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Experience Machine_ How Our Minds Pred - Andy Clark.pdf#p128]
- Threat perception failures involved Black victims in 80 percent of cases in Philadelphia where White and Black citizens are roughly equal. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Experience Machine_ How Our Minds Pred - Andy Clark.pdf#p128]
- An individual who has just experienced anger has been shown to be more prone to identify an innocent object as a gun than those caused to feel a different prior emotion such as sadness. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Experience Machine_ How Our Minds Pred - Andy Clark.pdf#p129]

## Inferences

- Inference: Combating affective realism effects requires both challenging bad stereotypes and using new training regimes to alter responses to bodily information [raw/books/neuroscience/The Experience Machine_ How Our Minds Pred - Andy Clark.pdf#p129]

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The extent to which immersive VR training can effectively reduce shooter bias in real-world police encounters requires further longitudinal study
