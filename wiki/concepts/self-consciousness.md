# Self-Consciousness

## Summary

McGilchrist distinguishes self-consciousness (being aware of oneself as the object of attention) from consciousness 'pure and simple' (simply aware of being). He argues that self-consciousness comes about when the left hemisphere inspects the right. Most functions mediated by the right hemisphere must remain outside the focus of awareness—implicit, intuitive, unattended to. Sufferers of excessive self-consciousness (as in social phobia) show the paralysing effect of dragging into awareness what should remain unconscious for skills to work effectively.

## Key facts

- Self-consciousness is distinguished from consciousness 'pure and simple', which is simply being aware of being, without awareness of oneself as the object of attention. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p315]
- Most functions mediated by the right hemisphere must remain outside the focus of awareness—implicit, intuitive, unattended to—for effective performance. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p315]
- The idea that self-consciousness is the left hemisphere inspecting the right is supported by: the attentional 'spotlight' being a function of the left hemisphere; casualties in self-consciousness being right-hemisphere-based social or empathic skills; and schizophrenic subjects showing relative hypofunction of the right hemisphere.
