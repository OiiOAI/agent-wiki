# Difference Principle

## Summary

The difference principle is part of the democratic interpretation of the second principle of justice. It holds that social and economic inequalities are just if and only if they work as part of a scheme which improves the expectations of the least advantaged members of society. The principle removes the indeterminateness of the principle of efficiency by singling out a particular position from which to judge inequalities. It is strongly egalitarian: unless there is a distribution that makes everyone better off, an equal distribution is preferred. The principle accepts inequalities only if they benefit the least advantaged, not merely if they maximize aggregate welfare.

## Key facts

- The difference principle holds that social and economic inequalities are just if and only if they result in compensating benefits for everyone, and in particular for the least advantaged members of society. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p25]
- The difference principle removes the indeterminateness of the principle of efficiency by singling out a particular position from which the social and economic inequalities of the basic structure are to be judged. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p77]
- Assuming the framework of institutions required by equal liberty and fair equality of opportunity, the higher expectations of those better situated are just if and only if they work as part of a scheme which improves the expectations of the least advantaged. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p77]
- The difference principle is perfectly satisfied only when the contribution to the least advantaged person's expectations made by greater expectations of the better situated is tangent to the highest indifference curve it touches. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p77]
- There is no injustice in the greater benefits earned by a few provided that the situation of persons not so fortunate is thereby improved. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p25]
- The difference principle requires that higher expectations of the more advantaged contribute to the prospects of the least advantaged. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p80]
- When the difference principle is fully satisfied, it is impossible to make any one representative man better off without making the least advantaged representative man worse off. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p81]
- A society should try to avoid the region where the marginal contributions of those better off are negative, since this violates the principle of mutual advantage as well as democratic equality. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p81]
- The difference principle is compatible with the principle of efficiency when both principles are perfectly fulfilled. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p82]
- No one deserves his place in the distribution of native endowments, any more than one deserves one's initial starting place in society. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p104]
- The naturally advantaged are not to gain merely because they are more gifted, but only to cover the costs of training and education and for using their endowments in ways that help the less fortunate. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p103]
- The Difference Principle applies to the basic structure of society as a whole, not to ad hoc allocations of goods among individuals. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p152]
- The principles of equal liberty and open positions prevent the extreme contingencies that the abstract objection to the Difference Principle imagines. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p152]
- The Difference Principle is equivalent to treating the distribution of natural abilities as a collective asset so that the more fortunate benefit only in ways that help those who have lost out. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p171]

## Inferences

- Inference: The difference principle rejects justifying institutions on the grounds that hardships of some are offset by greater good in the aggregate; it requires that everyone, including the least advantaged, actually benefit.
- Inference: The difference principle represents an agreement to regard the distribution of natural talents as a common asset and to share in the benefits of this distribution.
- Inference: The Difference Principle embodies the idea of reciprocity in social cooperation, treating persons not merely as means but also as ends in themselves.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: How far the incentives argument (that greater expectations encourage efficiency and innovation that benefits all) is actually true is acknowledged by Rawls as requiring examination.
