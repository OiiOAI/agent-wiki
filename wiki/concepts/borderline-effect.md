# Borderline Effect

## Summary

The borderline effect describes how arbitrary diagnostic cutoff points create hard distinctions between people who are virtually identical, with dramatically different life outcomes resulting from labels (e.g., prediabetic at 5.7% A1c vs. normal at 5.6%). Langer's diabetes study found that patients labeled 'prediabetic' ended up with soaring A1c results over time compared to those labeled 'normal,' suggesting that the label itself—not the medical difference—drives worse health outcomes, making the diagnosis a self-fulfilling prophecy.

## Key facts

- In Langer's diabetes study, patients with A1c scores of 5.7 percent (labeled prediabetic) ended up with soaring A1c results over time, while those with 5.6 percent (labeled normal) stayed normal. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p23-24]
- Endocrinologists consulted in the study all agreed there was no medically relevant difference between a 5.6 percent and 5.7 percent A1c score. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p22]
- The borderline effect has insurance implications, making the same A1c score a 'preexisting condition' for one person and no problem for a virtually identical person depending on which side of the line they fall. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p24]
- Langer argues that labels corresponding to chronic conditions rob people of personal control and prevent the possibility of optimal health, and that the outcome of many diagnoses can become self-fulfilling prophecies. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p25]
- Examples of the borderline effect include IQ 69 vs. 70 (only 70 is deemed normal), or athletes missing medals by milliseconds, where marginal differences produce vastly different labels. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p21-22]
