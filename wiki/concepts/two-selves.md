# Two Selves

## Summary

Part V of the book introduces the distinction between the experiencing self (which lives in the present moment, feeling pleasure and pain) and the remembering self (which keeps the story of our life, dominated by peak moments and endings). These two selves often disagree about what makes us happy, leading to mistakes in decision-making about experiences.

## Key facts

- The experiencing self lives in the present and knows current pain and pleasure. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p24]
- The remembering self keeps our story and dominates in our evaluation of life and choices about repetition of experiences. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p24]
- People often choose to repeat experiences that the experiencing self would prefer to avoid, because the remembering self is misled by peak moments and endings. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p24]
- The two selves explain why what makes the experiencing self happy differs from what satisfies the remembering self. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p24]
- The experiencing self answers 'Does it hurt now?' and registers pain and pleasure moment by moment. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p492]
- The remembering self answers 'How was it overall?' and stores memories that determine what we learn and the decisions we make. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p492]
- Duration neglect: the duration of an experience has little or no impact on how people retrospectively evaluate it. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p491]
- The peak-end rule: retrospective global ratings are well predicted by the average of the worst level of pain reported and the level of pain at the end of the procedure. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p491]
- In the cold-hand experiment, 80% of participants who reported pain reduction at the end of the 90-second episode chose to repeat that longer episode, demonstrating that people will choose to suffer 30 extra seconds of pain to have a better memory. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p495]
- What we learn from the past is to maximize the quality of future memories, not future experience—a tyranny of the remembering self. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p493]
- Duration neglect applies to evaluating entire lives: multiplying the duration of Jen's life by two had no effect on how pleasant her life was judged to be. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p501]

## Inferences

- Inference: The two selves distinction has important implications for policy-making regarding population well-being.
- Inference: Since the remembering self controls both what we learn from life and the decisions we make, a rational actor model that ignores these systematic distortions fails to describe actual human choice behavior.
- Inference: The two selves can conflict: the experiencing self suffers during a painful colonoscopy while the remembering self may evaluate it more positively if the ending is mild, leading to choices that prioritize memory quality over actual pain reduction.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Full discussion of the two selves occurs in Part V of the book, which extends beyond the current chunk.
- Uncertain: Whether duration neglect applies equally to all types of experiences—some evidence suggests duration matters for childbirth and vacation when quality of ending changes with duration, but the general principle that duration is ignored in retrospective evaluation appears robust.
