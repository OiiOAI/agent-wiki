# Kin Selection

## Summary

Kin selection is an evolutionary theory proposing that altruistic behavior evolves when it benefits genetically related individuals, even at cost to the altruist. Hamilton's rule (C < R × B) quantifies when altruism is favored by natural selection based on genetic relatedness and cost-benefit ratios.

## Key facts

- Hamilton's rule states that altruism evolves when C < R × B: cost to actor is less than genetic relatedness times benefit to recipient. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p90]
- Parents share 50% of DNA with children and siblings, explaining why self-sacrifice for close relatives can be genetically advantageous. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p93]
- Grandchildren and nieces/nephews share 25% of DNA, making kin selection a limited restraint on selfish competitive behavior. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p93]
- Hamilton's two 1964 papers are described as among the most important contributions to social ethology, introducing the concept that genes for altruism spread through kin selection. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p120]
- A gene for suicidal saving of five cousins would not spread, but a gene for saving five siblings (or ten first cousins) would, because rB must exceed C. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p124]
- The relatedness coefficient between full siblings is 0.5; between parent and child is exactly 0.5; between first cousins is 0.125; between identical twins is 1.0. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p123]
- Parental care is a special case of kin selection: an adult should theoretically care for a sibling as much as a child, since relatedness is equal (0.5). [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p124]
- Kin selection predicts that animals should be more altruistic toward maternal relatives than paternal ones, because maternity is more certain than paternity. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p138]
- Kin selection is not a special case of group selection; it is a consequence of gene selection acting on differential replication of alleles. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p125]
- Brian Bertram calculated average relatedness in lion prides: males within a pride average 0.22 (slightly less than half-brothers), females average 0.15 (slightly more than first cousins). [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p137]
- E.O. Wilson changed his definition of kin selection to include offspring as 'kin', though he noted the term is ordinarily used only if other relatives are also affected. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p346]
- Marshall Sahlins raised an 'epistemological' objection claiming that humans cannot calculate coefficients of relationship because primitive languages lack fractions. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p346]
- Dawkins rebuts Sahlins by analogy: snail shells exhibit logarithmic spirals without the snail needing to 'know' logarithms, just as embryological development requires complex mathematics without organisms performing calculations. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p347]
- Kin recognition in animals involves remarkably subtle abilities to discriminate relatives from nonrelatives, often by smell, with at least circumstantial evidence that humans can use nonverbal cues including the smell of relatives' sweat. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p347]
- Every human may carry on average two lethal recessives in the gene pool; mating with a full sibling means one in eight offspring will be born dead or die young for each lethal recessive possessed. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p347]
- Selection for active incest-avoidance could be as strong as any selection pressure that has been measured in nature. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p347]
- In many lion prides the two male lions are not related, challenging Bertram's emphasis on kin selection as the prime mover of cooperation in lions; Packer and Pusey suggest reciprocal altruism may be equally important. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p348]
- A guaranteed monogamous mother is as genetically valuable to you as an identical twin, because she is a full sibling-producing machine. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p348]
- In ant species where females mate only once and store sperm for life, a male can be sure his father's sperm are living on after him, making the mother an excellent genetic bet regardless of whether the father is dead. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p349]
- The 'mother's brother effect' that Dawkins predicted was already well known to anthropologists; Richard Alexander had made the same hypothesis earlier. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p349]

## Inferences

- Inference: Parental altruism is more common than sibling altruism partly because parents can be more certain of their children's relatedness (due to observable birth) than siblings can be of each other.
- Inference: Maternal grandmothers should be more altruistic than paternal grandmothers because they have certainty about their daughter's children but uncertainty about their son's children (paternity may be cuckolded).
- Inference: Dawkins argues that lifetime sperm-storage in insects may be at least as important as haplodiploidy in predisposing species to eusociality, because it makes mothers genetically equivalent to identical twins.
- Inference: Dawkins suggests that cooperation in lions may have started through kin effects that provided conditions for reciprocation to evolve, reconciling both Bertram's and Packer/Pusey's views.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The prediction that maternal uncles should be more altruistic than 'fathers' in species with high marital infidelity lacks direct evidence according to Dawkins.
- Uncertain: Whether adoption of orphans by non-relatives is always a 'misfiring' of kin-selected instincts or could have alternative explanations.
- Uncertain: The exact mechanism of kin recognition in humans remains uncertain; Wells' chapter provides only circumstantial evidence.
- Uncertain: Whether naked mole rat colonies produce dispersing reproductives with distinct morphology remains unknown.
