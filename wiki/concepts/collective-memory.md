# Collective Memory

## Summary

Collective memory is a concept developed by French philosopher Maurice Halbwachs in the mid-twentieth century, describing how personal memories are constantly being influenced and reconfigured through social interactions with family, friends, and communities. Research shows that storytelling with others reshapes our memories by allowing them to reinterpret our experiences from different perspectives. The memories constructed through social interactions contribute to identity and sense of self, but collective memory is selective and can reflect biases, stereotypes, and the perspectives of dominant voices.

## Key facts

- French philosopher Maurice Halbwachs developed the concept of collective memory in the mid-twentieth century. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p186]
- Forty percent of conversational time is spent on storytelling and creating or exchanging collective memories. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p185]
- The very act of sharing past experiences can significantly change what we remember and the meaning we derive from it. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p186]
- Developmental psychologist Robyn Fivush found that children whose mothers asked open-ended questions and elaborated on answers remembered more and developed more coherent narratives. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p187]
- Children encouraged to have a voice in family reminiscing develop more ownership over their sense of self. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p187]
- Teenagers from families whose dinner-table conversations include shared reminiscences about family experiences are less likely to be anxious or depressed. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p188]
- Frederic Bartlett's serial reproduction experiments showed that as memories were passed from person to person, drawings of an African shield gradually began to look like a man's face. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p194]
- In serial reproduction, stereotype-consistent information survives in memories while inconsistent information is lost, reinforcing preconceived biases. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p195]
- There is a negativity bias in social transmission: negative information is more likely to survive while positive information is more likely to be lost. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p195]
