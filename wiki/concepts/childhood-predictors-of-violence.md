# Childhood Predictors of Violence

## Summary

Warning signs and risk factors that may precede violence by young people, based on de Becker's work with cases including James Vance (Judas Priest case), Jason Massey, and child parricide cases. The book examines the role of child abuse, media exposure, and the absence of positive adult intervention.

## Key facts

- The single most reliable pre-incident indicator of parricide is child abuse. [raw/books/psychology/The Gift of Fear - Gavin de Becker.pdf#p146]
- Established risk factors for future criminality include: poverty, child abuse (violence, witnessing violence, humiliation, neglect), drug addiction in a parent, drug/alcohol abuse by the child, single-parent childhood, and absence of a father. [raw/books/psychology/The Gift of Fear - Gavin de Becker.pdf#p149]
- A Federal research project followed 1,600 abused or neglected children for nearly twenty years; as of last year measured, half had been arrested for some crime. [raw/books/psychology/The Gift of Fear - Gavin de Becker.pdf#p152]
- James Vance stated that 'violence excited me' and that 'TV is a really good teacher' for learning violence. [raw/books/psychology/The Gift of Fear - Gavin de Becker.pdf#p144]
- The book notes that 80 percent of young men in juvenile detention facilities were raised without fully participating fathers. [raw/books/psychology/The Gift of Fear - Gavin de Becker.pdf#p149]
- Jason Massey exhibited multiple warning signs: idolizing serial killers, studying Charles Manson, avidly following Slayer music, slaughtering animals and keeping skulls, and stalking a teenage girl for five years. [raw/books/psychology/The Gift of Fear - Gavin de Becker.pdf#p146]

## Inferences

- Inference: The book suggests that kindness and recognition from adults during significant periods can redirect potential violent offenders, citing de Becker's own experience with a teacher who 'fought monsters' in him.
- Inference: Children who kill parents often did so only after years of severe abuse, making parental behavior—not the child's inherent nature—the primary intervention point.
