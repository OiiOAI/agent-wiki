# The Macbeth Effect

## Summary

The psychological phenomenon where contemplating or committing immoral acts produces a metaphorical sense of dirtiness that manifests as increased desire for physical cleansing. The brain has trouble distinguishing between being a 'dirty scoundrel' and being in need of a bath, with the effect being specific to the body part associated with the transgression.

## Key facts

- Contemplating one's moral failings increases desire for cleansing products—participants who recounted immoral acts were more likely to choose antiseptic wipes over pencils as a thank-you gift. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p578]
- When people lie with their mouths, they prefer mouthwash afterward; when they lie in writing, they prefer hand soap—the effect is embodied and specific to the transgressing body part. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p578]
- East Asian subjects show the Macbeth effect through face-washing rather than hand-washing, reflecting cultural concepts of 'saving face.'. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p578]
- Washing hands after contemplating moral failures reduces subsequent helpfulness, suggesting that prosocial acts often function as restitution that is eliminated by literal cleansing. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p578]

## Inferences

- Inference: The Macbeth effect demonstrates that moral and physical purity are neurologically intertwined in ways that affect actual behavior, not just subjective feelings.
