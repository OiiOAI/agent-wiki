# Camera Eyes

## Summary

Camera eyes—with a lens that focuses an image on a retina—evolved independently in vertebrates and cephalopods. This represents one of the most striking cases of convergent evolution, where distantly related animals arrived at similar solutions to the problem of forming images. The human-cephalopod common ancestor had at most simple eyespots, not camera eyes.

## Key facts

- Vertebrates and cephalopods separately evolved camera eyes with a lens that focuses an image on a retina. [raw/books/neuroscience/Other Minds_ The Octopus, the Sea, and the - Peter Godfrey - Smith.pdf#p60]
- Our common ancestor may have had a pair of eyespots but did not have eyes anything like ours or those of cephalopods. [raw/books/neuroscience/Other Minds_ The Octopus, the Sea, and the - Peter Godfrey - Smith.pdf#p60]
- The capacity for learning of several kinds seems to have been invented independently several times in evolution. [raw/books/neuroscience/Other Minds_ The Octopus, the Sea, and the - Peter Godfrey - Smith.pdf#p61]

## Inferences

- Inference: Two experiments in the evolution of large nervous systems independently landed on similar ways of seeing, suggesting camera eyes represent an optimal solution to visual sensing.
- Inference: The octopus's camera eyes are similar to ours but the brains behind them are 'different on almost every scale.'
