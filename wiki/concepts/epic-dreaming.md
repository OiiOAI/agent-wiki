# Epic Dreaming

## Summary

Epic dreaming is a largely understudied form of disordered dreaming first described by Schenck and Mahowald in 1995. It involves nights filled with long, tedious dreams of repetitive physical activity (like endless housework or trudging through snow) that result in daytime fatigue despite apparently adequate sleep. Emotions within epic dreams are usually neutral or absent, and treatments have proven largely ineffective.

## Key facts

- Epic dreaming was first described in 1995 by Schenck and Mahowald, the same researchers who identified REM sleep behavior disorder. [raw/books/neuroscience/When Brains Dream - Antonio Zadra.pdf#p226]
- Epic dreaming affects women more than men, with sleep lab assessments usually coming up clinically normal. [raw/books/neuroscience/When Brains Dream - Antonio Zadra.pdf#p226]
- Emotions within epic dreams are usually described as neutral or entirely absent, despite the seemingly relentless and exhausting nature of the dreams. [raw/books/neuroscience/When Brains Dream - Antonio Zadra.pdf#p226]
- Psychological, behavioral, and pharmacological treatments for epic dreaming have proven largely ineffective. [raw/books/neuroscience/When Brains Dream - Antonio Zadra.pdf#p226]

## Inferences

- Inference: Epic dreaming remains unexplained and its relationship to NEXTUP or other dream functions is unclear, representing a gap in understanding of sleep and dream disorders.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Why epic dreamers feel exhausted despite normal-appearing sleep, and what role these patterns play in clinical symptoms, remains unanswered.
