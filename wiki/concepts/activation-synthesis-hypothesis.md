# Activation-Synthesis Hypothesis

## Summary

The activation-synthesis hypothesis, proposed by Allan Hobson and Robert McCarley of Harvard Medical School in 1977, is a neurobiological model of dreaming. The theory proposes that dreaming is triggered by the 'largely random' firing of giant neurons in the pontine reticular formation (PRF) of the brainstem, which stimulates the visual cortex and initiates rapid eye movements. The forebrain then responds by attempting to construct a narrative to explain these visual sensations, essentially 'making the best of a bad job' in producing coherent dream imagery from noisy signals.

## Key facts

- The activation-synthesis hypothesis was proposed by Allan Hobson and Robert McCarley of Harvard Medical School in 1977. [raw/books/neuroscience/When Brains Dream - Antonio Zadra.pdf#p98]
- The hypothesis proposes that dreaming is triggered by the 'largely random' firing of giant neurons in the pontine reticular formation (PRF) of the brainstem. [raw/books/neuroscience/When Brains Dream - Antonio Zadra.pdf#p98]
- The PRF plays a role in the regulation of REM sleep, and its firing during REM sleep stimulates the visual cortex while initiating rapid eye movements. [raw/books/neuroscience/When Brains Dream - Antonio Zadra.pdf#p98]
- According to Hobson, the dream is the result of the forebrain 'making the best of a bad job in producing even partially coherent dream imagery from the relatively noisy signals sent up to it from the brainstem.'. [raw/books/neuroscience/When Brains Dream - Antonio Zadra.pdf#p98]
- The activation-synthesis model is largely seen today as proclaiming dreams to be random and meaningless, though Hobson and McCarley also suggested a functional role for dreaming sleep in promoting learning. [raw/books/neuroscience/When Brains Dream - Antonio Zadra.pdf#p98]

## Inferences

- Inference: The theory was overtly anti-Freudian, focusing on how it differed from Freud's dream theory while largely glossing over areas of agreement.
