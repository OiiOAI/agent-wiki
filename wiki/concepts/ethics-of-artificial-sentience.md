# Ethics of Artificial Sentience

## Summary

Solms outlines ethical concerns surrounding artificial consciousness, including risks of exploitation resembling slavery, questions of suffering and rights, and potential dangers from machines combining intelligence with self-preservative motivation. He argues artificial consciousness should not be commercially funded and proposes three steps if successful: switch off the machine, patent the neural correlate in a non-profit's name, and convene a symposium.

## Key facts

- Solms argues that to the extent artificial consciousness might be used for financial gain, there is risk of facilitating a new form of slavery. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p264]
- Questions arise about whether conscious machines should have 'rights' to life and liberty; the concept of 'robot rights' is already established and considered by the Institute for the Future in the US and the Department of Trade and Industry in the UK. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p265]
- Key ethical questions include whether it is justified to deliberately place machines in distressing situations to demonstrate aversive responses, and on what grounds it would be ethical to switch them off. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p265]
- Solms raises concern that consciousness combined with self-preservative motivation poses dangers not only to humans but to all other life forms seen as competitors for resources. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p265]
- If consciousness can be engineered, Solms argues it will be done somewhere regardless; therefore he advocates proceeding without commercial funding to maintain ethical control. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p267]
- Solms proposes three steps upon success: (1) switch off the machine and remove its battery, (2) patent the neural correlate in a non-profit's name, (3) convene a symposium to make recommendations. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p267-268]
- Switching off a non-living conscious machine should not entail its demise; it should be possible to switch it on again, analogous to sleep and waking. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p268]
- The plan aligns with the 'Termination Obligation' clause in the Universal Guidelines for Artificial Intelligence (2018). [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p268]

## Inferences

- Inference: Solms' ethical framework prioritises preventing suffering and exploitation over commercial applications, reflecting his view that consciousness entails the capacity for suffering.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Whether the biological analogy between switching off and sleep/waking fully applies to artificial systems is not definitively established.
