# Moral Sense in Animals

## Summary

Gazzaniga examines whether non-human animals possess a moral sense. Many social species share behaviors foundational to morality—territoriality, dominance, coalition formation, and reciprocity—and show emotional reactions (anger at property violations, fear responses) that parallel human moral intuitions. However, self-conscious emotions like shame and guilt may require self-awareness not yet demonstrated in other animals.

## Key facts

- Humans share with other social species the emotional reactions to property violations and coalition attacks, including anger and fear responses. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p164]
- Self-conscious emotions (shame, guilt, embarrassment) require self-awareness beyond recognizing one's visible body; this expanded self-awareness has not been demonstrated in other animals. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p165]
- Dogs that appear ashamed when owners return home are likely showing fear of the owner's anger (submissive behavior), not true guilt—emotional reactions without the complex self-evaluative component. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p165]
- Patient X with destroyed visual cortex but intact amygdala can guess emotional expressions of angry or happy faces despite being functionally blind, demonstrating automatic emotional simulation. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p167]

## Inferences

- Inference: The human capacity to interpret and value-judge automatic moral reactions is uniquely human, suggesting a two-stage process where moral emotions evolved first, followed by the interpretive function.
