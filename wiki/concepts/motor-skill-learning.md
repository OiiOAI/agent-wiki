# Motor-Skill Learning

## Summary

Motor-skill learning is the nondeclarative memory process by which we acquire new physical skills and habits, such as riding a bicycle or tracing figures in a mirror. This type of learning is preserved in patients with medial temporal lobe damage because it depends on brain circuits distinct from the hippocampus.

## Key facts

- In 1955, Brenda Milner demonstrated that Henry could learn the mirror-tracing task, improving over three consecutive days despite having no conscious memory of previous sessions. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p154]
- Henry's mirror-tracing improvement was dramatic: on day one his errors dropped steadily, on day two his initial scores matched day one's end scores, and on day three he traced nearly perfectly. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p154]
- Henry said after his last mirror-tracing session, 'Well, this is strange. I thought that that would be difficult, but it seems as though I've done it quite well,' showing no declarative memory of previous attempts. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p155]
- To learn a new motor skill, the task must be performed repeatedly; once attained, motor skills are enduring, hence the saying about never forgetting how to ride a bicycle. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p156]
- Henry demonstrated clear improvement from trial to trial on rotary pursuit, bimanual tracking, and coordinated tapping tasks, despite his profound amnesia. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p179]
- Henry retained the new motor skill for one week with no additional training on the rotary pursuit task. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p179]
- Henry's suboptimal performance on rotary pursuit and bimanual tracking was due partly to Phenobarbital, a sedative that caused slow movement. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p180]
- The caudate nucleus plays a critical role in sequence-specific learning, while the putamen supports other motor skills. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p164]
- The cerebellum integrates perceptual and motor information to accommodate changes in the visual environment. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p165]

## Inferences

- Inference: Motor-skill learning depends on brain areas outside the medial temporal lobe, specifically circuits involved in motor control and procedural memory.
- Inference: Motor-skill learning does not require the hippocampus, indicating that different memory systems support different types of learning.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The exact contribution of residual cerebellar tissue to Henry's preserved motor learning remains unclear.
