# Tit for Tat

## Summary

Tit for Tat is the winning strategy in Axelrod's computer tournaments for the Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma. It begins by cooperating on the first move and thereafter simply copies the opponent's previous move. The strategy is characterized by three key properties: niceness (never being the first to defect), forgivingness (having a short memory for past misdeeds), and non-enviousness (not striving to beat the opponent but rather to achieve high mutual scores).

## Key facts

- Tit for Tat begins by cooperating on the first move and thereafter simply copies the previous move of the other player. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p249]
- Tit for Tat achieved an average score of 504.5 points, 84% of the benchmark score, winning both of Axelrod's tournaments. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p251]
- The eight top-scoring strategies were all 'nice' strategies that never defected first; the seven 'nasty' strategies trailed behind. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p251]
- Tit for Tat is 'forgiving'—it raps a defector over the knuckles instantly but then lets bygones be bygones. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p251]
- The winning strategy was submitted by Anatol Rapoport, a psychologist and games theorist from Toronto. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p249]

## Inferences

- Inference: Tit for Tat cannot score more than its opponent in any particular game because it never defects except in retaliation
- Inference: Tit for Tat enjoys a kind of 'higher-order stability'—unlike Always Defect, it can benefit from local clustering in populations
