# Attention to Variability in Healthcare

## Summary

Langer advocates for medical professionals to attend to the variability in patients' symptoms, demeanor, and overall well-being rather than relying on rigid checklists or preconceived categories. This approach keeps everyone mindful and engaged, reduces burnout for doctors and nurses, makes patients feel heard, and uses information about changes to improve patient care and speed recovery.

## Key facts

- In mindful hospitals, medical professionals would be taught to attend to the variability in their patients' symptoms, demeanor, and overall well-being. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p180]
- Attending to variability keeps everyone mindful and engaged, which Langer argues would reduce burnout for doctors and nurses. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p181]
- Arzy et al. found that when doctors were given just one misleading detail, it led them down the wrong path in diagnosis 90 percent of the time. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p182]
- Atul Gawande pioneered surgical checklists which, across approximately 1,000 procedures in 8 hospitals, reduced errors by 50 percent. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p182]
- Langer suggests that instead of yes/no checklists, more nuanced questions like 'How alert is the patient?' or 'How dilated are the patient's pupils?' would lead to closer inspection. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p183]

## Inferences

- Inference: The suggestion to use continuous scales rather than binary yes/no responses may extend to many areas of medical assessment, potentially improving diagnostic accuracy.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Whether the benefits of nuanced questioning would outweigh the additional time required in busy clinical settings.
