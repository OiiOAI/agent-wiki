# Illusion of Validity

## Summary

A cognitive illusion in which people maintain high confidence in their judgments despite evidence of their inaccuracy. First identified by Kahneman during his military service, the illusion of validity occurs when coherent and fluent narratives about individuals lead to overconfident predictions that ignore base rates and regression to the mean.

## Key facts

- Kahneman first identified the illusion of validity while serving in the Israeli military, where he and colleagues made confident predictions about leadership potential based on assessment exercises that later proved to have almost no predictive validity. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p271]
- Despite being shown that their predictions were barely better than random guessing, Kahneman and colleagues continued to feel and act as if each prediction were valid. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p273]
- The illusion of validity is driven by the WYSIATI principle and the representativeness heuristic, where a coherent story leads to high subjective confidence that does not reflect actual predictive accuracy. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p273]
- The illusion persisted because knowing the statistical rule did not influence the feeling of validity for individual cases, paralleling how Nisbett and Borgida's participants ignored base rate statistics when evaluating specific individuals. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p273]

## Inferences

- Inference: The illusion of validity illustrates that cognitive biases can persist even when people intellectually accept contradictory evidence, because the feeling of knowing is produced by System 1's coherence-making processes rather than by rational evaluation of accuracy.
