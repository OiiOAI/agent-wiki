# Chain-Link Systems

## Summary

A system has chain-link logic when its performance is limited by its weakest subunit or 'link.' When there is a weak link, a chain is not made stronger by strengthening other links. Chain-linked systems can become stuck in low-effectiveness states due to quality matching—investing in improving one link yields no payoff if other links remain weak. This explains both how organizations become stuck and how excellent organizations like IKEA maintain sustained advantage.

## Key facts

- A system has chain-link logic when its performance is limited by its weakest subunit; if a link must not fail, there is no point in strengthening only some of the links. [raw/books/strategy/Good strategy, bad strategy _ the difference and why it - Richard P_ Rumelt.pdf#p50]
- Quality matters when quantity is an inadequate substitute—a three-star chef cannot be replaced by any number of short-order cooks. [raw/books/strategy/Good strategy, bad strategy _ the difference and why it - Richard P_ Rumelt.pdf#p50]
- In chain-linked systems, striving for higher quality in just one linked unit may make matters worse because improvements to one link will not improve overall performance. [raw/books/strategy/Good strategy, bad strategy _ the difference and why it - Richard P_ Rumelt.pdf#p51]
- To unstick a chain-linked system, a strong leader must possess the insight and fortitude to make necessary investments in each link of the chain. [raw/books/strategy/Good strategy, bad strategy _ the difference and why it - Richard P_ Rumelt.pdf#p52]

## Inferences

- Inference: KEA's sustained competitive advantage comes from chain-link logic—its many policies fit together in a coherent design such that adopting only one policy does no good for competitors.
