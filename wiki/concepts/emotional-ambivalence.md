# Emotional Ambivalence

## Summary

Emotional ambivalence refers to the coexistence of opposing emotional attitudes (such as love and hate, or affection and hostility) toward the same object or person. Freud employed this concept, coined by Bleuler, to explain the psychological basis of taboo prohibitions and the treatment of the dead.

## Key facts

- The term 'ambivalence' was coined by Bleuler (1910b) to describe the coexistence of opposing emotional attitudes toward a single object. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p4145]
- In obsessional neurosis, the prohibition and the persistent desire remain separate: the prohibition is conscious while the desire to touch is unconscious and unknown to the subject. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p4145]
- Freud argued that primitive peoples exhibit an ambivalent attitude toward their taboos: in their unconscious there is nothing they would like more than to violate them, but they are afraid to do so because the fear is stronger than the desire. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p4147]
- The coexistence of affectionate and hostile feelings toward the dead person leads to the survivor's conflict, which is dealt with through projection: the unconscious hostility is ejected from internal perception into the external world, transforming the dead into a demon. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p4172]
- Emotional ambivalence in the proper sense—simultaneous existence of love and hate towards the same object—lies at the root of many important cultural institutions. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p4253]
- The tension of ambivalence in the father complex persists in totemism and in religions generally. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p4244]
- Freud considered the possibility that ambivalence was not a fundamental phenomenon but was acquired by the human race in connection with their father complex. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p4253]
- Totemic religion not only comprised expressions of remorse and attempts at atonement, but also served as a remembrance of the triumph over the father. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p4244]

## Inferences

- Inference: Freud's analysis suggests that ambivalence is a fundamental feature of human emotional life, manifesting not only in individual psychology but also in social and cultural phenomena like taboo and ancestor worship.
- Inference: The ambivalence inherent in the father complex creates a perpetual tension in religious and social institutions, as these structures must simultaneously express both submission to paternal authority and the memory of having overcome it.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Freud acknowledged uncertainty about whether ambivalence is a fundamental phenomenon of emotional life or was acquired through the father complex. He noted: 'We know nothing of the origin of this ambivalence.'
