# Executive Intent

## Summary

Executive intent is what a leader wants to accomplish when assigning a task. Adapted from the military concept of 'commander's intent,' it involves explaining not just what subordinates should do but why. This enables subordinates to improvise and adapt when they encounter unexpected obstacles.

## Key facts

- If subordinates don't understand the intent, there's a greater chance for unexpected obstacles to derail the plan because they won't know how to adapt appropriately. [raw/books/strategy/The Power of Intuition - Gary Klein, Ph.D.pdf#p250]
- When subordinates understand the intent, they can react to events without waiting for permission and can recognize opportunities for achieving goals even if they weren't part of the original plan. [raw/books/strategy/The Power of Intuition - Gary Klein, Ph.D.pdf#p250]
- In Shattuck's experiment, only 34 percent of company commanders' responses matched the expectations of their battalion commanders when faced with unexpected events. [raw/books/strategy/The Power of Intuition - Gary Klein, Ph.D.pdf#p249]
- The defining feature of information is that it reduces uncertainty; stating 'customer satisfaction is my number-one priority' is public relations, not information. [raw/books/strategy/The Power of Intuition - Gary Klein, Ph.D.pdf#p252]

## Inferences

- Inference: Executive intent becomes especially critical in fast-moving or ambiguous situations where detailed instructions would become obsolete before they could be executed.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The book acknowledges that while executive intent is important, it has not received the same level of attention in the corporate world as it has in the military.
