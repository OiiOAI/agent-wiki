# Medium, Substances, and Surfaces

## Summary

The triad of medium, substances, and surfaces constitutes Gibson's ecological description of the environment, replacing the physicist's description of bodies in space. The medium (air or water) permits locomotion and the transmission of light, sound, and odor. Substances are substantial (more or less rigid, opaque, and heterogeneous). Surfaces separate the medium from substances and have characteristic properties including layout, texture, reflectance, and color.

## Key facts

- The planet consists mainly of earth, water, and air—a solid, a liquid, and a gas. The earth-air interface is the most important surface for terrestrial animals: the ground. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p43]
- The medium permits locomotion, is filled with illumination for vision, transmits vibrations for hearing, and allows chemical diffusion for smelling. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p44]
- The medium has an intrinsic polarity of up and down due to gravity and the fact that radiant light comes from above. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p45]
- Substances are more or less rigid, impenetrable, and permanent in shape, usually opaque to light, and heterogeneous unlike the homogeneous medium. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p46]
- The surface is where most of the action is: where light is reflected or absorbed, where things touch the animal, where chemical reactions take place, and where vibrations are transmitted. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p49]
