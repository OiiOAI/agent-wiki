# Conflict of Interest

## Summary

Conflicts of interest are influences that bias evaluation and decision-making, extending beyond financial interests to include cognitive biases such as confirmation bias, outcome bias, self-serving attributions, and social comparison. Our brains have 'built-in conflicts of interest' interpreting the world to confirm existing beliefs and protect self-image. The 1967 Harvard sugar industry study—where researchers paid by a sugar trade group blamed fat rather than sugar for heart disease—demonstrates how undisclosed conflicts can cause lasting harm to public health and decision quality.

## Key facts

- In 1967, three Harvard scientists published a paper in the New England Journal of Medicine blaming fat for heart disease, later revealed to have been paid for by a sugar industry trade group. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking_in_Bets.epub#L1325-L1477]
- The Harvard researchers attacked methodology of studies finding sugar links to heart disease while defending studies finding no such link. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking_in_Bets.epub#L1325-L1477]
- Had the conflict of interest been disclosed, the scientific community would have viewed the conclusions with much more skepticism. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking_in_Bets.epub#L1325-L1477]
- Our brains have built-in conflicts of interest: confirming beliefs, avoiding admission of error, taking credit for good results, and blaming external factors for bad results. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking_in_Bets.epub#L1325-L1477]
- At the time of the 1967 paper, the NEJM did not require conflict of interest disclosures; that policy changed in 1984. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking_in_Bets.epub#L1325-L1477]
