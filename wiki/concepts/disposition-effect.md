# Disposition Effect

## Summary

The disposition effect is the tendency of investors to sell assets that have increased in value (winners) while holding assets that have declined in value (losers). It arises from mental accounting combined with loss aversion: investors want to close each mental account with a positive balance and experience the realization of a loss as more painful than the pleasure of realizing a gain. This bias systematically reduces portfolio returns.

## Key facts

- Research in financial markets documents a strong preference for selling winners over losers even though tax considerations and rational portfolio theory both favor selling losers to harvest tax losses and maintaining winners that have demonstrated upward momentum. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p444]
- The disposition effect is a specific case of narrow framing: investors create separate mental accounts for each stock purchase and prefer to close each with a positive balance, ignoring the portfolio-level perspective that a rational investor would adopt. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p444]
- US investors show a pronounced December effect where losing stocks are sold specifically to harvest tax losses, but the remaining 11 months are dominated by mental accounting, demonstrating the seasonal override of financial rationality by emotional accounting. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p445]
- One estimate suggests the average annual cost of the disposition effect is approximately 3.4% in foregone returns from holding losing positions too long and selling winning positions too early. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p445]

## Inferences

- Inference: The disposition effect creates an asymmetry between realized and unrealized gains/losses: investors who monitor their portfolios frequently are more susceptible because they encounter the emotional pain of paper losses more often, reinforcing the bias.
