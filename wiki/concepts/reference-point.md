# Reference Point

## Summary

In Prospect Theory, the reference point is the neutral baseline against which gains and losses are evaluated. Typically it is the status quo (current wealth), but it can also be an aspirational level such as an expected or deserved outcome. Outcomes above the reference point are coded as gains; outcomes below it are coded as losses. The reference point determines what counts as a loss versus a gain, and shifts in the reference point change preferences even when objective wealth is identical.

## Key facts

- In Prospect Theory, outcomes above the reference point are coded as gains and outcomes below it as losses; the reference point is usually the status quo but can also be an expected or deserved outcome such as a promised raise or a colleague's bonus. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p364]
- Problems 3 and 4 in the book illustrate that identical final wealth states produce different risk preferences depending on the reference point: after receiving a $1,000 gift most people prefer certainty, but after receiving a $2,000 gift most gamble—because the certain option is coded as a $500 gain in problem 3 and a $500 loss in problem 4. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p363]
- Standard indifference curves omit the reference point, creating a theoretical blind spot comparable to Bernoulli's error: preferences are assumed to depend only on current state, ignoring the history and starting point that actually influence evaluations. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p375]
- Prospect Theory cannot handle disappointment: when a large gain is expected, 'nothing' is experienced as a significant loss relative to the temporary new reference point, but the theory assigns the same zero value to 'nothing' in all contexts. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p371]

## Inferences

- Inference: The reference point in negotiations is typically the existing contract, and loss aversion creates an asymmetry where concessions feel more painful to the giver than they feel beneficial to the receiver, making deal-making more difficult when the pie is shrinking.
