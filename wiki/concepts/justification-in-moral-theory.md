# Justification in Moral Theory

## Summary

Rawls outlines his method of justifying the principles of justice, rejecting both the Cartesian approach (self-evident principles from which standards are derived) and naturalistic approaches (definitions of moral concepts in non-moral terms). Instead, he treats moral theory like any other theory, where justification is a matter of the mutual support of many considerations into one coherent view, achieved through reflective equilibrium between principles and considered judgments. The three parts of the exposition—theoretical structure, institutional analysis, and stability considerations—support one another and together justify the principles chosen.

## Key facts

- Justification is not based on self-evident principles or naturalistic definitions, but is a matter of the mutual support of many considerations fitting together into one coherent view. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p511]
- To justify a conception of justice to someone is to give them a proof of its principles from premises that both parties accept, with conclusions matching considered judgments. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p512]
- The argument for principles of justice proceeds from a consensus that includes representative theories from the tradition of moral philosophy. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p513]
- Publicity is explained as ensuring that the process of justification can be perfectly carried through without self-defeating or disturbing consequences. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p514]
- The purity of heart that one could attain is to see clearly and to act with grace from the perspective of the original position viewed sub specie aeternitatis. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p518]

## Inferences

- Inference: Rawls's method does not require that first principles be self-evident or necessary truths; they are chosen in the original position in the light of general facts.
