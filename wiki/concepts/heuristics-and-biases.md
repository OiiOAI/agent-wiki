# Heuristics and Biases

## Summary

Heuristics and biases refer to the research program pioneered by Kahneman and Tversky examining how people make judgments under uncertainty. Heuristics are cognitive shortcuts (simplifying strategies) that usually work but systematically deviate from normative rules. Biases are these systematic errors that occur predictably in specific situations. The program documented dozens of biases including overconfidence, base rate neglect, availability bias, and representativeness.

## Key facts

- Heuristics are simplifying strategies used to make intuitive judgments when facing complex problems. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p14]
- Reliance on heuristics causes systematic biases in predictions and probability judgments. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p14]
- The 1974 Science article 'Judgment Under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases' documented approximately 20 biases as manifestations of three main heuristics. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p15]
- The heuristics and biases approach has been productively applied in medicine, law, intelligence analysis, philosophy, finance, and military strategy. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p15]
- Intuitive predictions match representative descriptions even when statistical base rates contradict them. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p14]
- Representativeness: people judge probability by how much A resembles B, leading to insensitivity to base rates, insensitivity to sample size, and the gambler's fallacy. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p544]
- The law of small numbers: people expect even small samples to be highly representative of their parent populations, causing excessive confidence in findings from small samples and overestimation of replicability. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p549]
- Availability: people judge frequency by ease of retrieving examples, leading to biases from recency, vividness, and search set—explaining whyplane crashes seem more common than car accidents despite the opposite being true. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p554]
- Anchoring: insufficient adjustment from starting values affects expert estimates (e.g., estimating the number of countries in the UN from a wheel-of-fortune spin) and occurs even when the anchor is obviously random. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p558]
- Regression to the mean: because extreme outcomes are partly due to luck, subsequent measurements tend to be less extreme. People fail to anticipate this because they expect extreme outcomes to produce equally extreme follow-ups. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p552]
- The illusion of validity: people express high confidence in predictions based on high representativeness even when the evidence has low predictive validity, because consistency of pattern is mistaken for validity of prediction. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p551]

## Inferences

- Inference: The heuristics and biases research program fundamentally challenged the assumption that humans are generally rational.
- Inference: The three heuristics are not irrational per se—they are adaptive shortcuts that usually work well. The contribution of Tversky and Kahneman was to identify the specific conditions under which each heuristic produces systematic, predictable errors.
