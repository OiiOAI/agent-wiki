# Gene-Culture Coevolution

## Summary

A process by which genetic and cultural evolution influence each other, each acting as a selective environment for the other. Richerson and Boyd argue that human evolution became a 'two-stranded affair' once shared intentionality emerged—cultural innovations altered which traits were adaptive, which in turn altered genetic evolution. Examples include lactose tolerance evolving in response to cattle domestication, and potentially moral emotions evolving in response to increasingly complex social environments created by cultural practices. This theory challenges earlier claims that human evolution essentially stopped 50,000 years ago.

## Key facts

- Richerson and Boyd argue that gene-culture coevolution helped move humanity from small-group sociability to tribal ultrasociality. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p232]
- Genetic evolution accelerated during the last 50,000 years, reaching a crescendo during the Holocene era (last 12,000 years). [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p237]
- The 'tribal instincts hypothesis' proposes that humans evolved psychological mechanisms expecting social worlds structured by moral norms and divided into symbolically marked groups. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p232]
- Early humans may have 'self-domesticated' by selecting partners based on ability to live within the tribe's moral matrix, showing traits similar to domesticated animals. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p233]

## Inferences

- Inference: Gene-culture coevolution may explain why humans are uniquely suited to both individual competition and group cooperation—our psychology was shaped by both genetic and cultural selection pressures.
