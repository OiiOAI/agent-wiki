# Multiple selves models

## Summary

Multiple selves models propose the existence of two largely independent decision-making systems: one associated with 'limbic' emotional areas and another with 'rational' cortical areas. These models are largely unsupported by existing neurobiological data. Both posterior parietal cortex (traditionally considered rational) and basal ganglia (traditionally considered emotional) show activity that predicts choices across all conditions studied, suggesting a unitary system rather than multiple independent selves.

## Key facts

- Multiple selves models propose two largely independent decision-making systems: one limbic/emotional and one rational. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p500]
- LIP activity predicts choices under conditions associated with both rational and emotional decision making. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p500]
- Diseases of the basal ganglia are only very weakly associated with emotional dysfunction. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p500]
- The amygdala projects strongly to the ventral striatum and influences valuation-related activity, but this does not support a Freudian multiple-self model. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroeconomics _ Decision Making and the Brain - Paul W_ Glimcher, Ernst Fehr, Colin Camerer, Antonio Rangel,.pdf#p500]

## Inferences

- Inference: The evidence suggests that existing multiple-self models are largely unsupported, though emotions do influence decision-making through projections to valuation areas.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Whether specific neural substrates for self-control conflicts exist remains an open question.
