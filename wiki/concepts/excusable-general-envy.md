# Excusable General Envy

## Summary

Rawls examines whether the principles of justice are likely to arouse excusable general envy in a well-ordered society. He identifies three conditions that encourage hostile outbreaks of envy: (1) persons lack confidence in their own value and ability; (2) many occasions arise when this psychological condition is experienced as painful and humiliating due to social structures making discrepancies visible; and (3) the less fortunate see their social position as allowing no constructive alternative to opposing the favored circumstances of the advantaged. Rawls argues that a well-ordered society works to mitigate all three conditions, and therefore the principles of justice are not likely to arouse excusable general envy to a troublesome extent.

## Key facts

- The main psychological root of liability to envy is a lack of self-confidence in our own worth combined with a sense of impotence. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p473]
- Three conditions encourage hostile outbreaks of envy: lack of self-confidence, visibility of discrepancies through social structure, and the absence of constructive alternatives. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p473]
- The contract conception of justice supports self-esteem more firmly than other political principles, as each person is treated with respect due to a sovereign equal. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p474]
- The plurality of associations in a well-ordered society tends to reduce the visibility of variations in men's prospects through noncomparing groups. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p475]
- The principles of justice are not likely to arouse excusable general envy to a troublesome extent. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p475]
- Insisting upon equality as defined by the two principles is not giving voice to envy, as shown by the content of these principles and the characterization of envy itself. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p476]

## Inferences

- Inference: Rawls's argument implies that the removal of excusable envy conditions also removes conditions for jealousy, grudgingness, and spite—the converses of envy.
