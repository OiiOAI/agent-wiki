# The Plastic Paradox

## Summary

The plastic paradox is a phenomenon described by Norman Doidge where neuroplasticity renders the brain not only more resourceful but also more vulnerable and capable of producing more rigid behaviors. Once a particular plastic change becomes well established in the brain, it can prevent other changes from occurring, making some stubborn habits and disorders products of neuroplasticity itself.

## Key facts

- Neuroplasticity renders the brain both more resourceful and more vulnerable to outside influences. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p11]
- Neuroplasticity can produce not only more flexible behaviors but also more rigid ones, which the author calls 'the plastic paradox.'. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p11]
- Some of the most stubborn habits and disorders are products of the brain's plasticity. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p11]
- Once a plastic change becomes well established in the brain, it can prevent other changes from occurring. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p11]
- The plastic paradox is that the same neuroplastic properties that allow us to change our brains and produce more flexible behaviors can also allow us to produce more rigid ones. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p247]
- Neuroplasticity is compared to pliable snow on a hill—when we choose the same path repeatedly, tracks develop and soon we get stuck in ruts as neural circuits become self-sustaining. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p247]
- Anything involving unvaried repetition—careers, cultural activities, skills, and neuroses—can lead to rigidity because neuroplastic brains can develop rigid behaviors in the first place. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p247]
- Neuroses become entrenched by force of habit because they involve repeating patterns of which we are not conscious, making them almost impossible to interrupt without special techniques like psychoanalysis. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p247]
- Freud was right that absence of plasticity seemed related to force of habit, and once Mr. L. understood the causes of his defensive habits and his view of himself and the world, he could make use of his innate plasticity despite his age. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p247]
