# Biological naturalism (Searle)

## Summary

John Searle's 'biological naturalism' holds that consciousness is a biological process — subjective, qualitative, and first-personal — that is ontologically irreducible to neurophysiological processes but causally reducible to the brain. Thompson agrees that consciousness is a subjective biological process irreducible in Searle's sense, but argues: (1) we need an expanded notion of 'physical' already for life itself; (2) the irreducibility points to a transcendental status for consciousness beyond the merely empirical; (3) the view that consciousness is entirely reducible to the brain may be biologically premature.

## Key facts

- For Searle, conscious states have a first-person ontology existing only insofar as experienced by a conscious subject, yet are entirely caused by and realized in neurobiological processes. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p229]
- Thompson argues we need an expanded notion of 'physical' already for life: life realizes a kind of interiority — the interiority of selfhood and sense-making — that escapes standard materialist accounts. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p229]
- The irreducibility of consciousness is not trivial for Thompson — it indicates consciousness has a transcendental status as condition of possibility for appearances, requiring a transcendental rather than merely empirical perspective. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p230]
- The brain-in-a-vat thought experiment does not establish that consciousness is entirely causally reducible to the brain; any vat meeting the requirements would likely need to be a surrogate body. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p231]

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Thompson acknowledges we simply do not yet know whether the brain alone is the minimal causal basis for consciousness or whether bodily processes also belong to this minimal sufficient condition.
