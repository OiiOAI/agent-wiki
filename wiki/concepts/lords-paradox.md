# Lord's Paradox

## Summary

Lord's paradox, originally posed by Frederic Lord in 1967, involves conflicting statistical conclusions about whether a diet has differential effects on boys versus girls based on weight gain measurements. Two statisticians reach opposite conclusions using the same data.

## Key facts

- Lord's paradox involves a school studying diet effects on student weight, measuring weight in September and the following June. [raw/books/strategy/The_Book_of_Why.epub#L1840-L1855]
- The first statistician notes that average weight is the same in June as in September for both boys and girls, concluding no differential effect. [raw/books/strategy/The_Book_of_Why.epub#L1857-L1865]
- The second statistician stratifies by initial weight and finds boys gain more weight than girls in each stratum. [raw/books/strategy/The_Book_of_Why.epub#L1867-L1880]
- Lord's pessimism stated that the question cannot be answered rigorously from available data, but Pearl argues this pessimism is unjustified using causal diagrams. [raw/books/strategy/The_Book_of_Why.epub#L1885-L1895]
- The key insight is that the sure-thing principle only works when relative proportions of subpopulations do not change from group to group. [raw/books/strategy/The_Book_of_Why.epub#L1895-L1905]
- Lord's paradox (Lord, 1967) concerns interpreting group comparisons in a way that can lead to contradictory conclusions. [raw/books/strategy/The_Book_of_Why.epub#L3679-3681]
- Versions of Lord's paradox are described in Glymour (2006), Hernández-Díaz, Schisterman, and Hernán (2006), Senn (2006), Wainer (1991), and Wainer and Brown (2007). [raw/books/strategy/The_Book_of_Why.epub#L3679-3681]
- Pearl (2016a) provides a comprehensive analysis of Lord's paradox in the paper titled "Lord's paradox revisited—(Oh Lord! Kumbaya!)". [raw/books/strategy/The_Book_of_Why.epub#L3681-3682]

## Inferences

- Inference: Lord's paradox demonstrates that the correct analytical approach depends on the causal structure—what constitutes the 'treatment' variable.
- Inference: The multiple references to Lord's paradox across different fields (social epidemiology, psychology, statistics) suggest it has broad applicability in causal analysis.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The original framing concerned gender rather than diet, making it unclear whether diet effects can even be assessed from the data.
