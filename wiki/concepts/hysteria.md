# Hysteria

## Summary

Hysteria is a neurosis characterized by somatic symptoms (anaesthesia, paralyses, contractures, convulsive attacks) and psychological disturbances, without detectable organic changes in the nervous system. In the pre-psychoanalytic writings of Volume I, hysteria is discussed primarily through the lens of Charcot's clinical approach, which Freud adopted and began to extend beyond purely neurological explanation. The texts trace Freud's growing recognition of psychical factors in hysteria, culminating in the attribution of the cathartic treatment method to Breuer.

## Key facts

- Freud's unsigned article on hysteria (1888) states that hysteria is 'based wholly and entirely on physiological modifications of the nervous system' and that its essence should be expressed in a formula accounting for conditions of excitability in different parts of the nervous system. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p43]
- The article on hysteria describes 'hysterogenic zones'—supersensitive areas of the body where slight stimulation releases an attack or aura sensations—found more often on the trunk than extremities and with a predilection for the ovarian area. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p45]
- The article explicitly describes Breuer's cathartic method of treatment as being in use, four years before its first publication in the Breuer & Freud 'Preliminary Communication' of 1893. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p82]
- Freud's 1886 case of male hysteria (August P.) demonstrated that hysterical hemianaesthesia oversteps the mid-line, includes the tongue, larynx or genitals as a whole, and is accompanied by amblyopia rather than hemianopsia in the eyes. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p67]
- The article states that hysterical symptoms 'do not in any way present a copy of the anatomical conditions of the nervous system' and that 'hysteria is as ignorant of the science of the structure of the nervous system as we ourselves before we have learnt it.'. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p85]
- Freud's article asserts that the influence of psychical processes on physical processes in the organism is increased in hysteria, with a 'surplus of excitation in the nervous system' manifesting 'now as an inhibitor, now as an irritant.'. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p85]
- Hysteria never, even in its most threatening manifestations, involves a serious risk to life. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p97]
- Complete intellectual clarity and capacity for unusual achievements is retained in the most protracted cases of hysteria. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p97]
- Hysteria may be combined with many other neurotic and organic nervous diseases, and the commonest combination is with neurasthenia. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p97]
- The male nervous system has as preponderant a disposition to neurasthenia as the female to hysteria. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p97]
- The frequency of female hysteria is overestimated; the majority of women feared to be hysterical by physicians are strictly speaking merely neurasthenic. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p97]
- Feverish illnesses usually interrupt the development of a hysterical neurosis; a hysterical hemianaesthesia will recede in fever. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p97]
- Direct treatment consists in the removal of the psychical sources of stimulus for hysterical symptoms, and is understandable if we look for the causes of hysteria in unconscious ideational life. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p99]
- Hysteria is an anomaly of the nervous system which is based on a different distribution of excitations, probably accompanied by a surplus of stimuli in the organ of the mind. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p100]
- The symptomatology of hysteria shows that this surplus is distributed by means of conscious or unconscious ideas. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p100]
- Hysteria is caused by memories of passive sexual experiences occurring before puberty, typically between infancy and age eight to ten. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p163]
- The memory of the precocious event becomes active during or after puberty and operates with the force of a contemporary experience. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p154]
- Hysteria and anxiety neurosis share the characteristic of somatic excitation accumulation, but differ in that hysteria involves psychical excitation (from conflict) rather than purely somatic sexual excitation. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p115]

## Inferences

- Inference: Freud's attribution of the cathartic method to Breuer in the 1888 article establishes Breuer's priority over Janet's similar notions published in 1889.
- Inference: The treatment of hysteria involves three distinct tasks: treating the hysterical disposition, treating hysterical outbreaks (acute hysteria), and treating individual hysterical symptoms (local hysteria).
- Inference: Freud's concept of hysteria as involving unconscious ideas anticipates his later psychoanalytic theories.
- Inference: Freud recognized an intimate relationship between hysteria and anxiety neurosis, suggesting they regularly combine and that hysteria may 'borrow' symptoms from anxiety neurosis.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The article on hysteria was unsigned in Villaret's encyclopaedia; its attribution to Freud, though strongly supported by the reference to Breuer's unpublished cathartic method, is not absolutely conclusive.
- Uncertain: The exact physiological mechanism of how excitations are distributed differently in hysteria is not fully specified in this text.
