# Convexity Bias

## Summary

Convexity bias (or convexity effects) refers to the principle that volatility of an exposure can matter more than its average—the difference being the convexity bias. Taleb applies this mathematical insight extensively to medicine, showing that in convex systems (where small doses provide benefits and larger doses provide proportionally less benefit), randomness in exposure is superior to steady exposure. For example, lung ventilators that provide variable pressure (mimicking natural breathing) are safer than constant pressure; humans are antifragile to such variability. The medical community has largely failed to formalize convexity effects into decision-making methodology, instead linearly extrapolating risks and benefits, causing both underestimation and overestimation of harm.

## Key facts

- In convex cases, 50% of a dose during one period followed by 150% of the dose in a subsequent period is superior to 100% in both periods—this bias is a necessary result of convexity by theorem. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p429]
- J. F. Brewster discovered that providing higher pressure occasionally and low pressure at other times allowed more volume to lungs for a given mean pressure and decreased patient mortality in acute respiratory distress syndrome. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p430]
- Taleb found only one explicit use of Jensen's inequality in medical literature, thanks to his friend Eric Briys, and only one paper using it properly. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p430]
- The medical community has not formalized convexity effects into decision-making methodology despite the ubiquity of nonlinear responses in biology. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p429]
- A paper on radiation states: 'The standard model currently in use applies a linear scale, extrapolating cancer risk from high doses to low doses of ionizing radiation,' illustrating systematic failure to account for convexity. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p429]
- Taleb compares ignoring nonlinear effects in empirical work to cataloging every apple falling from a tree and calling it 'empiricism' instead of using Newton's equation. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p431]

## Inferences

- Inference: The lack of convexity analysis in medicine explains why nutrition science has failed to model the difference between random calories and steady nutrition—caloric restriction and intermittent fasting produce benefits unattainable through steady dietary intake.
- Inference: Pharmaceutical companies exploit (knowingly or not) convexity effects by bundling very ill and less ill patients in clinical trials, obscuring the true harm-benefit ratio for each group.
