# Reversible Occlusion

## Summary

Reversible occlusion is a principle stating that whatever goes out of sight as the observer travels comes into sight as the observer returns, and vice versa. This principle applies to both movements of the point of observation and motions of detached objects. Occluding edges separate projected (visible) from unprojected (hidden) surfaces, and this separation reverses with opposite movements. The principle enables perception of hidden surfaces and the continuation of surfaces behind occluders.

## Key facts

- Whatever goes out of sight as the observer travels comes into sight as the observer returns; going out of sight is the inverse of coming into sight, and occluding and occluded surfaces interchange. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p103]
- An occluding edge is a dihedral where only one of the surfaces is projected to the point of observation—there are sharp occluding edges (apical) and rounded occluding edges (curved convexities). [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p103]
- A surface is projected at a point of observation if it has a visual solid angle in the ambient optic array; it is unprojected if it does not. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p102]
- The continuation of the far side with the near side of an object is specified by the reversibility of occlusion—whatever is revealed by a given movement is concealed by the reverse movement. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p109]
- Reversible occlusion is a consequence of the reversibility of locomotion and motions in the medium. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p214]
- The occlusion of a surface can be nullified by a movement of the body, head, or limbs in the opposite direction, whereas destruction cannot be canceled. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p217]
- For any fixed point of observation, the persisting layout is divided into hidden and unhidden surfaces, and every movement that hides surfaces has an opposite movement that reveals them. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p215]
- Young children experience reversible occlusion constantly through peek-a-boo, head-turning, and watching their hands, contrasted with irreversible events like spilling milk or breaking objects. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p217]
- The surface being covered is seen to persist after being concealed, and the surface being uncovered is seen to pre-exist before being revealed. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p213]

## Inferences

- Inference: Reversible occlusion explains how observers can perceive surfaces not currently in view—the information for hidden surfaces becomes available as the observer moves, contradicting the assumption that perception requires direct sensory contact with surfaces.
- Inference: Gibson argues that this demonstrates perception extends into the past and future, contradicting the doctrine that perception is confined to the present moment.
- Inference: The distinction between reversible occlusion and irreversible destruction suggests humans learn to see the difference between optical transitions that can be nullified and those that cannot.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Whether infants notice the difference between reversible occlusion and irreversible destruction has not been experimentally tested according to Gibson's preferred methods.
