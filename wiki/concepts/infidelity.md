# Infidelity

## Summary

Infidelity (or affairs/adultery) involves a breach of the couple's implicit or explicit agreement about exclusivity. Perel defines infidelity using three constitutive elements: secrecy, sexual alchemy (erotic charge beyond physical acts), and emotional involvement. The definition varies across cultures and historical periods, and contemporary infidelity occurs within changed expectations of modern marriage.

## Key facts

- Perel defines infidelity using three constitutive elements: secrecy, sexual alchemy, and emotional involvement. [raw/books/psychology/The State of Affairs - Esther Perel.pdf#p31]
- Secrecy is identified as the number one organizing principle of an infidelity. [raw/books/psychology/The State of Affairs - Esther Perel.pdf#p31]
- Estimates of infidelity prevalence among American couples range from 26 to 70 percent for women and from 33 to 75 percent for men, due to varying definitions. [raw/books/psychology/The State of Affairs - Esther Perel.pdf#p26]
- Research indicates a 40 percent jump in women's infidelity rates since 1990, while men's rates have held steady. [raw/books/psychology/The State of Affairs - Esther Perel.pdf#p26]
- The revelation of an affair can trigger trauma-like symptoms including obsessive rumination, hypervigilance, numbness, and panic. [raw/books/psychology/The State of Affairs - Esther Perel.pdf#p55]
- Affairs have a lot to teach about relationships—what we expect, what we think we want, and what we feel entitled to. [raw/books/psychology/The State of Affairs - Esther Perel.pdf#p9]

## Inferences

- Inference: Perel's three-element definition (secrecy, sexual alchemy, emotional involvement) suggests that affairs exist on a spectrum rather than as a binary phenomenon.
- Inference: The rise in female infidelity rates (40% increase since 1990) correlates with women's increased economic independence and changing social norms.
