# Friction in war

## Summary

Friction, as analyzed by Alan Beyerchen through Clausewitz's work, is the concept that distinguishes real war from war on paper and encompasses two related notions: physical resistance leading to entropy and heat dissipation in a system, and information-theoretical noise that distorts signals within military organizations. Unnoticeably small causes can be disproportionately amplified in war until they produce macro-effects that can never be anticipated.

## Key facts

- Friction in Clausewitz's sense entails two different but related notions: physical resistance related to heat leading to the Second Law of Thermodynamics and entropy, and information theory 'noise' that garbles plans and commands during communication. [raw/books/strategy/ScienceStrategyWar_Osinga.pdf#p171]
- Clausewitz's 'fog of war' is not so much about a dearth of information as how distortion and overload of information produce uncertainty as to the actual state of affairs. [raw/books/strategy/ScienceStrategyWar_Osinga.pdf#p172]
- Organizations are always slower and more inflexible than the natural events they are intended to control, with training, regulations, and procedures serving as redundancies that enhance the probability of signal recognition through noise. [raw/books/strategy/ScienceStrategyWar_Osinga.pdf#p172]
- Clausewitz emphasized that no other human activity is so continuously or universally bound up with chance as is war. [raw/books/strategy/ScienceStrategyWar_Osinga.pdf#p172]

## Inferences

- Inference: Friction reveals Clausewitz's understanding of how unnoticeably small causes can become amplified in war until they produce macro-effects, and that one can never anticipate those effects, making precise knowledge of initial conditions unattainable.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Whether Beyerchen's interpretation of Clausewitz's friction fully captures Clausewitz's intended meaning or represents modern theoretical reconstruction.
