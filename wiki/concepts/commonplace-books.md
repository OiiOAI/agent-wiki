# Commonplace Books

## Summary

Commonplace books were personal notebooks used by educated individuals from Leonardo da Vinci to Virginia Woolf to record ideas they found most interesting. Popularized during the Industrial Revolution, they served as learning tools and portals through which people interacted with the world. The practice involved breaking texts into fragments and assembling them into new patterns, making reading and writing inseparable activities.

## Key facts

- Commonplace books were used from the Renaissance through the 18th and early 19th centuries by writers, politicians, philosophers, and scientists. [raw/books/productivity/Building a Second Brain - Tiago Forte.pdf#p22-23]
- Historian Robert Darnton explained that early modern readers read 'in fits and starts,' broke texts into fragments, and assembled them into new patterns by transcribing them into notebooks. [raw/books/productivity/Building a Second Brain - Tiago Forte.pdf#p23]
- Similar practices existed in China (biji, meaning 'notebook') and Japan (zuihitsu, 'pillow books'). [raw/books/productivity/Building a Second Brain - Tiago Forte.pdf#p31]
- The author positions the Second Brain as a modern digital equivalent of the commonplace book, with added searchability and accessibility. [raw/books/productivity/Building a Second Brain - Tiago Forte.pdf#p24]
