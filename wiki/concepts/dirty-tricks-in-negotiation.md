# Dirty Tricks in Negotiation

## Summary

Fisher and Ury categorize tricky bargaining tactics into three categories: deliberate deception (phony facts, ambiguous authority, dubious intentions), psychological warfare (stressful situations, personal attacks, good-guy/bad-guy routines, threats), and positional pressure tactics (refusal to negotiate, extreme demands, escalating demands, lock-in tactics). They recommend three-step response: recognize the tactic, raise the issue explicitly, and negotiate over it using principled negotiation about the negotiating process itself.

## Key facts

- Tricky tactics are illegitimate because they fail the test of reciprocity—they are designed to be used by only one side. [raw/books/strategy/Getting_to_Yes_Fisher_Ury.pdf#p64]
- The three steps to negotiate the rules of the game are: recognize the tactic, raise the issue explicitly, and question the tactic's legitimacy and desirability. [raw/books/strategy/Getting_to_Yes_Fisher_Ury.pdf#p65]
- The good-guy/bad-guy routine is a form of psychological manipulation; when the good guy makes a pitch, ask for principled justification of their position. [raw/books/strategy/Getting_to_Yes_Fisher_Ury.pdf#p67]
- Warnings are much more legitimate than threats and are not vulnerable to counterthreats: suggest consequences that will occur independently of your will. [raw/books/strategy/Getting_to_Yes_Fisher_Ury.pdf#p68]
- Lock-in tactics are gambles—you may call the other side's bluff and force them to make a concession they will then have to explain to their constituency. [raw/books/strategy/Getting_to_Yes_Fisher_Ury.pdf#p69]
