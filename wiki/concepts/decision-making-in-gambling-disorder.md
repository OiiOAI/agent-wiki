# Decision-Making in Gambling Disorder

## Summary

Gambling disorder is a psychiatric syndrome in DSM-5 grouped alongside substance use disorders as the only recognized behavioral addiction. The chapter examines four psychological phenomena (loss aversion, probability weighting, perceptions of randomness, and illusory control) that are relevant to understanding why some individuals develop disordered gambling and how decision neuroscience experiments contribute to understanding the neural circuitry underlying these biases.

## Key facts

- Gambling disorder is diagnosed if an individual meets at least four of nine DSM-5 criteria, including symptoms reminiscent of drug addiction such as withdrawal and tolerance. [raw/books/neuroscience/Decision Neuroscience _ An Integrative Perspective - Jean - Claude Dreher; Léon Tremblay; Institut de sciences.pdf#p326]
- The prevalence of full DSM-diagnosed gambling disorder is around 1% in most jurisdictions, with 3-5% of the population displaying subclinical features labeled as 'problem gambling' or 'at-risk gambling'. [raw/books/neuroscience/Decision Neuroscience _ An Integrative Perspective - Jean - Claude Dreher; Léon Tremblay; Institut de sciences.pdf#p326]
- Single gambling events can be decomposed into three stages: selection (which slot machine to play), an anticipatory period (the reel spin), and the gain or loss feedback. [raw/books/neuroscience/Decision Neuroscience _ An Integrative Perspective - Jean - Claude Dreher; Léon Tremblay; Institut de sciences.pdf#p326]
- Loss aversion refers to the phenomenon where losses loom larger than gains, with participants typically requiring wins to be approximately twice as large as losses before accepting a gamble (lambda values of around 2). [raw/books/neuroscience/Decision Neuroscience _ An Integrative Perspective - Jean - Claude Dreher; Léon Tremblay; Institut de sciences.pdf#p327]
- Patients with brain damage to the ventromedial prefrontal cortex, amygdala, or insula were more likely to accept positive expected value coin tosses than healthy controls due to reduced loss aversion. [raw/books/neuroscience/Decision Neuroscience _ An Integrative Perspective - Jean - Claude Dreher; Léon Tremblay; Institut de sciences.pdf#p327]
- The probability weighting function describes the relationship between objective probability and subjective perception, where low-probability events are overestimated and high-probability events are underestimated. [raw/books/neuroscience/Decision Neuroscience _ An Integrative Perspective - Jean - Claude Dreher; Léon Tremblay; Institut de sciences.pdf#p328]
- Pathological gamblers showed an overall elevation in subjective probability across the full range of odds, correlated with gambling severity and gambling affinity. [raw/books/neuroscience/Decision Neuroscience _ An Integrative Perspective - Jean - Claude Dreher; Léon Tremblay; Institut de sciences.pdf#p329]
- The gambler's fallacy refers to the tendency to predict against recent runs of outcomes, such as guessing 'tails' after observing four consecutive 'heads' in coin flips. [raw/books/neuroscience/Decision Neuroscience _ An Integrative Perspective - Jean - Claude Dreher; Léon Tremblay; Institut de sciences.pdf#p329]
- Damage to the insula abolished the gambler's fallacy on a roulette task, and insula patients were also unresponsive to 'near miss' outcomes on a simulated slot machine. [raw/books/neuroscience/Decision Neuroscience _ An Integrative Perspective - Jean - Claude Dreher; Léon Tremblay; Institut de sciences.pdf#p330]
- The illusion of control refers to an expectancy of personal success probability inappropriately higher than objective probability would warrant, created when features of skill-based situations are present in games determined by chance. [raw/books/neuroscience/Decision Neuroscience _ An Integrative Perspective - Jean - Claude Dreher; Léon Tremblay; Institut de sciences.pdf#p331]
- Frontostriatal circuitry, including striatum, insula, and prefrontal cortex, is implicated across all four decision-making distortions in gambling disorder. [raw/books/neuroscience/Decision Neuroscience _ An Integrative Perspective - Jean - Claude Dreher; Léon Tremblay; Institut de sciences.pdf#p332]

## Inferences

- Inference: The shared brain circuitry (striatum, insula, PFC) implicated across decision-making distortions suggests a common neural substrate for gambling-related cognitive biases.
- Inference: Loss chasing in gambling disorder may be driven by failure to re-reference between successive bets, causing players to shift progressively on the value function away from the origin.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Individual differences in loss aversion may show bimodality in gambling disorder, with gamblers polarizing toward extreme high or low values.
- Uncertain: The extent to which decisional constructs like loss aversion and probability weighting specifically contribute to substance use disorders remains unclear.
