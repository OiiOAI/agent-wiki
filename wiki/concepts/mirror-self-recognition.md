# Mirror Self-Recognition

## Summary

Mirror self-recognition is a test for self-awareness in animals, developed by Gordon Gallup Jr in the 1970s. Animals are anesthetized and marked in an unseen body location, then observed with a mirror when awake. Passing the test requires spontaneously looking for the mark on one's own body rather than investigating the mirror image. Among mammals, great apes, some dolphins and killer whales, and one Eurasian elephant pass. Monkeys consistently fail despite apparent consciousness and selfhood.

## Key facts

- Mirror self-recognition typically develops in humans between 18 and 24 months of age, indicating developing personal identity self-consciousness. [raw/books/neuroscience/Being You _ A New Science of Consciousness - Seth, Anil.pdf#p219]
- The mirror test was developed by Gordon Gallup Jr in the 1970s. [raw/books/neuroscience/Being You _ A New Science of Consciousness - Seth, Anil.pdf#p220]
- Among mammals, some great apes, a few dolphins and killer whales, and a single Eurasian elephant pass the mirror test. [raw/books/neuroscience/Being You _ A New Science of Consciousness - Seth, Anil.pdf#p220]
- Pandas, dogs, and various monkeys have failed the mirror test, at least using standard procedures. [raw/books/neuroscience/Being You _ A New Science of Consciousness - Seth, Anil.pdf#p220]
- There is no convincing evidence that any non-mammal passes the mirror test, although manta rays and magpies may come close, with current controversy about cleaner wrasse. [raw/books/neuroscience/Being You _ A New Science of Consciousness - Seth, Anil.pdf#p220]
- Monkeys consistently fail the mirror test despite being undoubtedly conscious and possessing some kind of selfhood—they are not 'furry little people.'. [raw/books/neuroscience/Being You _ A New Science of Consciousness - Seth, Anil.pdf#p222]
- Gallup developed the mirror test by anesthetizing chimpanzees, placing red marks on their ear and eyebrow, and observing whether they touched the marks after seeing themselves in a mirror. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p318]
- Not all chimps exhibit MSR; it develops in some around puberty and may deteriorate over time. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p318]
- Orangutans show MSR, but only a rare gorilla possesses it; two dolphins and one out of five Asian elephants have passed the mark test. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p318]
- Children pass the mark test by age two. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p318]
- Robert Mitchell divides self into three levels: implicit self (experiences, acts, emotions), kinesthetic-visual self (leads to MSR), and symbolic self (language, artifacts, cultural beliefs). [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p319]

## Inferences

- Inference: Animals could fail the mirror test for many reasons besides lacking self-recognition: not liking mirrors, not understanding how they work, or preferring to avoid eye contact.
- Inference: MSR does not necessarily mean the absence of self-awareness, as demonstrated by prosopagnosia patients who cannot recognize themselves in mirrors but clearly have self-awareness (causing them distress).

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Rhesus monkeys can pass after extensive training to do so, but spontaneous use of mirrors for self-recognition is different from trained performance.
- Uncertain: The degree of self-awareness demonstrated by MSR is debated—some researchers argue only body awareness is required, not abstract self-concept.
