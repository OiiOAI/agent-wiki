# Cognitive Reserve

## Summary

A theoretical construct suggesting that years of education create additional neural networks devoted to mental activity, which can be called upon as the brain declines with age. Studies show that people with more education use different brain regions for cognitive tasks and appear better protected from mental decline and Alzheimer's disease.

## Key facts

- The cognitive reserve hypothesis suggests that years of education create many more networks devoted to mental activity that can be called upon as brains decline. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p257]
- Brain scans show young people (14-30) with more education use their temporal lobes more for cognitive tasks. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p256]
- Subjects over 65 show different patterns, using their frontal lobes more for the same cognitive tasks, with more education correlating with greater frontal lobe use. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p257]
- People with more education, who are more socially and physically active and participate in mentally stimulating activities, are less likely to get Alzheimer's disease or dementia. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p257]

## Inferences

- Inference: The shift in brain activation patterns from temporal to frontal lobes in aging may represent the brain drawing on cognitive reserve networks to maintain function.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The exact mechanism by which education creates cognitive reserve is not definitively established.
