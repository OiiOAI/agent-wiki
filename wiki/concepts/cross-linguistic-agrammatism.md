# Cross-Linguistic Variation in Agrammatism

## Summary

Cross-linguistic studies of agrammatism reveal that the same grammatical operation may recruit different cortical regions depending on how it is encoded syntactically in different languages. Languages relying on word order (like English) show different lesion patterns than languages relying on inflectional morphology (like Italian), demonstrating that grammatical functions are distributed and task-dependent rather than localized.

## Key facts

- English speakers appear especially susceptible to disruption of grammatical abilities from damage associated with Broca's area, particularly difficulties interpreting sentences depending on grammatical function words and word-order transformations. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p333]
- In speakers of highly inflected languages like Italian, Wernicke's aphasics are more impaired in producing and analyzing grammatical transformations than are patients with Broca's area damage. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p333]
- When word order is more critical for grammar, damage to ventral frontal regions produces greater impairment; when inflection and morphology are more critical, damage to temporal regions produces greater impairment. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p334]
- The localization of syntactic functions is determined by the surface characteristics of syntactic devices employed in a given language, not by the underlying grammatical logic they support. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p335]

## Inferences

- Inference: This evidence argues against a universal grammar module that is localized to a specific brain region, since the same logical operation maps differently to brain structures depending on language-specific implementation.
