# Moral Shame

## Summary

Moral shame arises when someone prizes as excellences of his person those virtues that his plan of life requires. Actions and traits that manifest or betray the absence of these attributes occasion shame. Moral shame differs from guilt: guilt focuses on infringement of others' rights and their probable resentment, while shame focuses on loss of self-esteem.

## Key facts

- Someone is liable to moral shame when he prizes as excellences of his person those virtues that his plan of life requires. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p395]
- Moral shame is distinguished from guilt: guilt focuses on infringement of just claims and injury to others; shame focuses on loss to self-esteem and diminishment of self. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p396]
- Natural shame arises from injury to self-esteem due to not having or failing to exercise certain excellences, not from loss of exclusive goods. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p394]
- Feelings of shame are relative to one's aspirations and to what one tries to do and with whom one wishes to associate. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p395]

## Inferences

- Inference: The virtues are excellences that are good from the standpoint of both ourselves and others, so their lack undermines both self-esteem and others' esteem.
