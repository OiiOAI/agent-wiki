# Perspective Taking

## Summary

Perspective taking is the ability to mentally simulate another person's point of view. Research shows this ability emerges around 18 months in human infants and involves specific brain regions including the temporoparietal junction and ventral prefrontal cortex. Some nonhuman primates demonstrate limited visual perspective-taking abilities in competitive contexts.

## Key facts

- Perspective taking emerges at about 18 months, when infants begin offering others the type of food those others prefer rather than what the infant prefers. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p198]
- The temporoparietal junction integrates input from many brain regions including thalamus, visual, auditory, somesthetic, limbic areas, and prefrontal cortex, and plays a critical role in distinguishing self from other. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p200]
- Electrical stimulation of the right angular gyrus during epilepsy evaluation caused out-of-body experiences, suggesting this area is involved in self-location and perspective. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p201]
- Damage to the ventral prefrontal cortex in childhood results in impaired perspective-taking ability and egocentric behavior, while adults with similar damage can compensate better. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p201]
- Chimpanzees in competitive situations with humans spontaneously avoided food that humans were watching, approaching only food behind opaque barriers when humans could see them. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p204]

## Inferences

- Inference: Chimpanzees showing indirect approach behavior when watched suggests they understood the importance of being hidden from a competitor's view, indicating intentional deception capacity in competitive contexts.
