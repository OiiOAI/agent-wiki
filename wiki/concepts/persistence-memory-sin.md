# Persistence (Memory Sin)

## Summary

Persistence is the seventh sin of memory—remembering things we wish we could forget. It involves intrusive, unwanted memories of negative experiences, trauma, and failures. Unlike other memory sins that involve forgetting, persistence involves remembering too much of painful material. The sin is especially associated with emotional events, traumatic experiences, and clinical depression, and is a core feature of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD).

## Key facts

- Donnie Moore, the pitcher who gave up the home run to Dave Henderson in the 1986 ALCS, was haunted by the memory and eventually shot his wife before killing himself, demonstrating the devastating potential of persistence. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p217]
- Epidemiological studies suggest just over half of women and 60% of men will experience at least one traumatic event in their lives. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p230]
- Intrusive memories of trauma usually take the form of vivid perceptual images, with visual memories being most common, preserving minute details trauma survivors would most like to forget. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p230]
- Nearly all trauma survivors report having experienced intrusive memories in the days and weeks after trauma, but not everyone continues to be plagued months or years later. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p231]
- Those who remain focused on the past for years after a traumatic event exhibit higher levels of psychological distress, which in turn stimulates greater focus on the past, creating a destructive self-perpetuating cycle. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p231]
- Persistence is associated with elevated activity in the amygdala and stress hormones like norepinephrine and cortisol. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p384]
- Rumination, particularly about negative events, can strengthen and maintain persistent memories. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p384]
- Propranolol, a beta-blocker, has been shown to reduce the emotional impact of memories when given shortly after trauma or during memory reconsolidation. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p384]
- Memory reconsolidation provides a window during which memories become temporarily malleable and susceptible to modification. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p384]
- Counterfactual thinking ('what if') about negative events can maintain their emotional impact and prevent closure. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p384]

## Inferences

- Inference: The contrast between Donnie Moore's persistence and Jean Van de Velde's recovery suggests that how we evaluate and appraise events influences whether we become trapped by persistent memories.
- Inference: Self-schemas—accumulations of past experiences that shape current evaluations—may determine vulnerability to persistence, with negative self-schemas creating conditions for depressive rumination.
- Inference: Persistence may reflect an adaptive system for maintaining important threat-related information, but which can become maladaptive in conditions like PTSD.
