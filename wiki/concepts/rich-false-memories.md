# Rich False Memories

## Summary

Rich false memories are detailed, compelling recollections of complex events that never actually occurred. The term, coined by Elizabeth Loftus, describes memories that go beyond minor suggestion effects. By the end of the 1990s, studies established that suggestive procedures could induce approximately 20-30% of participants to produce rich false memories. The 2015 Shaw and Porter study claimed a 70% rate, later revised to 25-30% when distinguishing false memories from false beliefs.

## Key facts

- By the end of the 1990s, various studies using paradigms like 'lost in the mall' had established that suggestive procedures could induce approximately 20 to 30% of participants to produce rich false memories. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p181]
- In 2015, Shaw and Porter claimed that 70% of their college student sample developed false memories of committing crimes as adolescents, but this was later critiqued and revised to 25-30% when distinguishing false memories from false beliefs. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p181]
- Kimberley Wade and colleagues found that Shaw and Porter's original 70% figure included false beliefs—accepting suggestions as true and speculating—versus genuine false memories where people report a subjective experience of remembering. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p182]
- The combination of social pressure (telling students their parents confirmed the events) and repeated visualization produced the high rates of false beliefs reported by Shaw and Porter. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p181]
- Even with the revised lower figures, 70% of participants in Shaw and Porter's study falsely believed they had committed crimes, suggesting that developing false beliefs may be more common than false memories proper. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p182]

## Inferences

- Inference: The distinction between false beliefs and false memories is theoretically important but may be less significant practically for false confessions, as a false belief in one's guilt likely increases confession likelihood regardless of whether full false memories develop.
