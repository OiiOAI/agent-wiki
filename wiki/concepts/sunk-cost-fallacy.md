# Sunk Cost Fallacy

## Summary

The Sunk Cost Fallacy describes the tendency to continue investing in something because of previously invested resources (time, money, energy) even when dealing with a lost cause. The more we invest, the greater the urge to continue, leading to rational decision-making failures.

## Key facts

- The sunk cost fallacy is most dangerous when we have invested a lot of time, money, energy or love in something, as this investment becomes a reason to carry on even if dealing with a lost cause. [raw/books/strategy/The Art of Thinking Clearly _ Better thinking, Better - Rolf Dobelli; Nicky Griffin.pdf#p22]
- Concorde is a prime example of a government deficit project where both Britain and France continued investing enormous sums even though they knew the supersonic aircraft business would never work. [raw/books/strategy/The Art of Thinking Clearly _ Better thinking, Better - Rolf Dobelli; Nicky Griffin.pdf#p23]
- Rational decision-making requires forgetting about costs incurred to date; only assessment of future costs and benefits should count. [raw/books/strategy/The Art of Thinking Clearly _ Better thinking, Better - Rolf Dobelli; Nicky Griffin.pdf#p23]
- The sunk cost fallacy explains why two sports fans differ in willingness to drive through an announced blizzard: the one who paid for the ticket has a negative balance to close in the mental account, making the trip feel necessary to avoid the loss of the ticket cost, while the free-ticket holder feels no such obligation. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p443]
- A company that has spent $50 million on a failing project and needs an additional $60 million to complete it will often continue investing rather than accept the sunk loss, even when the alternative project offers better expected returns—this is the 'throwing good money after bad' pattern described in the fourfold upper-right cell (high-probability loss). [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p445]
- Executive incentives often misalign with company interests in sunk cost situations: abandoning a project would create a permanent stain on the executive's record, while continuing the gamble offers hope of recovery or at least delay, aligning personal interests with the bias. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p446]
- Boards of directors often replace CEOs specifically because the new CEO does not carry the same mental accounts as the predecessor, enabling more rational evaluation of new opportunities without the weight of past sunk costs. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p446]

## Inferences

- Inference: The sunk cost fallacy can be partially overcome through education: graduate students in economics and business are more willing to abandon failing projects than students in other fields, suggesting that explicit instruction in the fallacy has measurable effects.
