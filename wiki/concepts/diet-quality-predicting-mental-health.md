# Diet quality predicting mental health onset

## Summary

Prospective studies have established that poor diet precedes and may contribute to poor mental health. Whole food diets appear to be protective. Multiple longitudinal studies across different countries have shown that dietary patterns can predict the onset of depression and other mental health conditions.

## Key facts

- A prospective UK study of more than 3,000 middle-aged people showed that a diet mainly consisting of sweet desserts, fried food, processed meat, and refined grains was a risk factor for the emergence of depression five years later. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Better Brain - Bonnie J. Kaplan.pdf#p108]
- The SUN project in Spain followed over 9,000 adults and found that almost 500 new cases of depression emerged at the six-year mark, with higher risk related to consumption of fast food and commercially baked goods. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Better Brain - Bonnie J. Kaplan.pdf#p108]
- A Japanese study of close to 100,000 people found that a dietary pattern with high consumption of vegetables, fruits, mushrooms, seaweed, and fish was related to about a 50 percent decreased risk of death from suicide four years later. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Better Brain - Bonnie J. Kaplan.pdf#p109]
- Adolescents in Australia who improved their diet over two years also improved their mental health, while those whose diet got worse had poorer psychological functioning. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Better Brain - Bonnie J. Kaplan.pdf#p109]

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Not all studies show these associations; a study of almost 4,000 adolescents did not find that fruit and vegetable consumption was associated with the development of depression twelve years later, showing that prediction over long periods is challenging and multifactorial.
