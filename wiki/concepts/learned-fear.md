# Learned Fear

## Summary

Learned fear is the association of a neutral stimulus with an aversive outcome, a process studied extensively in mice. When a tone is paired with a foot shock, mice learn to fear the tone and exhibit freezing behavior. The neural circuit for learned fear centers on the amygdala, which receives information about conditioned and unconditioned stimuli through both cortical and subcortical pathways.

## Key facts

- Joseph LeDoux and Michael Davis found that in rodents, both innate and learned fear recruit a neural circuit focused on the amygdala. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p352]
- The lateral nucleus of the amygdala receives information about the conditioned stimulus (tone) through both a direct subcortical pathway bypassing the cortex and an indirect pathway through the auditory cortex. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p353]
- The direct pathway allows a frightening stimulus to cause physiological fear responses before conscious realization through the slow cortical pathway, supporting the James-Lange theory. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p354]
- Learned fear increases synaptic strength in the lateral nucleus of the amygdala through a variant of long-term potentiation involving cyclic AMP, protein kinase A, and CREB. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p355]
- A single exposure to a threat can allow the amygdala to retain the memory of that threat throughout an organism's entire life. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p352]
