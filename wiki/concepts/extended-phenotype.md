# Extended Phenotype

## Summary

The extended phenotype is Dawkins' concept that the phenotypic effects of a gene extend beyond the body in which it sits to include effects on the world outside, such as artefacts like beaver dams, bird nests, and caddis houses, as well as effects on other organisms. It resolves the paradox of viewing life from both the gene's perspective and the individual organism's perspective.

## Key facts

- The phenotypic effects of a gene need to be thought of as all the effects that it has on the world, not just effects on the body in which it sits. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p278]
- The caddis house is an adaptation evolved by Darwinian selection, and genes 'for' stone shape, stone size, and stone hardness can be spoken of as legitimate extended phenotypic effects. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p280]
- The central theorem of the extended phenotype states: An animal's behaviour tends to maximize the survival of the genes 'for' that behaviour, whether or not those genes happen to be in the body of the particular animal performing it. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p295]
- The gene reaches out through the individual body wall and manipulates objects in the world outside, some of them inanimate, some of them other living beings, some of them a long way away. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p307]

## Inferences

- Inference: The extended phenotype concept implies that the traditional boundary of 'an organism' is somewhat arbitrary from the gene's perspective.
- Inference: Parasites manipulating host behaviour can be understood as extended phenotypic effects of parasite genes on host bodies.
