# Stereotype Bias

## Summary

Stereotype bias refers to the distorting influence of generic knowledge and expectations on memory. Activated stereotypes can bias what we remember about individuals, leading us to recall information consistent with our expectations while forgetting incongruent details. Research shows stereotype bias can occur automatically, outside conscious awareness, and is linked to the left hemisphere's 'interpreter' system that relies on general knowledge to make sense of experiences.

## Key facts

- When people were told Julian was an artist, they were more likely to recall that he was creative and temperamental than generous and fearless, consistent with artistic stereotypes. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p204]
- College students were nearly twice as likely to identify stereotypically Black names (Tyrone Washington, Darnell Jones) as criminal names compared to stereotypically white names, even when instructed not to use race in judgments. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p203]
- People were far more likely to make false fame errors for male names than female names, reflecting a gender stereotype that men are more likely to be famous. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p203]
- Stereotype bias is most pronounced when people are given difficult tasks to carry out at the same time they form impressions, leaving less cognitive effort for individual differences. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p204]
- The left hemisphere's 'interpreter' system shows a bias to respond on the basis of general knowledge, falsely recognizing novel incidents consistent with stereotypes while the right hemisphere claims only exact memories. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p207]
