# Sunk-Cost Bias

## Summary

Sunk-cost bias is a psychological tendency to continue investing time, money, or energy into something one knows is a losing proposition simply because one has already incurred costs that cannot be recovered. The more one invests, the harder it becomes to let go, creating a vicious cycle that traps people in unproductive commitments.

## Key facts

- The Concorde jet lost money for more than four decades, yet British and French governments continued pouring money in due to sunk-cost bias. [raw/books/productivity/Essentialism_ The Disciplined Pursuit of L - Mckeown, Greg.pdf#p135]
- The sunk costs for developing and building the Concorde were around $1 billion. [raw/books/productivity/Essentialism_ The Disciplined Pursuit of L - Mckeown, Greg.pdf#p136]
- Sunk-cost bias explains why people sit through terrible movies because they already paid for the ticket. [raw/books/productivity/Essentialism_ The Disciplined Pursuit of L - Mckeown, Greg.pdf#p136]
- Adults are more vulnerable to sunk-cost bias than children due to a lifetime of exposure to the 'Don't waste' rule. [raw/books/productivity/Essentialism_ The Disciplined Pursuit of L - Mckeown, Greg.pdf#p139]

## Inferences

- Inference: The sunk-cost bias is deeply connected to loss aversion—when we have invested something, the fear of wasting that investment outweighs the logical recognition that additional investment will not recover prior losses.
