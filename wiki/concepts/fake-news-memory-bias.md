# Fake News and Memory Bias

## Summary

Research on fake news reveals that consistency bias and the illusory truth effect shape how people remember (or misremember) news events. Studies show that people are more likely to develop false memories for fake news that aligns with their existing beliefs. The illusory truth effect—where repetition increases perceived accuracy—operates even for obviously false statements and news people know to be fake.

## Key facts

- In a study of political events, 27% claimed to remember seeing fabricated events on the news, and about 50% claimed some type of memory for fabricated events. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p209]
- Conservative participants were especially likely to report false memory for fabricated events depicting Obama negatively, while liberal participants showed the opposite pattern for fabricated Bush events. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p209]
- In the 2018 Irish abortion referendum study, 48% of participants indicated they remembered at least one of two fake news stories, with bias clearly playing a crucial role in shaping these false memories. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p210]
- The illusory truth effect—where repetition increases perceived accuracy—was demonstrated even for implausible fake news stories and stories people knew were fake. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p211]
- Following exposure to fake headlines, people judged it less unethical to share the news and indicated greater likelihood of sharing repeated fake headlines than novel ones. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p211]

## Inferences

- Inference: The internal 'Ministry of Truth' operating within individuals colors memory so that we are more likely to falsely remember fake events when they fit our personal 'party line.'
