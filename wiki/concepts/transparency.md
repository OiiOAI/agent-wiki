# Transparency

## Summary

Transparency is the assumption that people's behavior and demeanor—the way they represent themselves externally—provides an authentic and reliable window into their internal states. Gladwell argues this is a deeply flawed assumption, using examples from the Amanda Knox case, bail hearings, and cross-cultural studies (Trobrianders vs. Spanish schoolchildren) to demonstrate that facial expressions do not reliably reveal emotions universally.

## Key facts

- Transparency is the idea that behavior provides a reliable window into internal emotional states. [raw/books/psychology/Talking to Strangers - Gladwell, Malcolm.pdf#p89]
- Jennifer Fugate's FACS analysis of Friends actors showed every emotion was expressed perfectly on their faces. [raw/books/psychology/Talking to Strangers - Gladwell, Malcolm.pdf#p89]
- Trobrianders only identified happiness faces correctly 58% of the time compared to 100% for Spanish schoolchildren. [raw/books/psychology/Talking to Strangers - Gladwell, Malcolm.pdf#p92]
- In Schützwohl and Reisenzein's surprise experiment, only 5% of participants showed wide eyes, shooting eyebrows, and dropped jaws—yet 8.14/10 rated their surprise. [raw/books/psychology/Talking to Strangers - Gladwell, Malcolm.pdf#p95]
- Judges in bail hearings who could see defendants performed worse than computers at predicting reoffending. [raw/books/psychology/Talking to Strangers - Gladwell, Malcolm.pdf#p95]

## Inferences

- Inference: Gladwell suggests transparency may work with 'matched' people (whose behavior matches their internal state) but fails with 'mismatched' people (whose behavior contradicts their feelings)
- Inference: The Friends TV show creates unrealistic expectations about how emotions are displayed
