# Kinetic Depth Effect

## Summary

The kinetic depth effect (or stereokinesis) refers to the perception of depth that emerges when flat patterns undergo motion—discs or ellipses that look flat when stationary appear three-dimensional when rotating. Originally demonstrated by Musatti (1924), it was extensively studied by Wallach, Johansson, and others. Gibson argues this demonstrates that coherent optical transformations do not need to be 'organized' by the brain—they are already coherent information.

## Key facts

- Musatti (1924) first demonstrated that a drawing of circles or ellipses that looked flat when stationary would appear to go into depth when undergoing orbital motion on a turntable. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p197]
- Johansson found that if a set of several separate motions in the frontal plane can be resolved into a single motion of a rigid volume, the rigid motion will be perceived in depth. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p197]
- Wallach's kinetic depth effect uses shadow projection of a bent wire configuration onto a translucent screen—when the wire object is turned, the disposition of wires in space becomes evident. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p197]
- Gibson argues that if an optical transformation is already coherent, its elements do not need to be 'made coherent' in perception—they are simply picked up. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p198]

## Inferences

- Inference: The kinetic depth effect supports the direct perception hypothesis because the perception of 3D structure emerges directly from coherent motion information without requiring inference or reconstruction from 'sensations.'
