# Culturally Modified Brain

## Summary

The culturally modified brain concept proposes that culture is not only produced by the brain but also actively reshapes it. Sustained cultural activities—including reading, studying music, learning languages, and even media use—change brain structure and function. This creates a two-way street between brain and culture, where signature activities of a culture lead to specially wired brain circuits that differ from those of our ancestors.

## Key facts

- Neuroplastic research has shown that every sustained activity ever mapped—including physical activities, sensory activities, learning, thinking, and imagining—changes the brain as well as the mind. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p290]
- Merzenich states: 'Our brains are vastly different, in fine detail, from the brains of our ancestors... Each one of us can actually learn an incredibly elaborate set of ancestrally developed skills and abilities in our lifetimes.'. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p290]
- Brain scans of London taxi drivers show that the more years a cabbie spends navigating London streets, the larger the volume of his hippocampus. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p294]
- Studies of musicians show that the more they practice, the larger the brain maps for their active left hands become, and neurons and maps that respond to string timbres increase. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p293]
- Meditators and meditation teachers have a thicker insula, a part of the cortex activated by paying close attention. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p294]
- Literacy can be taught to illiterate hunter-gatherer tribes in a single generation, proving that specific reading circuits are not genetically based but acquired through cultural learning. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p296]
- The human genome differs from chimpanzees in a gene that determines how many neurons we will make, allowing humans to have about 100 billion neurons compared to chimps having a brain one-third the size. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p297]
