# Syntagmatic and Paradigmatic Word Associations

## Summary

Roman Jakobson's theory divides word associations underlying language processes into two broad categories: syntagmatic operations, which involve complementary relationships between words from different parts of speech in sequential arrangements, and paradigmatic operations, which involve substitution relationships between words of the same category. Brain imaging and lesion studies suggest these are distributed to different cortical regions.

## Key facts

- Paradigmatic operations are reflected in substitution relationships between words; metaphors, anaphors, and pronouns all serve this role as words of the same part of speech can substitute for one another. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p331]
- Syntagmatic operations are reflected in complementary relationships between words from different parts of speech and the way different word classes alternate in sequence; metonymy employs similar logic of complementarity. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p331]
- Metaphoric association requires selecting words with common semantic features, while metonymic association requires shifting attention to specifically alternative features. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p332]
- There may be a posterior cortical bias to metaphoric operations and a prefrontal cortical bias to metonymic operations because associations based on common perceptual features recruit posterior regions while associations requiring complementary shifts recruit prefrontal contributions. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p332]
