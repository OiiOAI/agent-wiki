# Managerial Training

## Summary

Training is one of the highest-leverage activities a manager can perform. Grove argues that managers themselves should conduct training because it must be closely tied to actual organizational practices, be a continuing process rather than a one-time event, and require a suitable role model.

## Key facts

- Training is one of the highest-leverage activities a manager can perform; twelve hours of preparation and instruction to ten students can yield 200 hours of equivalent organizational output at 1% improvement. [raw/books/business/High_Output_Management.epub#L2061-L2065]
- For training to be effective, it must be closely tied to how things are actually done in the organization and maintain a reliable, consistent presence. [raw/books/business/High_Output_Management.epub#L2068-L2071]
- Training should be a process, not an event—it must be systematic and scheduled rather than a rescue effort for immediate problems. [raw/books/business/High_Output_Management.epub#L2071-L2073]
- Managers should train their direct subordinates and perhaps the next few ranks below them, creating a chain of training throughout the organization. [raw/books/business/High_Output_Management.epub#L2078-L2080]
- At Intel, 2% to 4% of employee time is spent in classroom learning, with much of the instruction given by managerial staff. [raw/books/business/High_Output_Management.epub#L2088-L2090]
- The two different training tasks are teaching new employees skills needed to perform their jobs and teaching new ideas, principles, or skills to current employees. [raw/books/business/High_Output_Management.epub#L2097-L2100]
- Training is harder than doing—much deeper knowledge of a task is required to teach it than to simply do it. [raw/books/business/High_Output_Management.epub#L2124-L2126]
- The trainer will have learned the most from developing and delivering the course. [raw/books/business/High_Output_Management.epub#L2127-L2128]
