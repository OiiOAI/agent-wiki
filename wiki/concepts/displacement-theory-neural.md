# Displacement theory (neural)

## Summary

The displacement theory proposes that changes in relative proportions within the brain produce competitive-selective consequences on function. Enlarged brain structures gain competitive advantage in the battle for influence over target synaptic activity. The relative enlargement of one structure compared to another translates into displacement of connections during development and displacement of computational influence in adulthood. This theory extends classic notions of proper mass by extrapolating to circuitry relationships.

## Key facts

- If one brain structure is relatively enlarged compared to another, this should translate into both displacement of connections during development and displacement of computational influence in adulthood with respect to competing inputs from other brain structures. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p239]
- The principal effect of changes in relative scale within the brain will also have a competitive-selective consequence on function: more inputs equals more votes influencing the computational outcome. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p239]
- Shifts in relative proportions will be translated into functional trade-offs: the class of neural computations supported by an enlarged region will tend to have more influence over final global outputs than that supported by a reduced region. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p239]
- Differential growth alters the context of functional competition and parcellation so that certain classes of connections—those with input and output connections to other enlarged regions—gain an unfair competitive advantage. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p240]
