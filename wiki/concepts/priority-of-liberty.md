# Priority of Liberty

## Summary

The Priority of Liberty is the lexical ordering of Rawls's two principles of justice, placing the equal liberties first in a serial order. This means that the first principle cannot be sacrificed for greater social or economic benefits; basic liberties must be protected before considerations of efficiency or the difference principle come into play. The priority applies to the complete system of liberties, which must be assessed as a whole rather than each liberty in isolation.

## Key facts

- When lexical order holds, a basic liberty covered by the first principle can be limited only for the sake of liberty itself, only to insure that the same liberty or a different basic liberty is properly protected. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p190]
- The basic liberties must be assessed as a whole, as one system; the worth of one liberty normally depends upon the specification of the other liberties. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p189]
- Liberty and the worth of liberty are distinguished: liberty is the complete system of liberties of equal citizenship, while the worth of liberty is the value to individuals of the rights that the first principle defines. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p191]
- By the priority of liberty, Rawls means the precedence of the principle of equal liberty over the second principle of justice; the two principles are in lexical order. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p225]
- The precedence of liberty means that liberty can be restricted only for the sake of liberty itself, in two cases: a less extensive liberty must strengthen the total system of liberty shared by all, or a lesser liberty must be acceptable to those with the lesser liberty. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p225]
- First, restrictions can derive from natural limitations and accidents of human life or from historical and social contingencies—the question of justice does not arise for these. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p226]
- Second, restrictions may be needed where injustice already exists in social arrangements or individual conduct—the question is what is the just way to answer injustice. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p226]
- The ideal part of the theory assumes strict compliance and works out principles for a well-ordered society; nonideal theory addresses how to proceed under less favorable conditions. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p227]
- As conditions of civilization improve, the marginal significance for our good of further economic and social advantages diminishes relative to the interests of liberty. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p479]
- Beyond some point it becomes irrational from the standpoint of the original position to acknowledge a lesser liberty for the sake of greater material means and amenities of office. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p479]
- The desire for liberty is the chief regulative interest that the parties must suppose they all will have in common in due course, as the veil of ignorance forces them to abstract from the particulars of their plans of life. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p480]
- The basis for self-esteem in a just society is not one's income share but the publicly affirmed distribution of fundamental rights and liberties. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p481]
- The precedence of liberty entails equality in the social bases of esteem. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p483]

## Inferences

- Inference: The priority of liberty is embedded in the original position itself, where parties choose principles behind a veil of ignorance that deprives them of knowledge about their particular social position or natural endowments, ensuring they cannot favor arrangements that sacrifice some persons' liberties for others' advantages.
