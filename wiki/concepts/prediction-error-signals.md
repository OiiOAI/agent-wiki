# Prediction Error Signals

## Summary

Prediction error signals encode the difference between expected and actual outcomes during learning. These signals are central to computational models of learning including temporal difference (TD) learning and the Rescorla-Wagner model, and are implemented in brain regions including the ventral striatum and dopamine neurons.

## Key facts

- Prediction error signals encode the difference between the reward predicted and the actual reward delivered. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroscience of Decision Making - Mandel, David R.,Vartanian, Oshin.pdf#p261]
- Dopamine neurons show phasic bursts in response to unexpected rewards and reward-predicting cues, but decreased activity on omission of expected rewards. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroscience of Decision Making - Mandel, David R.,Vartanian, Oshin.pdf#p264]
- The temporal difference prediction error signal shows a positive response at reward presentation before learning, shifting back to predictive cues after learning is established. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroscience of Decision Making - Mandel, David R.,Vartanian, Oshin.pdf#p262]
- Prediction error signals in the ventral striatum are boosted by dopaminergic agonists and diminished by dopaminergic antagonists. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroscience of Decision Making - Mandel, David R.,Vartanian, Oshin.pdf#p266]
- Prediction error signals are present during aversive as well as appetitive conditioning in the ventral striatum. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroscience of Decision Making - Mandel, David R.,Vartanian, Oshin.pdf#p266]

## Inferences

- Inference: The demonstration that prediction error activity in the striatum follows dopaminergic manipulation supports a causal link between these signals and behavioral learning.
- Inference: The presence of prediction error signals for aversive events suggests that BOLD activity in the ventral striatum reflects more than just dopamine input.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Whether prediction error signals for aversive learning depend on phasic dopamine remains debated, as single-unit studies generally fail to observe strong dopaminergic activity in response to aversive events.
