# Faust Legend

## Summary

McGilchrist uses the Faust legend as a central metaphor for understanding the hemisphere relationship. In Goethe's version, the right hemisphere's desire for understanding and the left hemisphere's means for achieving it can be redeemed if the left returns its products to the right. Goethe's earlier poem 'The Sorcerer's Apprentice' shows the master (right) bidding the foolish apprentice (left) understand that only the master can safely conjure spirits. McGilchrist argues the modern situation is worse: a Faust who has no insight into his errors.

## Key facts

- In Goethe's Faust, God—not the devil—takes Faust's soul, acknowledging the value of his endless striving when his knowledge is directed towards helping others rather than himself. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p329]
- In 'The Sorcerer's Apprentice', Goethe refers to the returning sorcerer as 'der alte Meister' (the old master), bidding the foolish apprentice understand that he alone can conjure spirits safely. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p329]
- In the modern story, the left hemisphere acts like a sorcerer's apprentice that is blithely unaware he is about to drown, a Faust that has no insight into his errors. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p329]
