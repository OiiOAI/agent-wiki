# Brooding

## Summary

Brooding is a default coping mechanism where individuals stew in uncomfortable feelings, endlessly ruminating over hurt, perceived failure, shortcomings, or anxiety. Unlike bottlers, brooders are at least aware of their emotions, but they risk being overwhelmed as emotions gain strength through the rumination process, similar to a hurricane. Brooding is more common in women and involves loss of perspective where molehills become mountains. The research by Brad Bushman found that brooding over anger led to more aggression than bottling or control conditions.

## Key facts

- Brooders stew in their misery, endlessly stirring the pot around, and struggle to compartmentalize as they obsess over hurts or anxieties. [raw/books/psychology/Emotional Agility - Susan David.pdf#p45]
- Brooders are ahead of bottlers in that they are at least 'feeling their feelings'—aware of their emotions. [raw/books/psychology/Emotional Agility - Susan David.pdf#p45]
- Brooding makes emotions gain strength in the same way a hurricane does, circling and picking up more energy with each pass. [raw/books/psychology/Emotional Agility - Susan David.pdf#p45]
- Brad Bushman's study found that brooding over anger led to more aggressive behavior than bottling or control conditions. [raw/books/psychology/Emotional Agility - Susan David.pdf#p46]
- Co-brooding with friends or colleagues doesn't lead to resolution and likely results in feeling even more annoyed. [raw/books/psychology/Emotional Agility - Susan David.pdf#p46-47]
- Type 2 thoughts occur when people layer unhelpful thoughts about thoughts—'I worry that I worry so much'. [raw/books/psychology/Emotional Agility - Susan David.pdf#p47]
