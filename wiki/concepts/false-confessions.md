# False Confessions

## Summary

False confessions occur when individuals admit to crimes they did not commit. In some cases, people develop the false belief that they committed a crime due to coercive questioning, social pressure, and suggestion. Hugo Munsterberg first drew attention to this phenomenon in 1908. Research by Gisli Gudjonsson identifies 'interrogative suggestibility'—the tendency to change claims in response to misleading information. Experimental studies by Saul Kassin show that approximately 70% of college students can be induced to sign false confessions under certain conditions.

## Key facts

- Hugo Munsterberg was the first psychologist to call attention to false confessions in which people develop the false belief that they committed a crime, observing that emotional stress combined with social pressure and suggestion could distort memory. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p163]
- Gisli Gudjonsson developed a scale for measuring 'interrogative suggestibility'—the tendency to change claims about the past in response to misleading information and suggestive questions. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p164]
- Gudjonsson found that people who made criminal confessions they later retracted were more influenced by suggestive questions than 'deniers' who refused to acknowledge involvement despite forensic evidence against them. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p165]
- In Kassin's experiments, college students were falsely accused of hitting the Alt key on a computer; nearly 70% eventually signed a false confession, and 35% produced detailed false recollections of how they made the error. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p165]
- In 2015, Shaw and Porter reported that 70% of college students developed false memories of committing crimes as adolescents using suggestive procedures, though this figure was later revised to 25-30% when using stricter criteria distinguishing false beliefs from false memories. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p181]

## Inferences

- Inference: False confessions involve not just coerced statements but actual internalization of false beliefs about guilt, suggesting deep memory distortion mechanisms.
- Inference: The distinctiveness heuristic—expecting to remember distinctive details of significant events—normally protects against false confessions but can be undermined when circumstances make forgetting seem plausible.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The exact proportion of false confessions that involve internalized false beliefs versus coerced compliance is unknown.
