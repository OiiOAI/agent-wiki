# Déjà Vu

## Summary

Déjà vu is a misattribution phenomenon characterized by an intense feeling of familiarity for a novel experience, accompanied by conviction that a present experience is identical to a past one and the feeling of knowing what will happen next. The French psychiatrist François-Léon Arnaud described it as a misattribution of current sensations onto the past rather than an actual memory. Modern research by Anne Cleary shows that configurally similar scenes can induce déjà vu even when people don't consciously recall the related original scene.

## Key facts

- In 1896, French psychiatrist François-Léon Arnaud introduced the term déjà vu and characterized it as misattribution of current sensations to the past rather than an actual memory. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p125]
- Déjà vu is characterized by its intensity, conviction that a present experience is identical to a past one, and the feeling that one knows precisely what is going to happen next. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p125]
- Bruce Whittlesea's 1993 experiment showed that people misattribute fast, fluent processing of highly predictable words to having seen them earlier. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p126]
- Anne Cleary's research showed that people are more likely to report déjà vu to configurally similar novel scenes when they fail to recall the original related scene. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p149]
- During déjà vu, people often feel they know which way to turn next but cannot predict with above-chance accuracy what the next turn will be. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p150]
