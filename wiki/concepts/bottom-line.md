# Bottom Line

## Summary

A 'bottom line' is a predetermined worst acceptable outcome in negotiation—the highest price a buyer would pay or the lowest amount a seller would accept. While it provides some protection against accepting bad deals, Fisher and Ury argue it involves high costs: it limits learning during negotiation, inhibits imaginative solutions, and is likely to be set too high or too low based on arbitrary rather than principled assessment.

## Key facts

- A bottom line limits your ability to benefit from what you learn during negotiation because by definition it is a position not to be changed. [raw/books/strategy/Getting_to_Yes_Fisher_Ury.pdf#p50]
- A bottom line also inhibits imagination—it reduces the incentive to invent tailor-made solutions which would reconcile differing interests. [raw/books/strategy/Getting_to_Yes_Fisher_Ury.pdf#p50]
- A bottom line is likely to be set too high. In a family setting, those hoping for a higher price will dominate the discussion. [raw/books/strategy/Getting_to_Yes_Fisher_Ury.pdf#p51]
- It is most unlikely that any arbitrarily selected bottom line truly reflects your interests, unlike a BATNA which is based on realistic alternatives. [raw/books/strategy/Getting_to_Yes_Fisher_Ury.pdf#p51]

## Inferences

- Inference: The contrast between bottom line and BATNA represents a fundamental shift from positional thinking (fixed targets) to interest-based thinking (realistic alternatives).
