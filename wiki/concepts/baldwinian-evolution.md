# Baldwinian Evolution

## Summary

Baldwinian evolution is a modification of Darwinian natural selection proposed by James Mark Baldwin in 1896, whereby learning and behavioral flexibility can amplify and bias natural selection by enabling organisms to modify the selective context affecting future kin. Unlike Lamarckian inheritance, it does not claim that acquired traits are directly passed to offspring, but rather that behavioral flexibility allows organisms to enter new niches, creating new selection pressures on subsequent generations.

## Key facts

- Baldwin proposed that by temporarily adjusting behaviors or physiological responses during its lifespan in response to novel conditions, an animal could produce irreversible changes in the adaptive context of future generations. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p347]
- Conrad Waddington described genetic assimilation as the process whereby natural selection replaces flexible adaptive responses with genetic predispositions, making the production of adaptive responses progressively more canalized. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p349]
- The ability to learn and thus inherit acquired behaviors may be one of the most powerful sources of evolutionary change, providing an organism with access to a repertoire of potential adaptations. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p351]
- Language adaptations depend on Baldwinian processes because symbol acquisition required prefrontal cortex processes that were invariant across applications, dependent on a specific common neural substrate in all brains, and imposed significant demands on an otherwise underdeveloped cognitive process. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Symbolic Species - Terrence W. Deacon.pdf#p361]

## Inferences

- Inference: Baldwinian evolution explains how symbolic communication could have changed human brains without requiring direct genetic encoding of language rules.
- Inference: The discontinuity between stimulus associations and symbolic reference associations, which makes symbolic associations difficult to learn, also makes them impossible to assimilate genetically.
