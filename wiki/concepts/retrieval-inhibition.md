# Retrieval Inhibition

## Summary

Retrieval inhibition is a form of blocking that occurs when retrieving some memories inhibits recall of related information. Michael Anderson's research using the think/no-think paradigm showed that suppressing items during retrieval makes them less accessible later. This has implications for understanding repression, eyewitness memory, and intrusive memories. Neuroimaging studies show prefrontal cortex involvement during suppression and decreased hippocampal activity.

## Key facts

- Retrieval inhibition occurs when the act of retrieving information from memory inhibits subsequent recall of related information. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p113]
- The think/no-think paradigm showed that suppressing items during retrieval makes them less accessible later, even when participants were paid to recall them. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p122]
- A 2020 meta-analysis confirmed a modest but significant suppression effect in healthy individuals and a reduced suppression effect in individuals who were anxious or depressed. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p123]
- During the 'no think' phase, parts of the prefrontal cortex show increased activity while the hippocampus shows decreased activity, and these changes predict later recall impairment. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Seven Sins of Memory - Daniel L. Schacter.pdf#p123]
- A 2017 study found that higher resting GABA concentration in the hippocampus is associated with greater ability to suppress 'no think' items and stronger coupling between hippocampus and prefrontal cortex during suppression.
