# Disruption Theory

## Summary

Disruption theory, originally developed by Clayton Christensen and Joseph Bower, proposes that established companies fail when they focus too much on their largest customers and miss lower-performance, lower-price competitors. However, subsequent research has challenged this theory, finding that incumbents often succeed through incremental improvement and that disruption can also come from above with better, more expensive products.

## Key facts

- Christensen and Bower's theory focused on why leading companies failed to 'stay on top' when competitors employed new technologies. [raw/books/strategy/The Crux - Richard Rumelt.pdf#p166]
- Historian Jill Lepore found that victory in the disk-drive industry went to manufacturers good at incremental improvements, whether or not they were first to market the disruptive new format. [raw/books/strategy/The Crux - Richard Rumelt.pdf#p168]
- Researchers Sood and Tellis found no strong pattern of low-price, low-performance disruption in their study of thirty-six new technologies. [raw/books/strategy/The Crux - Richard Rumelt.pdf#p168]
- The iPhone was high priced yet disruptive to BlackBerry and Nokia, contradicting the 'from below' disruption model. [raw/books/strategy/The Crux - Richard Rumelt.pdf#p167]

## Inferences

- Inference: Real disruption challenges fall into three categories: (A) responding costs more than it's worth, (B) the organization lacks necessary ability, or (C) the entire ecosystem collapses.
