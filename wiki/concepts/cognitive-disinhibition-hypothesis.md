# Cognitive Disinhibition Hypothesis

## Summary

The Cognitive Disinhibition Hypothesis (CDH), proposed by Martindale (1999), suggests that creative individuals are not characterized by a stable disposition toward defocused attention but are better at adjusting their focus of attention depending on task demands. Under conditions where the problem is relatively ill-defined and ambiguity is high, creative people defocus attention, making the central task more susceptible to interference by seemingly irrelevant information. Under well-defined conditions with low ambiguity, creative people focus attention to inhibit irrelevant stimuli.

## Key facts

- The Cognitive Disinhibition Hypothesis proposes that creative people exhibit differential rather than reduced focusing of attention depending on task demands. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroscience of Decision Making - Mandel, David R.,Vartanian, Oshin.pdf#p420]
- Under ill-defined, ambiguous conditions, creative people defocus attention, which may allow seemingly irrelevant information to provide building blocks for creative solutions. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroscience of Decision Making - Mandel, David R.,Vartanian, Oshin.pdf#p420]
- Under well-defined, unambiguous conditions, creative people focus attention to inhibit irrelevant stimuli and focus on promising candidate ideas. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroscience of Decision Making - Mandel, David R.,Vartanian, Oshin.pdf#p420]
- Martindale and Hines (1975) found that creative people showed lower cortical arousal during creativity tests but higher arousal during intelligence tests, unlike less creative subjects who showed equally high arousal across both. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroscience of Decision Making - Mandel, David R.,Vartanian, Oshin.pdf#p421]
- Martindale and Hasenfus (1978) showed that creative story generation involves defocusing during the inspiration phase but focusing during the elaboration phase. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroscience of Decision Making - Mandel, David R.,Vartanian, Oshin.pdf#p421]
- Vartanian, Martindale, and Kwiatkowski (2007) found that creative people are faster on tasks with unambiguous stimuli (narrowed attention) but slower on tasks with ambiguous, interference-inducing stimuli (widened attention). [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroscience of Decision Making - Mandel, David R.,Vartanian, Oshin.pdf#p422]
- The regulatory process for attention in creative people is argued to be automatic or reactive rather than requiring self-control, driven by the structure of the problem space. [raw/books/neuroscience/Neuroscience of Decision Making - Mandel, David R.,Vartanian, Oshin.pdf#p424]

## Inferences

- Inference: The evidence suggests that CDH is a state rather than a trait—creative people flexibly adjust their attentional focus based on task demands rather than maintaining a constant level of defocused attention.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The relationship between self-control and the attentional flexibility described by CDH remains debated, with some evidence suggesting top-down processes may also influence creative cognition.
