# Motivated Reasoning

## Summary

The psychological phenomenon where reasoning processes serve to reach pre-determined conclusions rather than discover truth. When people want to believe something, they ask 'Can I believe it?' and search for supporting evidence; when they don't want to believe something, they ask 'Must I believe it?' and search for contrary evidence. This explains why people can justify almost any conclusion and why scientific evidence often fails to persuade the public.

## Key facts

- When people want to believe something, they ask 'Can I believe it?' and stop searching after finding even a single piece of pseudo-evidence. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p101]
- When people don't want to believe something, they ask 'Must I believe it?' and dismiss claims if they find a single reason to doubt. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p101]
- Subjects wait longer for ambiguous test results when a color change is desirable than when it indicates a deficiency (Ditto's research). [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p101]
- Motivated reasoning even influences visual perception; subjects are more likely to see an ambiguous figure as the letter B when it would yield a reward rather than as the number 13. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p101]
- Google enables partisans to 'call up a team of supportive scientists for almost any conclusion twenty-four hours a day'. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p102]
- Mercier and Sperber concluded that reasoning evolved not to find truth but to engage in arguments, persuasion, and manipulation. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p106]
- Motivated reasoning means we alter interpretation of new information to fit our beliefs rather than altering beliefs to fit new information. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking_in_Bets.epub#L665-L668]
- The Dartmouth-Princeton study showed students from each school saw different games when watching the same film—Princeton students saw Dartmouth commit twice as many flagrant penalties. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking_in_Bets.epub#L680-L695]
- A 2012 Stanford Law Review study 'They Saw a Protest' confirmed that what subjects saw depended on congruence of protestor positions with their cultural values. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking_in_Bets.epub#L700-L710]
- West, Meserve, and Stanovich (2012) found that blind-spot bias is greater the smarter you are—in six of seven biases tested, more cognitively sophisticated participants showed larger bias blind spots. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking_in_Bets.epub#L720-L730]
- Dan Kahan's research found that more numerate people made more mistakes interpreting data on emotionally charged topics than less numerate subjects sharing those same beliefs. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking_in_Bets.epub#L740-L750]
- Motivated reasoning and self-serving bias are two habits of mind that are deeply rooted in how our brains work. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking_in_Bets.epub#L1268-L1272]
- We have a huge investment in confirmatory thought and fall into these biases all the time without even knowing it. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking_in_Bets.epub#L1268-L1272]
- Confirmatory thought is hard to spot, hard to change, and, if attempted, hard to self-reinforce. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking_in_Bets.epub#L1268-L1272]
- Being in an environment where the challenge of a bet is always looming works to reduce motivated reasoning by changing the frame through which we view disconfirming information. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking_in_Bets.epub#L1315-L1320]

## Inferences

- Inference: The confirmation bias is a built-in feature of an argumentative mind, not a bug that can be removed—this explains why teaching students to look at the other side is extremely difficult
- Inference: The book argues that being smart actually makes motivated reasoning worse because smarter people are better at constructing narratives supporting their beliefs and rationalizing data to fit their arguments.
