# Cuckoo Manipulation

## Summary

Cuckoo nestlings manipulate the behaviour of their foster parents through powerful stimuli like the red gape, acting like an addictive drug on the host's nervous system. This represents extended phenotypic action at a distance by cuckoo genes. The 'life/dinner principle' explains why hosts have not evolved complete resistance: the cost of failure is asymmetrical between parasite and host.

## Key facts

- Cuckoo nestlings act on the host's nervous system in the same way as an addictive drug, with the red gape being a powerful superstimulus. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p290]
- Cuckoo genes have extended phenotypic effects on host behaviour: cuckoo genes influencing the colour and shape of gape indirectly affect foster parent behaviour. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p292]
- The life/dinner principle states that there is an asymmetry in the cost of failure: a cuckoo nestling that loses its hold over its host dies, while a host that fails to resist may still rear another brood next season. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p291]

## Inferences

- Inference: The life/dinner principle explains the apparent suboptimality of host resistance: resisting manipulation may be too costly relative to the occasional failure it prevents.
