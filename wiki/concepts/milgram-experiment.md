# Milgram Experiment

## Summary

Stanley Milgram's famous psychology experiment where 65% of ordinary people were willing to administer apparently harmful electric shocks to protesting victims simply because a scientific authority commanded them to. Munger uses this to illustrate how multiple psychological principles combine (authority, social proof, incentive, consistency) to override moral judgment.

## Key facts

- In Milgram's experiment, sixty-five percent of subjects were willing to give apparently harmful electric shocks to a pitifully protesting victim because a scientific authority commanded them to. [raw/books/strategy/Poor Charlie's Almanack_ Charles T_ Munger, Peter D_ Kaufman, Ed Wexler, Warren E_pdf.pdf#p242]
- Milgram performed the experiment to understand how Hitler got believing Lutherans and Catholics to perform unholy acts they should have known were wrong. [raw/books/strategy/Poor Charlie's Almanack_ Charles T_ Munger, Peter D_ Kaufman, Ed Wexler, Warren E_pdf.pdf#p242]
- The Milgram experiment harnesses at least six psychological principles operating together, not just the authority principle typically cited. [raw/books/strategy/Poor Charlie's Almanack_ Charles T_ Munger, Peter D_ Kaufman, Ed Wexler, Warren E_pdf.pdf#p243]

## Inferences

- Inference: Munger's analysis suggests that psychology textbooks miss critical explanations by focusing on single factors rather than the lollapalooza combination of forces.
