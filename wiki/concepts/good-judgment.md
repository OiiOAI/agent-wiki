# Good Judgment

## Summary

Good judgment in strategy involves knowing yourself, your abilities and biases, knowing other individuals, understanding small groups, and comprehending large groups and markets. Rumelt argues that good judgment can be improved with practice, and that effective practice requires first committing judgments to writing through pre-commitment.

## Key facts

- Judgment begins with knowing yourself, your abilities and biases, then extends to knowing other individuals. [raw/books/strategy/Good strategy, bad strategy _ the difference and why it - Richard P_ Rumelt.pdf#p98]
- Judgment about small groups is the domain of managers, trial lawyers, and platoon leaders. [raw/books/strategy/Good strategy, bad strategy _ the difference and why it - Richard P_ Rumelt.pdf#p98]
- Judgment about large groups and markets is the domain of marketing experts, corporate management, and political leadership. [raw/books/strategy/Good strategy, bad strategy _ the difference and why it - Richard P_ Rumelt.pdf#p98]
- Good judgment can be improved with practice, and for that practice to be effective, one should first commit judgments to writing. [raw/books/strategy/Good strategy, bad strategy _ the difference and why it - Richard P_ Rumelt.pdf#p98]
- To commit to a judgment is to choose an interpretation of which issues are critical and which are not, and then to choose an implied action. [raw/books/strategy/Good strategy, bad strategy _ the difference and why it - Richard P_ Rumelt.pdf#p99]
- By committing to a judgment one increases the probability of disagreeing with others' assessments and thereby increases the chance of learning something. [raw/books/strategy/Good strategy, bad strategy _ the difference and why it - Richard P_ Rumelt.pdf#p99]

## Inferences

- Inference: Pre-commitment to written judgments creates opportunities for learning by enabling comparison between predictions and outcomes.
