# General Systems Theory

## Summary

General Systems Theory, developed by Ludwig von Bertalanffy starting in the 1950s, provided the first coherent framework describing principles of organization of living systems. Bertalanffy argued that living systems are 'open systems'—in contrast to closed systems studied by classical thermodynamics—that maintain themselves far from equilibrium through continual exchange of matter and energy with their environment. This theory integrated insights from biology, cybernetics, and process-thinking to establish systems thinking as a major scientific movement emphasizing interdisciplinary approaches to understanding complexity.

## Key facts

- Bertalanffy is credited with the first formulation of a comprehensive theoretical framework describing principles of organization of living systems, advocating a science of wholeness. [raw/books/strategy/ScienceStrategyWar_Osinga.pdf#p115]
- Bertalanffy recognized that living systems are open systems that cannot be described by classical thermodynamics, solving a dilemma that had puzzled scientists since the 19th century. [raw/books/strategy/ScienceStrategyWar_Osinga.pdf#p115]
- An open system is defined as 'a system in exchange of matter with its environment, presenting import and export, building up and breaking down of its material components,' unlike closed systems isolated from their environment. [raw/books/strategy/ScienceStrategyWar_Osinga.pdf#p115]
- Bertalanffy's Ashby's requisite variety principle states that internal regulatory mechanisms of a system must be as diverse as the environment it faces; greater variety means more states a system can achieve. [raw/books/strategy/ScienceStrategyWar_Osinga.pdf#p115]
- Bertalanffy extended the concept of homeostasis (internal self-regulation) to external interactions between a system and its environment, describing living systems as maintaining 'steady state' through continual flow and change. [raw/books/strategy/ScienceStrategyWar_Osinga.pdf#p115]
- Bertalanffy remarked that systems thinking implies 'a basic re-orientation of scientific thinking' to deal with complexities, 'wholes' or 'systems' in all fields of knowledge. [raw/books/strategy/ScienceStrategyWar_Osinga.pdf#p126]

## Inferences

- Inference: Boyd's emphasis on maintaining communication with the external environment to avoid entropy and dissolution directly reflects Bertalanffy's open systems theory.
- Inference: The integration of cybernetics into General Systems Theory in the 1960s created the theoretical foundation for later complexity theory developments.
