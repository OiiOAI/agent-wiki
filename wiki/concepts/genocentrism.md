# Genocentrism

## Summary

Genocentrism is the view that the fundamental units of life are not organisms but genes, advocating a 'gene's-eye view of evolution.' According to this perspective, genes vary, multiply by copying themselves, and evolve through natural selection. Organisms are conceived as 'vehicles' made by and for genes, enabling genes to replicate more successfully. Genocentrism accepts the adaptationist thesis of the received evolutionary view but shifts perspective to genes as the ultimate source and beneficiary of phenotypic adaptation.

## Key facts

- Genocentrism holds that genes are the fundamental units of life, organisms are vehicles constructed by and for genes, and genes evolve through competitive interaction and natural selection. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p171]
- According to genocentrism, replication and interaction are the two processes that make up evolution: replicators form lineages, and interactors mediate their interaction with the environment so they are differentially copied. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p171]
- Genocentrism is homologous to computationalism about the mind: both perpetuate hardware/software and body/mind dualisms while mischaracterizing the role of particular subsystems in fundamentally dynamic phenomena. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p172]
- The metaphor of DNA as program or information store is fundamentally misleading because DNA replication and gene activation depend entirely on the autopoiesis of the cell. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p177]

## Inferences

- Inference: The deepest fault of the DNA-as-information metaphor is that it implies a dualist framework of matter and information, structurally isomorphic to computationalist mind-body dualism.
- Inference: Thompson argues that despite its modern scientific garb, the informational dualism expressed by Dawkins and Dennett is philosophically less sophisticated than ancient soul-body dualism because it claims information 'passes through bodies and affects them, but is not affected by them.'
