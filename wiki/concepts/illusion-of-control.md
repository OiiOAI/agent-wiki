# Illusion of Control

## Summary

Langer's landmark dissertation research at Yale demonstrated that people irrationally believe they can control random outcomes, such as preferring chosen lottery tickets with familiar letters over unchosen tickets with better odds, or wagering more against incompetent-looking opponents in games of pure chance. Later, Langer revised her view, arguing that the illusion of control is not always an illusion from the actor's perspective and serves as a necessary psychological strategy that helps people cope with uncertainty, providing real psychological benefits.

## Key facts

- Langer's lottery ticket study showed that over four times as many people wanted to keep a ticket they had chosen with a familiar letter compared to an unchosen ticket with better odds. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p42]
- People who were more practiced with a game of luck were also more confident of their success, even though skill does not affect chance outcomes. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p43]
- In a competition study, people wagered much more against an opponent perceived as an incompetent schnook even though the competition involved drawing a high card—a game of pure chance where opponent skill is irrelevant. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p43-44]
- Langer revised her view, arguing that the illusion of control is not always an illusion for the person who holds it; believing in control provides real psychological power and helps people cope with stress. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p46]
- Elevator close-door buttons often do not function but still provide riders a sense of control that reduces stress, illustrating that the illusion of control has real benefits. [raw/books/psychology/The Mindful Body_ Thinking Our Way to Chro - Ellen J. Langer.pdf#p45-46]
