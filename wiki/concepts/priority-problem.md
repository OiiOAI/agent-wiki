# Priority Problem

## Summary

The priority problem is the question of how to assign weights to competing principles of justice when they conflict. Intuitionism denies that there exists any useful and explicit solution, holding that we must rely on intuitive capacities. Rawls argues that we should do what we can to reduce reliance on intuition while recognizing it cannot be entirely eliminated. Justice as fairness addresses the priority problem through several means: the grounds for accepting principles may also support giving them certain weights; principles may be arranged in serial or lexical order; and more limited questions can be posed that substitute prudential for moral judgment.

## Key facts

- The priority problem is the problem of assigning weights to competing principles of justice when they conflict. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p47]
- Mill and Sidgwick believed that at some point we must have a single principle to straighten out and systematize our judgments, maintaining that our moral judgments are implicitly utilitarian. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p47]
- A serial or lexical order requires satisfying the first principle before moving to the second, with earlier principles having absolute weight with respect to later ones. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p49]
- In justice as fairness, the priority problem is addressed by singling out a certain position in the social system (the least advantaged) from which the system is to be judged, substituting a judgment of rational prudence for ethical judgment. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p49]
- The practical aim of addressing the priority problem is to reach reasonably reliable agreement in judgment to provide a common conception of justice. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p50]
