# Wish Fulfillment

## Summary

Wish fulfillment, as formulated in Freud's 1895 Project, refers to the primary process mechanism by which wishes are hallucinatorially realized through the cathexis of mnemic images. Freud argued that 'the meaning of dreams as wish fulfilments' represents 'primary processes following upon experiences of satisfaction.' The wishful state activates memories associated with previous satisfaction, attempting to reproduce the experience of satisfaction through cathexis of the desired object's mnemic image.

## Key facts

- Freud stated: 'The meaning of dreams (of normal ones, at all events) can be established with certainty. They [dreams] are wish fulfilments—that is, primary processes following upon experiences of satisfaction.'. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p412]
- In a wishful state, 'the cathexis will also pass over on to the two memories [of object and specific action] and will activate them. Probably the mnemic image of the object will be the first to be affected by the wishful activation.'. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p391]
- Freud acknowledged: 'I do not doubt that in the first instance this wishful activation will produce the same thing as a perception—namely a hallucination. If reflex action is thereupon introduced, disappointment cannot fail to occur.'. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p391]
- Freud first articulated the theory of wish fulfillment definitively on July 24, 1895, when he analyzed his own dream of 'Irma's injection' in correspondence with Fliess. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p1264]
- The discovery of the omnipresence in dreams of the wish to sleep was announced by Freud as late as June 9, 1899 (Letter 108 to Fliess). [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p1267]
- Freud wrote that dreams are the guardians of sleep, generating a regressive hallucinatory state so the subject does not act on impulses in reality. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p1270]
- Dreams are psychical acts whose driving force is in every instance a wish seeking fulfilment; their not being recognizable as wishes is due to the influence of psychical censorship. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p1774]
- The dream process is transformed into a dream for the sake of fulfilling a wish; a daytime thought (residue) serves as entrepreneur but requires a wish from the unconscious as capital. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p1774]
- Unconscious wishes are always active and seek expression; they are 'immortal,' capable of being awakened to new life whenever an opportunity arises. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p1791]
- A dream wish must be an infantile one; in adults it originates from the unconscious; in children, where no division exists between preconscious and unconscious, it is an unfulfilled, unrepressed wish from waking life. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p1793]
- Anxiety dreams and unpleasurable dreams are just as much wish fulfillments as straightforward satisfaction dreams, though they may express a repressed wish whose fulfillment the ego experiences as distressing. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p1795]
- Every dream can be replaced by an optative clause expressing a wish; dreams represent wishes as already fulfilled, showing fulfillment as real and present. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p1882]
- Dreams of the second class—disguised wish fulfillments—form the overwhelming majority and require analysis to be understood. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p1903]
- The third class of dreams—repressed wishes with insufficient disguise—are accompanied by anxiety which replaces dream distortion. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p1904]
- The dream wishes are invariably derived from the unconscious, representing unconscious wishful impulses seeking discharge. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p1840]
- The part played by wishes in dream formation is the same as in psychoneurotic symptom formation—both represent compromises between repressed impulses and resisting forces. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p1839]
- What instigates a dream is a wish, and the fulfillment of that wish is the content of the dream. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p4904]
- A dream does not simply give expression to a thought but represents the wish fulfilled as a hallucinatory experience. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p4904]
- Linguistic usage has a suspicion of wish fulfillment as a chief characteristic of dreams; proverbs such as 'Pigs dream of acorns and geese dream of maize' assert that dreams satisfy needs. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p4905]
- Dreams fulfill not only wishes but also unmet bodily needs (hunger, thirst, sexual need), as demonstrated by dreams of prisoners and explorers under privation. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p4907]
- Freud cites Otto Nordenskjöld's Antarctic expedition where expedition members regularly dreamed of eating and drinking during periods of food shortage. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p4907]
- The wish to sleep is recognized as regularly playing a part in the construction of dreams, revealed openly in 'dreams of convenience' where the dreamer dreams of being already awake. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p4908-4909]
- Plato's saying that 'the good are those who are content to dream of what the others, the bad, really do' reflects the wish-fulfilling nature of dreams. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p4921]

## Inferences

- Inference: Wish fulfillment theory connects the 'experience of satisfaction' (Section 11) with dream theory, establishing the link between infantile needs and adult psychological functioning.
- Inference: The concept that dreams are 'wish fulfillments' represents one of Freud's most enduring theoretical contributions, though later modified to include anxiety dreams and punishment dreams.
- Inference: Even distressing dreams that seem to express worries rather than wishes must find a connection to an unconscious wish to become dream-worthy; the worry provides content while the unconscious wish provides driving force.
- Inference: Punishment dreams represent a special case where the dream-constructing wish belongs to the ego (reacting against the repressed) rather than to the repressed system itself.
- Inference: Children's dreams demonstrate undisguised wish fulfillment most clearly because they have not yet developed the censorship mechanisms that adults possess.
- Inference: Daydreams are wish fulfillments that are thought but never experienced as hallucinations; dreams differ by presenting wish fulfillment as hallucinated experience due to the state of sleep.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Whether every adult dream can be traced to an infantile unconscious wish is acknowledged as not provable universally, though claimed as frequently demonstrable.
