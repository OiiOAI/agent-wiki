# Binocular Rivalry

## Summary

Binocular rivalry is a phenomenon where different images shown to each eye cause conscious perception to alternate between them while sensory input remains constant. This allows researchers to distinguish brain activity that tracks conscious perception from activity that tracks sensory input, making it a key tool for identifying neural correlates of consciousness.

## Key facts

- In binocular rivalry, a different image shown to each eye causes perception to flip back and forth between them. [raw/books/neuroscience/Being You _ A New Science of Consciousness - Seth, Anil.pdf#p33]
- Conscious perception changes while sensory input remains constant, allowing researchers to identify NCCs. [raw/books/neuroscience/Being You _ A New Science of Consciousness - Seth, Anil.pdf#p33]
- Brain regions showing up in binocular rivalry studies are often associated with transitions between conscious perceptions. [raw/books/neuroscience/Being You _ A New Science of Consciousness - Seth, Anil.pdf#p39]
- Binocular rivalry occurs when distinct images are shown to each eye, causing perception to alternate between them rather than fuse. [raw/books/neuroscience/Consciousness and the Brain_ Deciphering H - Stanislas Dehaene.pdf#p41]
- Charles Wheatstone invented the stereoscope in 1838 to study this phenomenon. [raw/books/neuroscience/Consciousness and the Brain_ Deciphering H - Stanislas Dehaene.pdf#p41]
- Leopold and Logothetis found that neurons in early visual cortex (V1, V2) reflected objective stimuli, while neurons in higher areas (IT, STS) correlated with subjective awareness. [raw/books/neuroscience/Consciousness and the Brain_ Deciphering H - Stanislas Dehaene.pdf#p44]
- Binocular rivalry depends on attention: when attention is removed, the two images are jointly processed and no longer compete. [raw/books/neuroscience/Consciousness and the Brain_ Deciphering H - Stanislas Dehaene.pdf#p47]
- Binocular rivalry occurs when different images are presented to each eye, causing perceptual alternation. [raw/books/neuroscience/Consciousness and the Brain_ Deciphering H - Stanislas Dehaene.pdf#p27-30]
- The phenomenon is used to study conscious visual perception in humans and monkeys. [raw/books/neuroscience/Consciousness and the Brain_ Deciphering H - Stanislas Dehaene.pdf#p244-247]
- Neuronal activity in inferior temporal cortex reflects conscious perception during rivalry. [raw/books/neuroscience/Consciousness and the Brain_ Deciphering H - Stanislas Dehaene.pdf#p28-31]
- Logothetis and colleagues used binocular rivalry to study the neural basis of consciousness in monkeys. [raw/books/neuroscience/Consciousness and the Brain_ Deciphering H - Stanislas Dehaene.pdf#p28]
- In binocular rivalry, two different images are presented so each eye sees only one; people most commonly see first one image, then the next, with horizontal and vertical stripes alternating back and forth spontaneously. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p393]
- Eric Lumer and colleagues at University College London identified frontal and parietal cortical areas as the regions that become active when conscious attention switches from one image to another in binocular rivalry. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p394]
- People with damage to the prefrontal cortex have difficulty switching from one image to the other in situations of binocular rivalry. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p394]
- Binocular rivalry occurs when each eye has a different visual input for the same part of the visual field, and one sees first one input, then the other, in alternation. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p219]
- Logothetis and Schall trained macaque monkeys to signal whether seeing upward or downward movement while upward movement was projected into one eye and downward into the other. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p219]
- The monkey's percept changes every second or so even though the motion stimulus coming into the eyes is always the same. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p220]
- The firing of some MT neurons correlates with the percept, but for others the average firing rate is relatively unchanged and independent of which direction of movement the monkey is seeing. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p221]
- This result makes it unlikely that all neurons firing in the visual cortex at one moment are correlates of our visual percept. [raw/books/neuroscience/The astonishing hypothesis _ the scientific search for the - Crick, Francis.pdf#p221]

## Inferences

- Inference: The fact that rivalry depends on attention suggests that the competition for conscious access occurs at a level where attention can influence processing. [Supporting facts: p47]
- Inference: Binocular rivalry provides a 'minimal contrast' between identical visual input with different conscious contents, isolating consciousness variables from sensory input

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Whether the monkey was overtrained and exhibiting automatic rather than aware responses is a concern, though rivalry shows little variation with training.
