# Germ-line therapy

## Summary

Germ-line therapy involves modifying DNA in sperm, eggs, or embryos so that changes are passed to all future offspring. Combined with preimplantation genetic haplotyping (PGH), this technology could allow prospective parents to eliminate genetic diseases or potentially select for desired traits in their children.

## Key facts

- Germ-line therapy changes the embryo's DNA including in reproductive cells, so changes are passed to offspring—the altered DNA is passed to all future generations. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p386]
- Preimplantation genetic haplotyping (PGH) developed at Guy's Hospital in London allows screening of thousands of genetic defects from a single cell of an early embryo. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p385]
- Gregory Stock predicts that safeguarding children will include routine PGH, and those who can afford it will no longer leave their children's genetics to chance. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p390]
- An artificial human chromosome could be added to embryos, modified with desired genes including on-and-off switches controlled by chemical injections. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p389]
- In vitro fertilization made human germ-line manipulation possible; 1% of babies born in the United States are now the result of IVF. [raw/books/neuroscience/Human _ the science behind what makes us unique - Michael S_ Gazzaniga.pdf#p385]

## Inferences

- Inference: Stock predicts that germ-line therapy will be driven by parents' genetic programming to safeguard children, similar to how current parents ensure their children survive to reproduce.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Whether germ-line therapy will actually be implemented in humans remains uncertain due to unknown gene interactions and ethical concerns. Parents may be reluctant to interfere with their children's genes.
