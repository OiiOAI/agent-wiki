# Morality of Authority

## Summary

The first stage in Rawls' sequence of moral development, characteristic of the child's situation where the child is not in a position to assess the validity of precepts and injunctions addressed by those in authority, particularly parents. The child lacks both the knowledge and understanding on the basis of which their guidance can be challenged.

## Key facts

- The morality of authority consists in the child being disposed without the prospect of reward or punishment to follow certain precepts that may appear largely arbitrary but are addressed to him by powerful persons who have his love and trust. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p413]
- The conditions favoring the morality of authority being learned are: (1) parents must love the child and be worthy objects of his admiration; (2) they must enunciate clear and intelligible rules adapted to the child's level of comprehension; (3) they should exemplify the morality which they enjoin. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p413]
- The prized virtues of the morality of authority are obedience, humility, and fidelity to authoritative persons; the leading vices are disobedience, self-will, and temerity. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p414]

## Inferences

- Inference: The child's love does not have a rational instrumental explanation: he does not love parents as a means to achieve his initial self-interested ends. With this aim in view he could conceivably act as if he loved them, but his doing so would not constitute a transformation of his original desires.
