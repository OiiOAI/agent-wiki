# Motor Hierarchy

## Summary

The motor systems are organized in a functional hierarchy, with each level concerned with a different decision. The highest level determines the purpose of movement, the next level forms the motor plan based on spatial characteristics, and the lowest level coordinates the spatiotemporal details of muscle contractions needed to execute the planned movement.

## Key facts

- Motor systems are organized in a functional hierarchy with multiple levels, each concerned with a different decision. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p791]
- The highest level of the motor hierarchy, likely requiring the prefrontal cortex, deals with the purpose of a movement. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p791]
- The second level of the hierarchy, concerned with motor plan formation, involves interactions between the posterior parietal and premotor areas of the cerebral cortex. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p791]
- The premotor cortex specifies the spatial characteristics of a movement based on sensory information from the posterior parietal cortex about the environment and body position. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p791]
- The lowest level of the motor hierarchy coordinates spatiotemporal details of muscle contractions and is executed by the primary motor cortex, brain stem, and spinal cord. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p791]

## Inferences

- Inference: While described as serial, evidence suggests that many motor processes can occur in parallel rather than in strict sequential order
