# Prism Adaptation

## Summary

Prism adaptation is a nondeclarative learning task where participants wearing prism glasses that displace vision must gradually adjust their movements to accurately reach targets. Research demonstrated that the cerebellum is necessary for this type of learning, though Henry showed normal adaptation despite cerebellar damage.

## Key facts

- When prism glasses displace the target, participants initially point to the wrong location but gradually update their movements through practice. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p165]
- After removing prisms, control participants continue pointing incorrectly (negative aftereffect), indicating they had adapted to the visual shift. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p165]
- Patients with cerebellar disorders do not show the negative aftereffect, proving their brains did not retain the altered map created by the prisms. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p165]
- Henry performed just like controls on prism adaptation, showing normal adaptation despite marked cerebellar atrophy. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p166]

## Inferences

- Inference: Henry's preserved prism adaptation may have relied on residual cerebellar tissue or alternative neural circuits that remained intact despite his medication-induced cerebellar atrophy.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The specific cerebellar circuits that supported Henry's normal prism adaptation could not be determined until postmortem examination.
