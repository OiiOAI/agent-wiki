# Emotion Command Systems

## Summary

Jaak Panksepp's emotion command system hypothesis proposes that 'the mechanisms of affective experience and emotional behavior are intimately intertwined in comparatively ancient areas of the mammalian brain.' Ancient regions of the limbic system are said to be conserved across mammals, with functions mediated by command systems also being conserved. Panksepp distinguishes primary process affective states (primitive conscious feelings in all mammals) from cognitive conscious feelings created through memory, attention, and language. The fear command system involves the amygdala, hypothalamus, and periaqueductal gray. LeDoux critiques this view, arguing that electrical stimulation techniques are imprecise and that behaviors elicited do not necessarily indicate conscious feelings.

## Key facts

- Panksepp proposes that emotion command systems in ancient limbic areas are conserved across mammals and mediate both feelings and behaviors. [raw/books/neuroscience/Anxious - Joseph LeDoux.pdf#p186]
- The fear command system involves the amygdala, anterior and medial hypothalamus, and periaqueductal gray region. [raw/books/neuroscience/Anxious - Joseph LeDoux.pdf#p188]
- Panksepp distinguishes primary process affective states (primitive conscious feelings in all mammals) from cognitively elaborated conscious feelings. [raw/books/neuroscience/Anxious - Joseph LeDoux.pdf#p186]
- LeDoux argues that electrical stimulation may induce nonconscious central motivational states rather than conscious feelings. [raw/books/neuroscience/Anxious - Joseph LeDoux.pdf#p190]

## Inferences

- Inference: LeDoux proposes that subcortical circuits provide nonconscious ingredients that contribute to feelings but are not themselves the source of conscious emotional feelings.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Whether subcortical stimulation actually elicits conscious emotional feelings in animals or humans remains scientifically unresolved.
