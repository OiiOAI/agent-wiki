# Meiotic Drive

## Summary

Meiotic drive occurs when a gene biases meiosis to increase its own transmission to sperm or egg cells beyond the expected 50% ratio. Segregation distorters are genes that exploit this mechanism, spreading through populations even when detrimental to individual fitness. Examples include the t gene in mice.

## Key facts

- Normal meiosis is a completely fair lottery where half of sperm or egg cells contain one allele and half the other, but meiosis is a physical process influenced by genes. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p276]
- Segregation distorters are genes that bias meiosis so that they are more likely than their allelic partner to end up in the egg, spreading inexorably through a population at the expense of their allele. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p276]
- The t gene in mice is a segregation distorter: a male with one t gene will have up to 95% of his sperm containing the t gene, and when t homozygotes arise they die or are sterile, potentially driving entire populations extinct. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p277]
- Natural selection works at the genic level and favours the segregation distorter, even though its effects at the level of the individual organism are likely to be bad. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p277]

## Inferences

- Inference: The existence of segregation distorters demonstrates that natural selection does not always favour what is best for the individual organism, but rather what is best for the gene.
