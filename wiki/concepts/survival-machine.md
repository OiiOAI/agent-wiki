# Survival Machine

## Summary

Survival machines are organisms—animals, plants, bacteria, and viruses—that serve as vehicles built by genes to ensure their continued existence. Dawkins uses the term to emphasize that organisms are not the fundamental unit of selection but rather disposable vehicles for immortal genes. The first survival machines probably consisted of nothing more than protective coats, but they evolved into increasingly elaborate structures over billions of years.

## Key facts

- Survival machines include all living organisms: animals, plants, bacteria, and viruses. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p47]
- Genes swarm in huge colonies safe inside gigantic lumbering robots (survival machines), communicating with the outside world by tortuous indirect routes. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p46]
- The first survival machines probably consisted of nothing more than a protective coat of protein around the replicator. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p45]
- As new rivals arose with better and more effective survival machines, survival machines got bigger and more elaborate over time. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p45]
- Survival machines are temporary; the genes inside them are the immortals that persist through generations. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p46]
- Survival machines began as passive receptacles for genes providing chemical protection, evolved to exploit plants (animals) and then to exploit other animals. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p74]
- Multicellular survival machines distribute copies of all genes to every cell, with the body functioning as 'a colony of genes' and cells as 'working units for the chemical industries of the genes'. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p74]
- Dawkins prefers to think of the body as a colony of genes rather than a colony of cells, because the cell is merely a convenient working unit. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p74]
- Genes are 'master programmers' and 'the primary policy-makers'; brains are the executives who take moment-to-moment decisions. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p88]
- The logical conclusion of the trend toward brain autonomy would be a single policy instruction: 'do whatever you think best to keep us alive.'. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p88]
- Genes work by controlling protein synthesis, which is powerful but slow (months to build an embryo), while behavior operates on timescales of seconds—hence the need for autonomous brains. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p83]

## Inferences

- Inference: Consciousness may have evolved as the culmination of the trend toward brain autonomy, as the brain's simulation of the world became complete enough to include a model of itself.
- Inference: Survival machines that can simulate the future (rather than learning only through overt trial and error) have an evolutionary advantage because overt error is often fatal.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The precise mechanism by which consciousness arose and its evolutionary significance remains, in Dawkins's words, 'the most profound mystery facing modern biology.'
