# Neuroactive Peptides and Feeding

## Summary

Various neuropeptides modulate feeding behavior, either increasing or decreasing food intake. Key peptides include neuropeptide Y (NPY), which strongly promotes feeding, and cholecystokinin (CCK), bombesin, and GLP-1, which may promote satiety.

## Key facts

- Neuropeptide Y (NPY) can dramatically increase feeding to the point of inducing obesity with repeated injections. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p99]
- Cholecystokinin (CCK) reduces food intake by causing gastrointestinal distress transmitted via the vagus nerve, rather than by producing normal satiety. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p94]
- Bombesin, released from the stomach following eating, may represent a more genuine satiety signal that also increases social play. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p95]
- GLP-1 (glucagon-like peptide-1) may actually produce anxiety rather than satiety in some studies. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p95]
- Galanin can selectively increase fat appetite. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p99]
- Dynorphins can increase feeding behavior. [raw/books/neuroscience/Affective Neuroscience_ The Foundations of Human and Animal - Panksepp, Jaak.pdf#p99]

## Inferences

- Inference: Many purported satiety peptides may produce their effects through malaise, nausea, or anxiety rather than genuine satiety mechanisms.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Whether NPY knockout mice maintain normal appetite suggests redundancy in feeding control systems.
