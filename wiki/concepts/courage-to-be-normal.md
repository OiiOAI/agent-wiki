# The Courage to Be Normal

## Summary

The courage to be normal is presented as an alternative to the pursuit of being 'special.' The philosopher argues that people who cannot accept their normal selves may flip between trying to be 'especially good' (seeking recognition through achievement) or 'especially bad' (engaging in problem behavior). Both extremes aim to become a 'special being' through the pursuit of easy superiority. True courage involves accepting being ordinary without needing to be superior or inferior to others.

## Key facts

- The pursuit of easy superiority refers to trying to attract attention and become 'special' without healthy effort, such as a child disrupting lessons to stand out. [raw/books/psychology/The courage to be disliked_ the Japanese phenomenon that - Ichiro Kishimi, and Fumitake Koga.pdf#p262]
- Children who engage in problem behavior want to be a 'special being,' and the form attention takes (even rebuke) doesn't matter to them. [raw/books/psychology/The courage to be disliked_ the Japanese phenomenon that - Ichiro Kishimi, and Fumitake Koga.pdf#p263]
- Being normal is not being incapable; one does not need to flaunt superiority. [raw/books/psychology/The courage to be disliked_ the Japanese phenomenon that - Ichiro Kishimi, and Fumitake Koga.pdf#p265]

## Inferences

- Inference: The philosopher suggests that the Socratic paradox—'no one desires evil'—applies because even problem children view their disruptive acts as forms of 'good' since they achieve attention.
