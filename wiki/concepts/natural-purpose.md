# Natural Purpose (Kant)

## Summary

Kant's concept of natural purpose refers to organisms as self-organizing beings that are both cause and effect of themselves. A natural purpose is defined as a thing that exists as a natural purpose if it is both cause and effect of itself, where each part reciprocally produces the other parts. Kant argued that organisms cannot be explained mechanistically but must be understood teleologically, yet unlike artifacts, organisms are natural products whose purposiveness is intrinsic rather than designed by an external agent. The theory of autopoiesis provides a naturalized scientific account of precisely this self-producing organization that Kant identified as central to life.

## Key facts

- Kant defined a natural purpose as something whose parts reciprocally produce one another and that exists as both cause and effect of itself. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p134]
- Kant believed organisms are mechanistically inexplicable because their organized forms are contingent with respect to mechanical laws of inorganic nature. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p133]
- Kant stated that 'the organization of nature has nothing analogous to any causality known to us,' suggesting self-organization was beyond scientific comprehension in his time. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p137]
- An autopoietic system satisfies Kant's definition of a natural purpose—something whose parts reciprocally produce one another. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p139]

## Inferences

- Inference: Modern understanding of circular causality and nonlinear dynamics has resolved what Kant saw as the inexplicability of self-organization.
