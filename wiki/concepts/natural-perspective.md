# Natural Perspective

## Summary

Natural perspective, as distinguished from artificial or pictorial perspective, is the study of the ambient optic array as a nested hierarchy of visual solid angles corresponding to parts of the terrestrial environment. It derives from ancient optics (Euclid, Ptolemy) but emphasizes that the environment consists of the earth and sky with surfaces, not isolated objects. The concept involves gradients of size and density of angles, with sizes vanishing and density becoming infinite at the horizon.

## Key facts

- Natural perspective studies the ambient array of solid angles that correspond to distinct geometrical parts of a terrestrial environment, separated by edges and corners. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p94]
- There are gradients of size and density of angles along meridians of the lower hemisphere, with sizes vanishing and density becoming infinite at the horizon. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p94]
- Natural perspective should not be confused with pictorial perspective—the cues for depth in a picture are not the same as information for surface layout in a frozen ambient array. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p95]
- Natural perspective does not apply to shadows with penumbras and patches of light, nor does it apply to moving observers—these restrictions motivated development of the full ecological approach. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p94]

## Inferences

- Inference: The distinction between natural perspective and pictorial perspective reveals that confusing them leads to fundamental misconceptions about visual perception, such as assuming retinal images are 'pictures' to be perceived.
