# Religion as team sport

## Summary

Haidt argues that religion should be understood primarily as a group-level phenomenon—a team sport that binds people together through shared beliefs, rituals, and moral communities—rather than as individual beliefs about supernatural entities as the New Atheists claim.

## Key facts

- The New Atheists (Dawkins, Harris, Hitchens, Dennett) focus on religious beliefs as the essential feature of religion, but Durkheim's sociological approach emphasizes that religions are 'social systems whose participants avow belief in a supernatural agent'—the social system comes first. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p410]
- Religious groups generate moral capital through costly signaling: rituals and prohibitions that would be irrational for individuals but benefit groups by screening for committed members who will not free-ride. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p411]
- Putnam and Campbell found that religious Americans are more likely to give to charity, volunteer, and help strangers, and these correlations hold even when controlling for income, education, and other factors. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p413]
- Religious cooperation evolved because gods who enforced moral norms solved the free-rider problem that would otherwise dissolve groups, according to the 'supernatural punishment' hypothesis. [raw/books/philosophy/The Righteous Mind - Jonathan Haidt.pdf#p412]

## Inferences

- Inference: ligion functions primarily as a group-level adaptation for sustaining cooperation, then attacking religious beliefs may undermine the social systems that generate moral capital, with potential costs for civil society.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The causal direction of the correlation between religiosity and cooperative behavior is debated; Putnam and Campbell's longitudinal data support causation but cannot definitively rule out spurious third variables.
