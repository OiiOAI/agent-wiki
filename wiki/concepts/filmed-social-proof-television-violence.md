# Filmed Social Proof and Television Violence

## Summary

Research examining how filmed depictions of violence influence children's perception of appropriate behavior and increase aggressive actions. Liebert and Baron's (1972) well-controlled experiment found that children shown excerpts of television programs featuring intentional harm were significantly more harmful toward another child than those who watched nonviolent content, with effects observed across both age groups tested and in both genders.

## Key facts

- Liebert and Baron (1972) conducted a well-controlled experiment showing that children who watched television programs featuring intentional harm became significantly more harmful toward other children. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p255]
- Children who watched violent television programs (a program featuring people intentionally harming another) showed more aggression than children who watched a nonviolent program (a horserace). [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p255]
- The finding held true for five-to-six-year-olds and eight-to-nine-year-olds, and for both girls and boys. [raw/books/psychology/Influence_ The Psychology of Persuasion - Robert B. Cialdini.pdf#p255]

## Inferences

- Inference: The research suggests that filmed social proof showing aggressive behavior can teach children that aggression is an appropriate response, making them more likely to behave aggressively themselves.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The text notes that the consequences of televised violence on children's aggressive actions are far from simple, suggesting the relationship may be moderated by additional factors not fully explored in this chunk.
