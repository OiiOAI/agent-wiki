# Naked Mole Rat

## Summary

The naked mole rat (Heterocephalus glaber) is a hairless, nearly blind rodent native to East Africa that exhibits eusociality previously known only in insects. Discovered through studies by Jennifer Jarvis in South Africa and Robert Brett in Kenya, with captive colonies studied by Richard Alexander and Paul Sherman in America, they represent a mammalian analogue to social insects with cooperative colony living, division of labor, and a single breeding queen.

## Key facts

- Naked mole rats are hairless, nearly blind little rodents living in large underground colonies in Kenya, Somalia, and Ethiopia. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p367]
- Colonies typically number 70-80 individuals with burrow networks up to 2-3 miles total length; one colony may excavate 3-4 tons of soil annually with face workers digging at the front. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p367]
- Only one female in the colony breeds over several years, mated by 2-3 males; if she is removed, sterile females compete to replace her. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p367]
- Workers of both sexes engage in tunnel digging, soil transport, and feeding young; size determines role, with largest individuals possibly acting as soldiers against snakes. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p367]
- They feed mainly on large underground tubers; Robert Brett calculated a single mole rat would wear its teeth out searching before finding one, making cooperative colony foraging essential. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p371]
