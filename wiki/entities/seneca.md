# Seneca

## Summary

Roman Stoic philosopher and the wealthiest person in the Roman Empire who developed a practical philosophy that Taleb identifies as pure antifragility. Seneca advocated mentally writing off possessions to eliminate downside while keeping actual wealth for upside, achieving the asymmetry of antifragility. He survived a shipwreck, was tutor to Emperor Nero, and ultimately took his own life with dignity following Stoic principles.

## Key facts

- Seneca was the wealthiest person in the Roman Empire with a fortune of three hundred million denarii, yet wrote extensively about indifference to fate. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p199]
- Seneca's version of Stoicism achieves antifragility: he mentally wrote off possessions so losses wouldn't sting while keeping actual wealth for upside—the 'nihil perditi' (I lost nothing) approach. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p201]
- Seneca said 'The bookkeeping of benefits is simple: it is all expenditure; if any one returns it, that is clear gain; if he does not return it, it is not lost, I gave it for the sake of giving.'. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p205]
- Seneca's practical method: travel with minimal belongings (similar to shipwreck provisions), practice daily assumption that the worst has happened so the rest of the day is a bonus. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p203]
- Seneca's Stoicism domestifies emotions (transforming fear to prudence, pain to information, mistakes to initiation, desire to undertaking) without eliminating them. [raw/books/strategy/Antifragile_ Things That Gain from Disorde - Taleb, Nassim Nicholas.pdf#p204]
