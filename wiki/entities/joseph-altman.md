# Joseph Altman

## Summary

Joseph Altman (born 1933) was an untenured associate professor at MIT who provided the first evidence for adult neurogenesis in 1965 using radioactive tagging of newly synthesized DNA. His findings were initially well-received but within a few years rejected by senior neuroscientists including Pasko Rakic. Altman failed to get tenure at MIT, spent his career at Purdue University, and lost funding for his neurogenesis work. His discovery was eventually vindicated when other researchers using newer techniques confirmed adult neurogenesis in mammals including humans.

## Key facts

- Altman provided the first evidence for adult neurogenesis in 1965 using radioactive tagging of newly synthesized DNA in adult rat brains. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p150]
- Altman failed to get tenure at MIT and spent his career at Purdue University after his neurogenesis findings were rejected. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p150]
- Altman's work was rejected by Pasko Rakic who said new neurons 'don't in New Haven' what Altman found 'in New Mexico'. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p150]
- Altman was eventually completely vindicated when Elizabeth Gould, Fred Gage, and others confirmed adult neurogenesis in mammals using newer techniques. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p151]
