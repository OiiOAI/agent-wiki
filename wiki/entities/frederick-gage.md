# Frederick Gage

## Summary

A scientist at the Salk Laboratories in La Jolla, California, who, with Peter Eriksson, discovered neuronal stem cells in the human hippocampus in 1998. His research team has conducted extensive studies on how to increase neurogenesis through environmental enrichment and physical exercise, demonstrating that new neurons can form throughout life.

## Key facts

- Frederick 'Rusty' Gage works at the Salk Laboratories in La Jolla, California. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p253]
- He and Peter Eriksson discovered neuronal stem cells in the human hippocampus in 1998. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p253]
- His colleague Gerd Kempermann found that mice raised in enriched environments had a 15% increase in hippocampal volume and 40,000 new neurons. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p255]
- His colleague Henriette van Praag showed that running on a wheel doubled the number of new neurons in the hippocampus. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p255]
- Gage's theory is that in natural settings, long-term fast walking would take animals into new environments requiring new learning, sparking 'anticipatory proliferation' of new neurons. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p255]
- He theorizes that novel environments may trigger neurogenesis and that simply walking at a good pace stimulates the growth of new neurons. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p258]

## Inferences

- Inference: Gage's work has fundamentally changed the understanding of brain aging, showing that the brain retains the capacity to generate new neurons throughout life and that this capacity can be influenced by lifestyle factors.
