# Maurice Merleau-Ponty

## Summary

French phenomenological philosopher (1908–1961) whose work on perception and embodiment is central to McGilchrist's argument. Merleau-Ponty emphasized that vision involves mutual transformation between seer and seen, not passive reception. His concept of 'the flesh' (la chair) as neither wholly opaque nor transparent explains how embodied beings communicate through sight. His work 'The Visible and the Invisible' explores how the body enables both alienation (if viewed as wholly opaque) and intersubjectivity (if seen through). McGilchrist uses Merleau-Ponty extensively to argue for the reciprocal, embodied nature of perception that the right hemisphere captures but the left hemisphere's detached vision misses.

## Key facts

- Merleau-Ponty wrote that the eye is 'a certain power of making contact with things, and not a screen on which they are projected'. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p239]
- When two people withdraw into pure thinking and make themselves into an 'inhuman gaze', the objectification of each by the other's gaze is felt as unbearable only because it takes the place of possible communication. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p239]
- Merleau-Ponty refers to the moment when vision ceases to be solipsistic and 'the other turns back upon me the luminous rays in which I had caught him'. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p239]
- In Merleau-Ponty's concept, the 'thickness' (l'épaisseur) of flesh, far from being an obstacle, enables awareness of the other and of ourselves as embodied beings. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p239]
- Merleau-Ponty said we do not see paintings as much as see according to them. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p261]
