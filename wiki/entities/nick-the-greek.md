# Nick the Greek

## Summary

A poker player at the Crystal Lounge in Billings, Montana who held entrenched, irrational beliefs about poker strategy, specifically that playing the mathematically weakest starting hand (7-2 offsuit) was superior to playing the strongest hand (aces) because of an 'element of surprise.' Despite years of experience and consistent losses, he never changed his strategy, exemplifying the failure to learn from experience due to self-serving bias.

## Key facts

- Nick the Greek was the general manager of a hotel across the street from the Crystal Lounge in Billings, Montana, having been transferred from Greece by the hotel chain. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking_in_Bets.epub#L792-L800]
- He believed aces were the worst starting hand because opponents would expect them, and 7-2 offsuit was the best hand because it was unpredictable. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking_in_Bets.epub#L808-L815]
- Nick the Greek frequently threw away pairs of aces at the first sign of a bet. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking_in_Bets.epub#L817-L819]
- He rarely came out ahead but never changed his strategy, complaining about bad luck when he lost. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking_in_Bets.epub#L820-L823]
- He was eventually deported to Greece, with speculation that he went broke, dipped into the hotel till, or lost his work visa playing poker on company time. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking_in_Bets.epub#L827-L837]
- He lost money because he ignored feedback that his strategy was losing and didn't recognize learning opportunities. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking_in_Bets.epub#L837-L841]

## Inferences

- Inference: Nick the Greek's case represents an extreme example of self-serving bias: when he won with 7-2, he credited his 'brilliant strategy'; when he lost (more frequently), he blamed bad luck. This prevented him from ever questioning his beliefs.
