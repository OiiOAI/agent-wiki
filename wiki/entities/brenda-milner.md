# Brenda Milner

## Summary

Brenda Milner is a neuropsychologist who conducted pioneering studies on patient HM, demonstrating that motor skill learning could occur independently of explicit memory. Her work established the functional and anatomical separation of implicit and explicit memory systems.

## Key facts

- Milner's studies beginning in the 1950s with amnesic patients (most famously HM) showed that motor skills could be learned and retained without conscious recollection. [raw/books/neuroscience/I of the Vortex _ From Neurons to Self - Rodolfo Riascos Llinás.pdf#p190]
- Milner coined the term 'perceptual learning' (later called priming) based on her findings with amnesic patients identifying fragmented drawings. [raw/books/neuroscience/I of the Vortex _ From Neurons to Self - Rodolfo Riascos Llinás.pdf#p191]
- Her work demonstrated that implicit and explicit memory are functionally separate and separable from one another in more than a semantical sense. [raw/books/neuroscience/I of the Vortex _ From Neurons to Self - Rodolfo Riascos Llinás.pdf#p190]
- Milner and William Scoville reported in 1957 that memory could be traced to specific regions of the brain, particularly the medial temporal lobe and hippocampus. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p129]
- Milner found that H.M. retained perfectly good short-term memory, long-term memory for events before his surgery, but could not convert new short-term memories into permanent long-term memories. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p140]
- Milner showed that H.M. could learn to trace a star in a mirror and improve over days, despite having no conscious memory of performing the task—this demonstrated implicit memory storage. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p142]
- Milner identified the roles of the hippocampus and medial temporal lobe in explicit memory and provided the first evidence of implicit memory storage. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p143]
- Brenda Milner was born in 1918 in Manchester, England. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p41]
- She studied experimental psychology at the University of Cambridge under Sir Frederic Bartlett. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p41]
- She moved to Montreal in 1944 and became Hebb's graduate student at McGill University. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p41]
- Milner was the first psychologist to test Henry after his operation, beginning in 1955. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p46]
- Her 1957 paper with Scoville, 'Loss of Recent Memory after Bilateral Hippocampal Lesions,' became a landmark in neuroscience. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p46]
- She first examined Henry in April 1955, twenty months after his operation. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p46]
- Milner tested Henry's ability to remember three numbers (5-8-4) after 20 minutes using mental rehearsal techniques. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p62]
- In 1955, Brenda Milner conducted the first formal testing of Henry, evaluating his ability to form word associations and discovering that his IQ was above average. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p77]
- Milner's landmark discovery showed that some of Henry's long-term memory processes were disrupted while others were not, leading to the theoretical distinction between declarative and nondeclarative memory. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p117]
- In 1955, Milner demonstrated that Henry could improve on the mirror-tracing task over three days of practice despite having no conscious memory of previous sessions—the first formal evidence of preserved nondeclarative learning in amnesia. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p153]
- Milner's 1957 paper with William Scoville detailing Henry's operation and psychological test results launched the modern era of memory research. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p130]
- Brenda Milner conducted psychological examinations of Henry beginning in 1955 at the Montreal Neurological Institute. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p350]
- Milner's research with Henry demonstrated that he could learn new motor skills through mirror tracing despite his profound amnesia, revealing preserved nondeclarative memory. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p350]

## Inferences

- Inference: Milner's work disproved Lashley's theory of mass action, showing that memory is not distributed equally across the cortex but localized to specific structures.
- Inference: Milner's collaboration with Scoville and Penfield established the template for neuropsychological research linking brain lesions to specific cognitive deficits.
- Inference: Milner's work established the foundation for understanding the fractionation of memory systems in the human brain.
