# James J. Gibson

## Summary

James J. Gibson (1904–1979) was one of the most important psychologists of the 20th century, best known for his work on visual perception. He received his Ph.D. from Princeton University and developed the ecological approach to visual perception, advocating direct perception and direct realism as opposed to cognitivist indirect realism. His approach, termed 'ecological psychology,' is increasingly influential in contemporary post-cognitivist movements in psychology.

- His 1950 book 'The Perception of the Visual World' introduced the concept of texture gradients as specifying surface slant. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p19]
- Gibson's World War II research on motion picture testing for the Army Air Corps influenced his later work on the role of the ground in visual perception. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p19]

- He introduced the concept of the ambient optic array as the structured light surrounding a point of observation, contrasting with physical optics. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p89]
- Gibson proposed that vision involves registering invariant structure in the ambient array, not forming and transmitting retinal images to the brain. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p86]
- He formulated the principle of reversible occlusion to explain how hidden surfaces are perceived without direct observation. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception_ Classic - James J_ Gibson.pdf#p100]

## Inferences

- Inference: Gibson's shift from retinal-image-based explanation (1950) to ambient-optic-array explanation (1979) represents a fundamental paradigm shift in how visual perception is conceptualized.
- Inference: The ecological approach's focus on information pickup rather than stimulus-response marks a departure from both behaviorism and mentalism.
- Inference: Gibson's approach fundamentally reconceptualized the relationship between observer and environment, rejecting the Cartesian dualism implied by traditional theories of sensation and perception.
