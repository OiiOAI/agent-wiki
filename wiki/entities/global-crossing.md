# Global Crossing

## Summary

Global Crossing was a telecommunications company founded in 1997 by Gary Winnick that built the AC-1 transatlantic fiber optic cable. The company went bankrupt in December 2001 after its stock was valued at $38 billion. The case illustrates terrible industry structure where products are commodities, technology is non-proprietary, and overcapacity drives prices to near-zero cost.

## Key facts

- Global Crossing was founded in 1997 by Gary Winnick and two former AT&T managers (William Carter and Wallace Dawson). [raw/books/strategy/Good strategy, bad strategy _ the difference and why it - Richard P_ Rumelt.pdf#p98]
- Pacific Capital Group provided $75 million in equity and $660 million in debt to finance AC-1 construction. [raw/books/strategy/Good strategy, bad strategy _ the difference and why it - Richard P_ Rumelt.pdf#p98]
- AC-1 was an 8,886-mile optical fiber loop connecting the U.S. to the UK and Germany with initial capacity of 256 STM-1, costing $750 million to build. [raw/books/strategy/Good strategy, bad strategy _ the difference and why it - Richard P_ Rumelt.pdf#p98]
- By end of 1998, 35 percent of AC-1 capacity was sold for $950 million, more than paying back construction costs. [raw/books/strategy/Good strategy, bad strategy _ the difference and why it - Richard P_ Rumelt.pdf#p98]
- Six months after going public, Global Crossing was valued at $38 billion, more than Ford Motor Company. [raw/books/strategy/Good strategy, bad strategy _ the difference and why it - Richard P_ Rumelt.pdf#p98]
- By early 2002, one transatlantic STM-1 could be bought for $325,000, only 4 percent of Global Crossing's original asking price of $8 million. [raw/books/strategy/Good strategy, bad strategy _ the difference and why it - Richard P_ Rumelt.pdf#p99]
- Global Crossing declared bankruptcy on December 2001. [raw/books/strategy/Good strategy, bad strategy _ the difference and why it - Richard P_ Rumelt.pdf#p99]

## Inferences

- Inference: The Global Crossing case demonstrates how Five Forces analysis (commodity product, no differentiation, non-proprietary technology, price competition, sunk capital costs) would have predicted the outcome.
