# Francis Crick

## Summary

Francis Crick (1916–2004) was perhaps the most creative and influential biologist of the second half of the twentieth century. After solving the mystery of DNA structure with James Watson in 1953 and helping crack the genetic code, he turned at age 60 to studying consciousness. Working with Christof Koch, Crick brought consciousness into the scientific mainstream. His final hypothesis, developed until days before his death, proposed that the claustrum acts as the 'conductor' coordinating consciousness. Kandel describes Crick's contributions as placing him in a class with Copernicus, Newton, Darwin, and Einstein.

## Key facts

- In 1953, after just two years of collaboration, Crick and Watson solved the mystery of DNA structure, famously announcing at the Eagle Pub they had found 'the secret of life'. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p387]
- Crick turned to consciousness research in 1976 at age 60, studying it for the rest of his life in partnership with Christof Koch. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p387]
- Crick made consciousness a focus of the scientific community, which had previously ignored it, but despite almost thirty years of effort, was able to budge the problem only a modest distance. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p387]
- Crick died on July 28, 2004, still correcting his last paper on the claustrum on his way to the hospital. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p392]
- In Crick and Koch's last paper, they focused on the claustrum as the site that mediates unity of experience, comparing it to the conductor of an orchestra. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p392]
