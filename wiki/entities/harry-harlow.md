# Harry Harlow

## Summary

Harry Harlow (1905-1981) was an American psychologist at the University of Wisconsin who conducted groundbreaking research on attachment and love in infant rhesus monkeys. His surrogate mother experiments demonstrated that infant monkeys preferred contact comfort (terry-cloth covered surrogate) over food delivery (chicken-wire surrogate), disproving behaviorist and Freudian theories that attachment was solely about reinforcement. He also conducted controversial isolation studies showing that monkeys raised without mothers or peers developed severe behavioral abnormalities including autistic-like self-clutching and inappropriate social and sexual behaviors.

## Key facts

- Harlow's surrogate mother experiments showed infant monkeys preferred terry-cloth covered surrogate over chicken-wire surrogate even when only the latter provided food. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p196]
- Infant monkeys raised in isolation without mother or peers developed 'autistic' behaviors including solitary self-clutching and rocking, plus inappropriate social and sexual behaviors. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p197]
- Isolated monkeys performed normal behaviors but at wrong times and places, showing that mothers teach contextual appropriateness rather than motor patterns themselves. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p197]
- Harlow demonstrated that contact comfort was more important than food in infant attachment: 'Man cannot live by milk alone. Love is an emotion that does not need to be bottle- or spoon-fed'. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p196]

## Inferences

- Inference: Harlow's finding that isolated monkeys performed correct behaviors in incorrect contexts suggests that the primary function of social experience is teaching situational appropriateness—what behaviors are acceptable in which social contexts
