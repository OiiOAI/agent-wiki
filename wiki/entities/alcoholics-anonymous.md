# Alcoholics Anonymous

## Summary

Alcoholics Anonymous is the largest program for improving self-control regarding alcohol, attracting more problem drinkers than all professional programs combined. Research shows it is at least as effective as professional treatments. Its mechanisms include goal-setting (one day at a time), monitoring (chips and speaking), and social support from people with similar struggles.

## Key facts

- AA members receive chips for consecutive days of sobriety and typically begin speeches by stating how many days they have been sober, providing public monitoring. [raw/books/psychology/Willpower _ Rediscovering the Greatest Human Strength - Roy F_ Baumeister, John Tierney.pdf#p159]
- A study of over 2,000 men led by John McKellar found that attendance at AA meetings led to fewer future drinking problems, with benefits remaining after accounting for initial motivation and psychological problems. [raw/books/psychology/Willpower _ Rediscovering the Greatest Human Strength - Roy F_ Baumeister, John Tierney.pdf#p159]
- Project MATCH found that AA, cognitive-behavioral therapy, and motivational-enhancement therapy were all about equally effective for alcoholism treatment. [raw/books/psychology/Willpower _ Rediscovering the Greatest Human Strength - Roy F_ Baumeister, John Tierney.pdf#p159]
- Eric Clapton reported that kneeling and praying works for him, saying 'Because it works, as simple as that,' despite being an atheist who couldn't identify who he was praying to. [raw/books/psychology/Willpower _ Rediscovering the Greatest Human Strength - Roy F_ Baumeister, John Tierney.pdf#p156]

## Inferences

- Inference: The 'higher power' in AA may be experienced as the social support from other members, making 'the people at those meetings... the higher power.'

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The exact mechanism by which AA produces its effects remains debated, with 'warehousing' (simply spending time not drinking) possibly contributing to the benefits.
