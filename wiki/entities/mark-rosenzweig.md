# Mark Rosenzweig

## Summary

Mark Rosenzweig was a researcher at the University of California at Berkeley who demonstrated neuroplasticity by showing that rats raised in stimulating environments had heavier brains with more neurotransmitters and better blood supply than rats from less stimulating environments.

## Key facts

- Mark Rosenzweig was at the University of California at Berkeley. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p44]
- He studied rats in stimulating and nonstimulating environments. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p44]
- In postmortem exams he found that the brains of stimulated rats had more neurotransmitters, were heavier, and had better blood supply than those from less stimulating environments. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p44]
- He was one of the first scientists to demonstrate neuroplasticity by showing that activity could produce changes in the structure of the brain. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p44]
- His paper inspired Barbara Arrowsmith Young to believe that compensation might not be the only answer to learning disabilities. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p45]
- Over the years his labs and others showed that stimulating the brain makes it grow in almost every conceivable way. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p52]
- Acetylcholine, a brain chemical essential for learning, is higher in rats trained on difficult spatial problems than in rats trained on simpler problems. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p52]
- Mental training or life in enriched environments increases brain weight by 5 percent in the cerebral cortex of animals and up to 9 percent in areas that the training directly stimulates. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p52]
- Trained or stimulated neurons develop 25 percent more branches and increase their size, the number of connections per neuron, and their blood supply. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p52]
- These changes can occur late in life, though they do not develop as rapidly in older animals as in younger ones. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p52]
