# Michael Eisner

## Summary

Michael Eisner (b. 1942) is an American entertainment executive whose career illustrates the Law of Grandiosity. Rising from ABC programming to become president of Paramount, he developed a 'golden touch' formula for profitable films and was celebrated as a genius. After transforming Disney from 1984, his grandiosity led to disastrous decisions: Euro Disney's cultural miscalculation, the firing of successful executive Jeffrey Katzenberg, the ill-conceived acquisition of ABC, and the disastrous hiring and humiliation of Michael Ovitz. His case demonstrates how success inflated his self-opinion, causing him to forget luck's role, discount others' contributions, and make increasingly irrational decisions until his board removed him as chairman in 2004.

## Key facts

- Eisner developed a formula at Paramount emphasizing strong concepts over expensive productions: 'a film had to begin with a great concept—one that was original, easy to summarize, and dramatic.'. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p359]
- Eisner chose Paris over Barcelona for Euro Disneyland because 'this was going to be more than a theme park. This was going to be a cultural statement.'. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p361]
- Eisner fired Jeffrey Katzenberg in August 1994, despite Katzenberg having overseen The Lion King, one of the most profitable films in Hollywood history. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p364]
- Michael Ovitz lasted only fourteen months at Disney under Eisner's leadership, being systematically isolated and humiliated before being fired. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p366]
- In March 2004, shareholders voted a stinging rebuke of Eisner's leadership, and he was stripped of his chairman position before resigning as CEO in September 2005. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p367]

## Inferences

- Inference: Eisner's pattern of inflating his self-opinion after success, then making increasingly irrational decisions, exemplifies how grandiosity operates: success confirms superiority, which leads to overconfidence, which causes detachment from reality.
- Inference: The case demonstrates how grandiosity is self-reinforcing: to admit earlier success was partly luck would undermine the current self-image, so failures must be blamed on others.
