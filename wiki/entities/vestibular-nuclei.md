# Vestibular Nuclei

## Summary

The vestibular nuclei are four brainstem nuclei (medial, lateral, superior, and descending) that receive input from vestibular receptors via the vestibular nerve and integrate it with visual, proprioceptive, and motor signals. They project to the cerebellum, oculomotor nuclei, spinal cord, and thalamus.

## Key facts

- The four vestibular nuclei are medial, lateral, superior, and descending, located in the dorsal pons and medulla. [raw/books/neuroscience/Eric Kandel PRINCIPLES OF NEURAL SCIENCE Sixth Edition - Eric R Kandel; John Koester; Sarah Mack; Steven Siegelbaum.pdf#p683]
- The medial vestibular nucleus receives fibers predominantly from semicircular canals and projects to the cerebellum, reticular formation, and spinal cord. [raw/books/neuroscience/Eric Kandel PRINCIPLES OF NEURAL SCIENCE Sixth Edition - Eric R Kandel; John Koester; Sarah Mack; Steven Siegelbaum.pdf#p684]
- The lateral vestibular nucleus (Deiters' nucleus) projects through the lateral vestibulospinal tract to control postural reflexes through limb and axial musculature. [raw/books/neuroscience/Eric Kandel PRINCIPLES OF NEURAL SCIENCE Sixth Edition - Eric R Kandel; John Koester; Sarah Mack; Steven Siegelbaum.pdf#p684]
- Vestibular nuclei receive convergent motion information from the opposite ear through an inhibitory commissural pathway using GABA. [raw/books/neuroscience/Eric Kandel PRINCIPLES OF NEURAL SCIENCE Sixth Edition - Eric R Kandel; John Koester; Sarah Mack; Steven Siegelbaum.pdf#p684]
- The commissural system creates a push-pull comparator that increases response sensitivity and decreases noise from afferent signals. [raw/books/neuroscience/Eric Kandel PRINCIPLES OF NEURAL SCIENCE Sixth Edition - Eric R Kandel; John Koester; Sarah Mack; Steven Siegelbaum.pdf#p686]

## Inferences

- Inference: The reciprocal connections between vestibular nuclei and cerebellum suggest these circuits form regulatory mechanisms for eye movements, head movements, and posture.
- Inference: The loss of unilateral vestibular function leads to vertigo because the commissural system interprets the imbalance as head rotation.
