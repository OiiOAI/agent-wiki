# Hans Jonas

## Summary

Hans Jonas (1903-1979) was a German-American philosopher whose work on the philosophy of the organism profoundly influenced the 'deep continuity' approach to life and mind. In 'The Phenomenon of Life' (1966), Jonas argued that the philosophy of life comprises both the philosophy of the organism and the philosophy of mind, and that the organic prefigures mind while mind remains part of the organic. Jonas introduced concepts such as 'needful freedom' (the dialectical relation of organism to matter), 'conatus' (Spinoza's term for life's effort to preserve itself), and 'self-transcendence' (life's projection beyond its present condition). His work provides a phenomenological complement to the biological theory of autopoiesis.

## Key facts

- Jonas wrote that 'a philosophy of life comprises the philosophy of the organism and the philosophy of mind,' arguing that the organic prefigures mind. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p129]
- Jonas introduced the concept of 'needful freedom' to describe the organism's dialectical relation to matter—independent of material sameness yet dependent on material availability. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p150]
- Jonas argued that metabolism is the first form of freedom—life's independence of form with respect to its matter, achieved through constant material renewal. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p151]
- Jonas defined the 'self-transcendence' of life as 'entertaining a horizon beyond its point-identity,' whereby identity can only be achieved through moving beyond the given condition. [raw/books/neuroscience/Mind in Life _ Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of - Evan Thompson.pdf#p154]
