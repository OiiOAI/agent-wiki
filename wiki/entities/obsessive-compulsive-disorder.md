# Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (OCD)

## Summary

Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (OCD) is a psychiatric condition characterized by persistent, intrusive thoughts (obsessions) and repetitive behaviors (compulsions) that individuals feel driven to perform. The disorder involves hyperactivity in three brain regions: the orbital frontal cortex, cingulate gyrus, and caudate nucleus, resulting in 'brain lock' where the normal ability to 'turn the page' and move past mistakes is impaired. OCD often worsens over time, gradually altering brain structure, and is distinguished from normal worry by its intensity, duration, and interference with daily life.

## Key facts

- OCD is characterized by persistent obsessions (intrusive, unwanted thoughts) and compulsions (repetitive behaviors performed to reduce anxiety). [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p172]
- People with OCD are terrified that some harm will come, or has come, to them or to those they love. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p171]
- OCD often worsens over time, gradually altering the structure of the brain. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p171]
- In OCD, worry begets worry—the more a person thinks about his fear, the more he worries about it. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p171]
- Typical obsessions include fears of contracting a terminal illness, being contaminated by germs, being poisoned by chemicals, being threatened by electromagnetic radiation, or being betrayed by one's own genes. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p172]
- Common compulsions include washing rituals, checking behaviors (sometimes a hundred or more times), ordering and symmetry behaviors, and counting rituals. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p174]
- Brain scans show that in OCD, the orbital frontal cortex, cingulate gyrus, and caudate nucleus are all hyperactive and locked in the 'on position.'. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p176]
- The 'automatic gearshift' in the caudate nucleus does not work properly in OCD, preventing the person from moving on to the next thought. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p175]
- OCD causes include genetic factors (it often runs in families) and infections that swell the caudate. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p177]
- When a person with OCD tries to resist a compulsion, tension mounts to a fever pitch; if he acts on it, he gets temporary relief but makes the obsessive thought worse when it strikes again. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p175]
- Standard treatments like exposure and response prevention help only about half of OCD patients make some improvement, with most not getting completely better. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p178]

## Inferences

- Inference: The description suggests that OCD involves a malfunction in the brain's error-detection and anxiety-response systems, where normal neural 'gear-shifting' mechanisms fail to disengage once a perceived threat has been addressed.
