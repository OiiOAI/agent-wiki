# Werner Heisenberg

## Summary

Werner Heisenberg (1901–1976) was a German physicist who in 1927 uncovered the Uncertainty (or Indeterminacy) Principle, showing that one cannot simultaneously determine precisely both the velocity and position of a particle. His principle is cited to show that when observer and observed have similar precision, uncertainty values become large, representing the degree of intrusion by the observer upon the observed.

## Key facts

- Heisenberg's Indeterminacy Principle showed that one cannot simultaneously fix or determine precisely the velocity and position of a particle or body. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p449]
- Due to the presence and influence of an observer, the product of velocity uncertainty and position uncertainty is equal to or greater than Planck's constant divided by the mass of the particle being investigated (V × Q ≥ h/m). [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p449]
- When the precision or mass of phenomena being observed is little different than the precision or mass of the observing phenomena, uncertainty values become as large as or larger than the velocity and size frame-of-reference of bodies being observed. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p450]
- The magnitude of uncertainty values represents the degree of intrusion by the observer upon the observed, and consequently the degree of confusion and disorder perceived by that observer. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p451]
- When the intended distinction between observer and observed essentially disappears, uncertainty values indicate erratic behavior. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p450]
