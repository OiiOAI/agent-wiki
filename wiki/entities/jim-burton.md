# Jim Burton

## Summary

Lieutenant Colonel Jim Burton was John Boyd's deputy who arrived in June 1975 as a 'Chosen One'—a graduate of the first Air Force Academy class who was on track to become a general. Initially skeptical of Boyd, Burton underwent a gradual conversion to become one of Boyd's most dedicated Acolytes, eventually proving that 'one man can make a difference.' He was passed over for promotion twice before making colonel, ultimately choosing principle over career advancement.

## Key facts

- Burton was captain of the baseball team at the Air Force Academy and during his junior year was third in the nation in the college batting championship. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p294]
- Spinney warned Boyd that Burton was 'big trouble,' calling him standoffish, too serious, too remote, and too much a careerist. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p294]
- Boyd saw in Burton 'an unbending spirit, an uncompromising heart, and a backbone forged of carbon steel' with 'the persistence of a rutting moose.'. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p294]
- Burton was devastated when passed over for colonel, but Boyd gave him the 'To Be or to Do' speech, after which Burton decided he wanted to 'shake up the system' rather than be part of it. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p334]
- Burton was fired from his Pentagon job after refusing to change a chart showing the F-16 had better turning performance than the F-15. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p333]
- Burton made colonel on his third and last attempt and knew he would never make general in the Air Force. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p392]
- Burton served as military assistant to three consecutive assistant secretaries of the Air Force in both the Carter and Reagan administrations, a remarkable feat given the sensitive nature of the position. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p393]
- Unlike typical military assistants who spy on civilians for their generals, Burton informed his civilian bosses when the Air Force was trying to deceive them. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p393]
- Eighteen-star generals marched into an assistant secretary's office to oppose Burton's assignment to the OSD testing office, but the assistant secretary prevailed by invoking civilian control of the military. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p394]
- Burton arrived at the OSD testing office in June 1982 and was besieged by Pierre Sprey to conduct tests showing how vulnerable American vehicles were to Soviet weapons. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p395]
- The Army fired Rumrian-made rockets (with much smaller warheads than Soviet weapons) at the Bradley and filled fuel tanks with water to guarantee no explosion would result. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p396]
- Boyd gave Burton three guiding principles: never be wrong and do your homework; criticize the testing process rather than the Bradley itself; stay inside the system and don't go to the media or Congress. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p397]
- In June 1984 Burton wrote the 'Rubicon Memo' to Secretary of Defense Weinberger accusing the Army of not performing realistic tests on the Bradley. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p399]
- The Air Force tried to transfer Burton to Alaska with seven days' notice to remove him from the Bradley controversy, but he flooded the Building with copies of documents and the plan was reversed. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p399]
- Major newspapers ran stories that Burton had accused the Army of rigging Bradley tests and that the Pentagon had retaliated by abolishing his job, supporting Burton's position. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p400]
- Burton became known for saying 'I want you to know there is nothing personal in what I am about to do' before revealing damaging evidence to Army experts. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p402]
- Weinberger sent Burton a handwritten note in September 1985 asking to be personally informed of all Bradley test results. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p402]
- Nancy Kassebaum nominated Burton for the Director of Operational Test and Evaluation position, but Weinberger refused to accept him and the Air Force again tried to transfer him. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p405]
- Burton discovered the Army had replaced internal ammunition boxes with cans of water to give false test results and wrote his most famous memo accusing the Army of cheating. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p406]
- Congress mandated that all weapons systems be tested in the realistic fashion Burton had demanded, and the addition of Kevlar lining to the Bradley's troop compartment likely saved many lives in the Gulf War. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p436]
- Burton wrote 'The Pentagon Wars' which was adapted into an HBO movie starring Kelsey Grammer in 1998. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p436]
- Burton was elected Loudoun County supervisor in Virginia, defeating a major developer by applying the same principles he learned from Boyd in the Pentagon. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p436]

## Inferences

- Inference: Burton's conversion illustrates Boyd's ability to identify individuals with 'unbending spirit' who could be redirected from careerism toward principled action.
- Inference: Burton kept almost every version of 'Patterns of Conflict,' creating a stack of papers about two feet tall, making him potentially the only complete archive of the briefing's evolution.
- Inference: Burton's willingness to take on the entire U.S. Army demonstrated Boyd's teaching that one person with the right ideas and moral conviction could defeat much larger, more powerful institutions through maneuver tactics.
- Inference: The Bradley controversy prefigured the broader military reform movement's success in changing how weapons were tested.
