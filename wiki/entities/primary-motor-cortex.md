# Primary Motor Cortex (M1)

## Summary

The primary motor cortex (M1) plays an important role in generating motor commands that control spinal motor neuron activity. It contains a detailed topographic map of the contralateral body, with disproportionately large representations for the fingers, face, and mouth. M1 houses corticomotoneuronal cells that project monosynaptically to spinal motor neurons, particularly abundant for hand and finger control in primates. Neural activity in M1 reflects both spatial and temporal features of motor output, including causal forces and muscle activity, and population coding enables prediction of movement trajectories.

## Key facts

- The primary motor cortex (M1) plays an important role in generating motor commands that control spinal motor neuron activity, including essential information necessary to select and control the timing and magnitude of muscle activity. [raw/books/neuroscience/Eric Kandel PRINCIPLES OF NEURAL SCIENCE Sixth Edition - Eric R Kandel; John Koester; Sarah Mack; Steven Siegelbaum.pdf#p841]
- The motor map in M1 is not an exact point-to-point reproduction of the body's anatomical form; the most finely controlled body parts—fingers, face, and mouth—are represented by disproportionately large areas. [raw/books/neuroscience/Eric Kandel PRINCIPLES OF NEURAL SCIENCE Sixth Edition - Eric R Kandel; John Koester; Sarah Mack; Steven Siegelbaum.pdf#p841]
- Neurons controlling muscles of the digits, hand, and distal arm tend to be concentrated within a central zone, whereas those controlling more proximal arm muscles are located in a horseshoe-shaped ring around the central core. [raw/books/neuroscience/Eric Kandel PRINCIPLES OF NEURAL SCIENCE Sixth Edition - Eric R Kandel; John Koester; Sarah Mack; Steven Siegelbaum.pdf#p842]
- Corticomotoneuronal (CM) cells are found almost exclusively within the most caudal part of M1 that lies within the anterior bank of the central sulcus. [raw/books/neuroscience/Eric Kandel PRINCIPLES OF NEURAL SCIENCE Sixth Edition - Eric R Kandel; John Koester; Sarah Mack; Steven Siegelbaum.pdf#p843]
- CM cells are very rare or absent in nonprimate species and become progressively more prominent from prosimians to monkeys, great apes, and humans. [raw/books/neuroscience/Eric Kandel PRINCIPLES OF NEURAL SCIENCE Sixth Edition - Eric R Kandel; John Koester; Sarah Mack; Steven Siegelbaum.pdf#p843]
- A single CM cell axon often branches and terminates on spinal motor neurons for several different agonist muscles and can also influence antagonist muscles through spinal interneurons. [raw/books/neuroscience/Eric Kandel PRINCIPLES OF NEURAL SCIENCE Sixth Edition - Eric R Kandel; John Koester; Sarah Mack; Steven Siegelbaum.pdf#p843]
- The activity of M1 neurons reflects both causal movement kinetics (forces and muscle activity) and spatial movement kinematics (direction and trajectory) during reaching. [raw/books/neuroscience/Eric Kandel PRINCIPLES OF NEURAL SCIENCE Sixth Edition - Eric R Kandel; John Koester; Sarah Mack; Steven Siegelbaum.pdf#p850]
- Population vectors extracted from pooled M1 neuron activity during reaching movements predict arm trajectories 100 to 150 milliseconds into the future. [raw/books/neuroscience/Eric Kandel PRINCIPLES OF NEURAL SCIENCE Sixth Edition - Eric R Kandel; John Koester; Sarah Mack; Steven Siegelbaum.pdf#p850]
- Lesions of M1 in humans have a more profound effect on voluntary motor control compared to other mammals, likely due to the increased importance of direct CM projections to spinal motor neurons in humans. [raw/books/neuroscience/Eric Kandel PRINCIPLES OF NEURAL SCIENCE Sixth Edition - Eric R Kandel; John Koester; Sarah Mack; Steven Siegelbaum.pdf#p844]
- Motor adaptation to visual or mechanical disturbances leads to changes in M1 neuron activity, with large increases arising in cells whose preferred directional tuning is opposite to the applied force field. [raw/books/neuroscience/Eric Kandel PRINCIPLES OF NEURAL SCIENCE Sixth Edition - Eric R Kandel; John Koester; Sarah Mack; Steven Siegelbaum.pdf#p855]
- Error signals in M1 drive adaptation in reaching; M1 activity during postmovement visual feedback reflects the direction of reach endpoint errors. [raw/books/neuroscience/Eric Kandel PRINCIPLES OF NEURAL SCIENCE Sixth Edition - Eric R Kandel; John Koester; Sarah Mack; Steven Siegelbaum.pdf#p856]
