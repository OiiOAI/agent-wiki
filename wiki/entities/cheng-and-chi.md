# Cheng and Ch'i

## Summary

Cheng and Ch'i are complementary concepts from Sun Tzu's 'The Art of War' that Boyd identified as the essence of successful warfare. Cheng represents orthodox, conventional approaches that hold attention and fix the enemy's focus, while Ch'i represents unorthodox, unexpected approaches that exploit the opening created by cheng. General Patton exemplified this as 'holding them by the nose and kicking them in the ass.'

## Key facts

- Boyd called Sun Tzu's 'The Art of War' his 'Rosetta Stone,' the only theoretical book on war he did not find fundamentally flawed. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p325]
- Boyd eventually owned seven translations of The Art of War, each with long passages underlined and with copious marginalia. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p325]
- The cheng/ch'i concept means a commander should use two thrusts, either of which could attain the objective, with cheng fixing attention and ch'i delivering the decisive blow. [raw/books/strategy/Boyd - Robert Coram.pdf#p325]

## Inferences

- Inference: Boyd's emphasis on cheng and ch'i reflected his broader theory that effective action requires both maintaining predictable patterns to create expectations and shattering those patterns with unexpected moves to cause disorientation.
