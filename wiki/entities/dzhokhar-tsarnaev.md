# Dzhokhar Tsarnaev

## Summary

Dzhokhar Tsarnaev was convicted in 2015 for the Boston Marathon bombing and sentenced to death. His case illustrates how affective realism and cultural stereotypes affect jury perceptions of remorse. Jurors perceived Tsarnaev as remorseless because he sat 'stone-faced' throughout most of the trial—consistent with Chechen cultural norms for male stoicism. Meanwhile, a letter of apology Tsarnaev wrote two years earlier was sealed and excluded from evidence.

## Key facts

- Only two of the jurors believed Tsarnaev had felt remorse; the other 10 thought he had no regrets, forming opinions from observing him sit 'stone-faced.'. [raw/books/psychology/How Emotions Are Made_ The Secret Life of - Lisa Feldman Barrett.pdf#p253]
- Chechen culture expects men to be stoic in the face of adversity; if Tsarnaev felt remorse, he might well have remained stony-faced per Chechen 'wolf' norms. [raw/books/psychology/How Emotions Are Made_ The Secret Life of - Lisa Feldman Barrett.pdf#p254]
- Tsarnaev became tearful when his aunt took the stand to plead for his life—consistent with Chechen culture of honor where shaming family is painful. [raw/books/psychology/How Emotions Are Made_ The Secret Life of - Lisa Feldman Barrett.pdf#p254]
- Tsarnaev did convey remorse in a 2013 letter of apology, but it was sealed under U.S. Government Special Administrative Measures citing 'international security' and excluded from trial evidence. [raw/books/psychology/How Emotions Are Made_ The Secret Life of - Lisa Feldman Barrett.pdf#p254]
- At his 2015 sentencing, Tsarnaev stated: 'I am sorry for the lives that I've taken, for the suffering that I've caused you, for the damage that I've done. Irreparable damage.'. [raw/books/psychology/How Emotions Are Made_ The Secret Life of - Lisa Feldman Barrett.pdf#p255]
