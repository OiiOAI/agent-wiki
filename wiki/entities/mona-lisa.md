# Mona Lisa

## Summary

The Mona Lisa (c. 1503–1507, Louvre) is a portrait by Leonardo da Vinci of Lisa Gherardini, wife of Francesco del Giocondo. Freud analyzes this painting in connection with his theory of Leonardo's childhood, arguing that the painting's famous enigmatic smile awakened Leonardo's memory of his mother Caterina's smile, and that the smile subsequently appears in all his later works, becoming a 'Leonardesque' trademark.

## Key facts

- Leonardo spent four years painting the Mona Lisa, from approximately 1503 to 1507, during his second period of residence in Florence, when he was over fifty. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p3543]
- According to Vasari, Leonardo 'employed the most elaborate artifices to keep the lady amused during the sittings and to retain the famous smile on her features.'. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p3543]
- Freud argues that the smile 'exercised no less powerful a fascination on the artist than on all who have looked at it for the last four hundred years' and 'from that date the captivating smile reappears in all his pictures and in those of his pupils.'. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p3543]
- Freud interprets: 'It may very well have been that Leonardo was fascinated by Mona Lisa's smile for the reason that it awoke something in him which had for long lain dormant in his mind – probably an old memory' of his mother. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p3546]

## Inferences

- Inference: Freud infers that 'it was his mother who possessed the mysterious smile – the smile that he had lost and that fascinated him so much when he found it again in the Florentine lady.'
