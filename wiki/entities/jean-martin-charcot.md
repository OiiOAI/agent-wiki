# Jean-Martin Charcot

## Summary

Jean-Martin Charcot (1825–1893) was the French neurologist who established the Salpêtrière as a centre of neurological research and teaching. He was Professor of Pathological Anatomy at the Collège de France and later held the newly created Chair of Neuropathology at the Salpêtrière (1881). Freud studied under him during the winter of 1885–86 and credited this period with redirecting his scientific interests from neurology to psychopathology. Charcot's work on hysteria, hypnotism, and organic nervous diseases profoundly influenced Freud's early theoretical development.

## Key facts

- Charcot worked at the Salpêtrière for seventeen years before his appointment to the Chair of Neuropathology in 1881, having previously worked there voluntarily for twelve years. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p47]
- Charcot established at the Salpêtrière a laboratory for anatomical and physiological studies, a pathological museum, a photography studio, an ophthalmological room, and electrical and hydropathic institutes. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p47]
- Freud described Charcot as exhibiting 'the liveliness, cheerfulness and formal perfection of speech' attributed to the French national character, combined with 'the patience and love of work' attributed to the German character. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p47]
- Charcot's work on hysteria reduced the connection of the neurosis with the genital system to its correct proportions by demonstrating the frequency of male hysteria and traumatic hysteria. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p50]
- Charcot regarded hypnotism as a field of phenomena to be submitted to scientific description, comparing it to his earlier work on multiple sclerosis or progressive muscular atrophy. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p52]
- Charcot was sixty years old during Freud's stay in 1885–86 and had centreed his work 'almost entirely on the neuroses, and above all on hysteria.'. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p50]
- Charcot described as hystero-epilepsy the severe cases of hysteria which included in their attacks an epileptoid phase; he later abandoned this name and described such cases as 'grande hystérie'. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p102]
- According to Charcot, hysterical patients exhibit three stages of hypnosis, each distinguished by special physical signs including enormous neuromuscular hyperexcitability and somnambulistic contractures. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p83]
- Freud initially supported Charcot's viewpoint in his preface to Bernheim, though later research led him to reconsider. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p67]
- The profound effect which Charcot's clinic produced on Freud is shown in the report he made on his return from Paris in 1886. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p67]
- Charcot taught that to explain hysteria one must apply to psychology. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p197]
- Charcot set Freud the task of comparative study of organic and hysterical motor paralyses based on observations at the Salpêtrière. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p186]
- Charcot taught that hysteria is a disease of excessive manifestations, tending to produce symptoms with the greatest possible intensity. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p191]
- Freud described Charcot as 'the great discoverer' and his lectures as containing 'much that is novel' qualifying them as a textbook for students. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p201]
- In 1882 or 1883, Charcot was given a Chair of Neuropathology in the Faculty of Medicine and a clinic with auxiliary departments at the Salpêtrière. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p912]
- Charcot turned his attention almost exclusively to hysteria around 1882-1883, making it the focus of general interest. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p913]
- Charcot demonstrated that hysterical paralyses could be artificially reproduced by hypnotizing patients and using suggestion or light blows. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p922]
- Charcot's work restored dignity to the topic of hysteria, which had fallen into disrepute; patients were no longer necessarily regarded as malingerers. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p913]
- Freud spent the winter of 1885-1886 studying under Charcot at the Salpêtrière. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p924]

## Inferences

- Inference: The tension between Charcot's somatic approach and Bernheim's psychical approach to hypnosis foreshadowed Freud's later development of psychoanalysis, which emphasized psychical mechanisms.
- Inference: Charcot's clinical method relied on identifying present cases with stored clinical pictures and recognizing 'types' with their transitional forms and formes frustes.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Freud's report describes Charcot's methods and personality but does not detail specific theoretical formulations; the theoretical significance of Charcot's work was elaborated by Freud in later writings.
