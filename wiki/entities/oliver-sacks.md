# Oliver Sacks

## Summary

Oliver Sacks (1935–2015) was a neurologist and author who profoundly influenced Mark Solms. Sacks was known for taking seriously the subjective reports of his patients and for documenting in exquisite detail the clinical journeys of patients with neurological disorders. His books Migraine (1970), Awakenings (1973), A Leg to Stand On (1984), and The Man Who Mistook His Wife for a Hat (1985) provided models for understanding neurology from the patient's perspective. Solms entered into correspondence with Sacks after reading his work, and they remained in contact until Sacks's death.

## Key facts

- Sacks published Awakenings (1973), which recorded in detail the clinical journeys of post-encephalitic patients 'awakened' by levodopa, a drug that increases dopamine availability. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p21]
- In A Leg to Stand On (1984), Sacks wrote 'Neuropsychology is admirable, but it excludes the psyche — it excludes the experiencing, active, living I', a line that Solms says captured his disappointment with the field perfectly. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p21]
- Upon reading Sacks's work, Solms entered into a correspondence that continued until Sacks's death in 2015. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p21]
- Sacks's publications were widely derided by some colleagues, with one commentator calling him 'The man who mistook his patients for a literary career'. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p22]
- Sacks was one of the plenary speakers at the inaugural congress of the International Neuropsychoanalysis Society in 2000. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p48]
- Levodopa, which Sacks used to treat post-encephalitic patients, also increases dreaming frequency and intensity and can push patients into psychosis — a side effect whose onset is often marked by unusually vivid dreams. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Hidden Spring - Mark Solms.pdf#p33]

## Inferences

- Inference: Sacks's willingness to incorporate subjective patient reports into neurology provided an intellectual precedent for Solms's neuropsychoanalytic approach, which similarly foregrounds lived experience.
