# Glial Cells

## Summary

Glial cells are non-neuronal cells of the nervous system that are specialized to regulate various aspects of neuronal function. They play essential roles in enabling and guiding neural development, insulating axonal processes, controlling the extracellular milieu, supporting synaptic transmission, facilitating learning and memory, and modulating pathological processes. In the vertebrate nervous system, glia are divided into two major classes: macroglia (oligodendrocytes, Schwann cells, and astrocytes) and microglia. Approximately 90% of all glial cells are macroglia, with about half being myelin-producing cells and half being astrocytes.

## Key facts

- In the human brain, approximately 90% of all glial cells are macroglia, with roughly half being myelin-producing cells (oligodendrocytes and Schwann cells) and half being astrocytes. - Microglia are the brain's resident immune cells and phagocytes but also have homeostatic functions in the healthy brain. [raw/books/neuroscience/Eric Kandel PRINCIPLES OF NEURAL SCIENCE Sixth Edition - Eric R Kandel; John Koester; Sarah Mack; Steven Siegelbaum.pdf#p181]
- Some glial cells have receptors for neurotransmitters and voltage-gated ion channels that enable them to communicate with one another and with neurons to support neuronal signaling. [raw/books/neuroscience/Eric Kandel PRINCIPLES OF NEURAL SCIENCE Sixth Edition - Eric R Kandel; John Koester; Sarah Mack; Steven Siegelbaum.pdf#p178]
- Glial cells and neurons develop from common neuroepithelial progenitors of the embryonic nervous system. [raw/books/neuroscience/Eric Kandel PRINCIPLES OF NEURAL SCIENCE Sixth Edition - Eric R Kandel; John Koester; Sarah Mack; Steven Siegelbaum.pdf#p181]
