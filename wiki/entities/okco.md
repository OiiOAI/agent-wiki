# OKCo

## Summary

OKCo is a disguised manufacturer of home and business climate-control systems that illustrates how strategic recommendations can be developed but then ignored. The company had stale products compared to competitors with Wi-Fi-enabled systems, but the CEO's strategic plan ignored these issues and focused on financial projections.

## Key facts

- OKCo was a significant manufacturer of home and business-office climate-control systems with fourteen different models in its product line. [raw/books/strategy/The Crux - Richard Rumelt.pdf#p261]
- OKCo's systems had to be programmed by moving jumpers around on a printed circuit board, while competitors were bringing out systems tying into Ethernet or Wi-Fi. [raw/books/strategy/The Crux - Richard Rumelt.pdf#p261]
- The problem at OKCo was that the key challenge—the declining product—was not owned by the major policy makers. [raw/books/strategy/The Crux - Richard Rumelt.pdf#p288]
- The CEO and CFO presented a strategic plan that projected growing EBITDA and listed eight priorities, none of which addressed the technology gap identified in the study work. [raw/books/strategy/The Crux - Richard Rumelt.pdf#p288]
- The strategic plan essentially proposed paying down debt and cozying up to key customers whose needs matched the existing product concept. [raw/books/strategy/The Crux - Richard Rumelt.pdf#p288]
- Three years later, OKCo's decline in value triggered takeover bids; the three top managers retired with hefty payment packages and half the employees were let go. [raw/books/strategy/The Crux - Richard Rumelt.pdf#p289]

## Inferences

- Inference: OKCo's fate demonstrates that strategic analysis without executive ownership of the challenge cannot produce effective strategy.
