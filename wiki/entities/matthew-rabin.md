# Matthew Rabin

## Summary

Matthew Rabin is an American behavioral economist at UC Berkeley who, in 2000, provided a rigorous mathematical proof that attempts to explain loss aversion through wealth-level utility are necessarily absurd. His theorem showed that any person who turns down a small fair gamble must (by consistency axioms alone) be committed to wildly unrealistic levels of risk aversion in larger gambles—a result that drew widespread attention to the limitations of expected utility theory.

## Key facts

- Rabin proved mathematically in 2000 that a person who rejects a 50-50 gamble losing $100 to win $200 is committed by the axioms of expected utility to rejecting a 50-50 gamble losing $200 to win $20,000—an absurd conclusion showing that loss aversion cannot be explained by curvature of the utility function over wealth. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p369]
- Rabin and Thaler co-authored a famously provocative article concluding that 'the time has come for utility economists to admit that expected utility is ex-hypothesis'—a statement comparing expected utility theory to the dead parrot in the Monty Python sketch, which provoked strong reactions from mainstream economists.
