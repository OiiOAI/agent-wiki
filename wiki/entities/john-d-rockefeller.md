# John D. Rockefeller

## Summary

John D. Rockefeller (1839-1937) rose from poverty to become the world's first billionaire and founder of Standard Oil. His story serves as a case study in what Greene calls the 'sophisticated aggressor'—an individual driven by an overwhelming need for control, cloaked in a righteous narrative that masks aggressive intent. Rockefeller's tactics included: reading opponents' willpower and exploiting their weaknesses, creating artificial urgency to make rivals act emotionally, using 'dull' appearances as camouflage for relentless strategy, and absorbing short-term losses to outlast competitors. His methods systematically eliminated rivals in the oil industry, demonstrating how sophisticated aggression operates through psychological manipulation rather than overt force.

## Key facts

- Rockefeller was born in 1839 to William Rockefeller, a con artist who alternately abandoned and returned to the family, creating an unpredictable childhood that instilled both a desperate need for order and a visceral love of money. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p592]
- In 1858, at age 18, Rockefeller partnered with Maurice B. Clark, borrowing $2,000 to start Clark and Rockefeller, a produce business in Cleveland, Ohio. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p578]
- Rockefeller used his religious beliefs to construct what Greene calls the 'aggressor's narrative'—convincing himself that establishing order in the chaotic oil business was a divine mission, making his ruthless actions feel morally justified. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p593]
- In 1865, Rockefeller engineered the dissolution of Clark and Rockefeller, buying out Clark's share of the refinery at $72,500—an apparently inflated price that Clark thought foolish but which Rockefeller had prepared for months in advance. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p581]
- Rockefeller developed superior ability to read people's psychology, particularly their relative willpower and resiliency, sensing weakness through body language and patterns of action. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p594]
- In early 1872, Rockefeller executed the 'Cleveland Massacre,' buying out twenty of twenty-six refineries outside his control in a few months, using the rumored Southern Improvement Company (SIC) as a tool to instill fear. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p584]
- Rockefeller defeated Tom Scott, president of the Pennsylvania Railroad, by shutting down Standard Oil's Pennsylvania operations, forcing Scott's pipelines to have no oil to ship, absorbing massive short-term losses until Scott was financially ruined. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p586]
- Rockefeller systematically bought up Byron Benson's Tidewater Pipeline Company after outlasting him in a brutal competition involving purchasing land in the pipeline's path and drastically reducing Standard Oil's own rates. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p589]
- Rockefeller presented himself to the public as 'the embodiment of sweetness and light' during court cases, maintaining a charming facade while secretly keeping Standard Oil's monopoly intact through secret agreements. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p591]
- Rockefeller's primary motivation was an overwhelming need for control over his environment, developed unconsciously from childhood anxieties caused by his father's unpredictable presence and financial instability. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p593]

## Inferences

- Inference: Rockefeller's 'dull, bookkeeper' appearance functioned as camouflage, making rivals underestimate his ruthlessness and strategic patience until it was too late.
