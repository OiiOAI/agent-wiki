# Martin Heidegger

## Summary

German philosopher (1889-1976) whose work McGilchrist discusses extensively in relation to hemisphere differences. Heidegger's philosophy represents, according to McGilchrist, an expression of the stance or disposition towards the world of the right hemisphere - emphasizing Being (Sein), the importance of context, the lived body, and truth as unconcealing (aletheia) rather than correctness. Heidegger reached intuitively towards metaphor and valued the ambiguity of poetic language.

## Key facts

- For Heidegger, Being (Sein) is hidden, and things as they truly are can be 'unconcealed' only by a certain disposition of patient attention towards the world. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p218]
- Heidegger related truth to the Greek concept of aletheia, literally 'unconcealing' - truth is a coming into being, an act, a journey, not a thing. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p218]
- Heidegger's concept of Gestell (framing) represents the detachment of seeing things as re-presented, with context severed at the edges of the frame. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p222]
- For Heidegger, Dasein is 'to be there' in the world - the literal, actual, concrete, daily world - to be human at all is to be immersed in the earth. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p220]
- Heidegger emphasised the primacy of context: things only are what they are because they find themselves in the surroundings in which they find themselves. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p209]

## Inferences

- Inference: Without any knowledge of hemisphere differences, Heidegger was compelled to derive the reality and ultimate importance of the right-hemisphere world through philosophical analysis.
- Inference: Heidegger found himself increasingly resorting to poetry to convey the complexity and depth of his meaning, suggesting that philosophical language was inadequate to express what he understood.
