# Henry Molaison

## Summary

Henry Molaison (1926–2008), known in scientific literature as H.M., was an American amnesic patient who became one of the most important subjects in the history of neuroscience. Born in Manchester, Connecticut, he developed epilepsy at age ten and experienced both petit mal and grand mal seizures. At age 27, he underwent experimental bilateral medial temporal-lobe surgery by Dr. William Beecher Scoville in 1953. The surgery successfully controlled his seizures but left him with profound, permanent anterograde amnesia—he could not form new long-term memories. Despite his disability, Henry retained his intelligence, personality, and short-term memory. He was studied by neuroscientists for 55 years, providing fundamental insights into memory systems and localization of function in the brain.

## Inferences

- Inference: Henry's case demonstrated that the hippocampus and medial temporal-lobe structures are critical for forming new long-term memories, while short-term memory processes remain intact with cortical involvement.
- Inference: Despite his profound amnesia, Henry could learn motor skills (nondeclarative memory) without conscious recollection, revealing that different memory systems operate independently.
- Inference: Henry's case established that the capacity to form new memories is separate from overall intelligence.
- Inference: Henry's preserved motor-skill learning demonstrated that the hippocampus is not required for all forms of learning.
- Inference: Henry's preserved ability to learn motor skills while unable to form new declarative memories provided the first clear evidence that memory is not a single faculty but comprises multiple independent systems.
- Inference: Henry's preserved nondeclarative memory (skills, priming) contrasted sharply with his impaired declarative memory, demonstrating that different memory systems rely on distinct brain structures.
- Inference: His ability to learn crossword puzzle solutions that linked new information to preexisting schemas showed that some semantic learning can occur without hippocampal function when anchored to existing knowledge.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The exact cause of Henry's epilepsy remains uncertain—possibly related to a childhood bicycle accident, genetic predisposition from his father's side of the family, or both.
- Uncertain: The exact extent of his damage was not fully understood until MRI imaging became available in the 1990s.
- Uncertain: Whether the emotional intensity of his two retained memories (first cigarette, plane ride) explains their preservation, or whether other factors were involved, remains uncertain.
- Uncertain: Whether Henry experienced genuine REM dreams or merely recounted preoperative memories when questioned was uncertain; Corkin suspected the latter.
- Uncertain: The exact cause of Henry's dementia in his final years—Alzheimer disease, vascular dementia, or a combination—could not be determined definitively during his life.
