# Acetylcholine

## Summary

Acetylcholine (ACh) is a neurotransmitter used at the neuromuscular junction, autonomic ganglia, and throughout the central nervous system. It acts on two major receptor types: nicotinic (ionotropic) and muscarinic (metabotropic) receptors.

## Key facts

- Acetylcholine was the first neurotransmitter identified, discovered by Otto Loewi in the 1920s as the chemical messenger of the vagus nerve. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p228]
- ACh is released from presynaptic terminals contained within synaptic vesicles (typically 100-200 vesicles per terminal). [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p235]
- ACh binds to nicotinic receptors at all neuromuscular junctions to trigger muscle contraction. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p237]
- The same transmitter substance (ACh) can excite some postsynaptic cells and inhibit others depending on the receptor type expressed. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p237]

## Inferences

- Inference: The diversity of ACh receptor types allows a single neurotransmitter to produce varied physiological responses across different tissues.
