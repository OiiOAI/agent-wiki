# Sigmund Freud

## Summary

Sigmund Freud (1856-1939) was an Austrian neurologist and the founder of psychoanalysis. Before his work on the unconscious, Freud had a career as a neural scientist, including drawing the spinal ganglion of the lamprey Petromyzon, which appears on the cover of the Fifth Edition of Principles of Neural Science.

## Key facts

- Freud made a drawing of the spinal ganglion of the lamprey Petromyzon before he discovered the unconscious, documenting his early career as a neural scientist. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p7]
- Freud's drawing was published in 1878 in 'Über Spinalganglien und Rückenmark der Petromyzon' in the Sitzungsberichte der Mathematisch-Naturwissenschaftlichen Classe der Kaiserlichen Akademie der Wissenschaften. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p7]
- Freud began his career as a laboratory neuroscientist before becoming a neurologist in private practice due to financial necessity. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p228]
- In 1891 Freud wrote On Aphasia, showing flaws in localizationism and proposing that the brain dynamically reorganizes itself for culturally acquired functions like reading and writing. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p228]
- In 1895 Freud completed the 'Project for a Scientific Psychology,' proposing the synapse concept several years before Sherrington and describing how synapses might be changed by learning. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p229]
- Freud proposed in 1888 that when two neurons fire simultaneously, this firing facilitates their ongoing association—the law of association by simultaneity, later called Hebb's law. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p229]
- Freud developed the concept of psychological critical periods, arguing that human sexuality and the ability to love have critical periods in early childhood called 'phases of organization.'. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p229]
- Freud observed that memories can be altered by subsequent events and retranscribed, writing in 1896 that 'memory is present not once but several times over.'. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p230]
- Freud discovered that patients projected transferences onto others unconsciously, and helping patients understand their transferences allowed them to improve their relationships. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p231]
- Freud used the term 'mental plasticity' to describe people's capacity for change and observed that some people lose this plasticity prematurely while others retain it beyond the usual age limit. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p246]
- Freud remarks that the sense of justice is the outgrowth of envy and jealousy, with the less favored moved by envy to take away advantages from the more favored. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p476]
- Rawls argues that Freud's account assumes an incorrect description of initial attitudes, confusing opposing interests with being moved by envy. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p477]
- Rawls distinguishes between envy and resentment, arguing that Freud conflates these and therefore wrongly traces the sense of justice to envy rather than to resentment at unfair treatment. [raw/books/philosophy/A Theory of Justice_ Original Edition - Rawls, John.pdf#p477]

## Inferences

- Inference: Rawls's critique implies that even if Freud's psychological observations were accurate, they would not establish that justice is founded on envy rather than on legitimate claims to fair treatment.
