# Jonathan Levav and Shai Danziger Parole Board Study

## Summary

A study by Jonathan Levav of Columbia University and Shai Danziger of Ben-Gurion University reviewed over 1,000 parole decisions by Israeli judges over ten months. The study found that prisoners appearing early in the morning received parole about 65 percent of the time, while those appearing late in the day received parole less than 10 percent of the time, with a lunch break restoring approval rates to over 60 percent.

## Key facts

- Prisoners who appeared at 8:50 A.M. received parole about 65 percent of the time, while those appearing at 4:25 P.M. received parole less than 10 percent of the time. [raw/books/psychology/Willpower _ Rediscovering the Greatest Human Strength - Roy F_ Baumeister, John Tierney.pdf#p93]
- Prisoners who appeared just before the midmorning snack break had only about 15 percent chance of parole, but those who appeared right after the food break had around 65 percent chance. [raw/books/psychology/Willpower _ Rediscovering the Greatest Human Strength - Roy F_ Baumeister, John Tierney.pdf#p93]
- The prisoner in Case 2 (first after lunch at 1:27 P.M.) received parole, while the prisoner in Case 3 (same crime, same sentence, but 3:10 P.M. and twelfth that session) was denied parole. [raw/books/psychology/Willpower _ Rediscovering the Greatest Human Strength - Roy F_ Baumeister, John Tierney.pdf#p94]
- Judges appeared to go for the 'easier and safest option' when depleted—leaving the prisoner in prison—which retains the option of paroling later without sacrificing the option of keeping the prisoner securely confined. [raw/books/psychology/Willpower _ Rediscovering the Greatest Human Strength - Roy F_ Baumeister, John Tierney.pdf#p94]

## Inferences

- Inference: The pattern demonstrates that decision fatigue affects real-world high-stakes decisions, with glucose replenishment (food) temporarily restoring decision-making capacity.
