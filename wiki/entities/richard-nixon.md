# Richard Nixon

## Summary

Richard Nixon (1913-1994) was the 37th President of the United States whose career exemplified the dangers of repressing one's dark side. His case study illustrates how a crafted public persona (RN) conflicted with an unconscious shadow side, ultimately leading to the Watergate scandal and his resignation in 1974.

## Key facts

- Nixon lost the 1960 presidential election to Kennedy and the 1962 California gubernatorial race, with the press declaring his political career over after his bitter concession speech. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p287]
- Nixon installed a secret voice-activated taping system throughout the White House to monitor staff and secure evidence for his future legacy. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p289]
- Henry Kissinger observed that Nixon had two distinct personalities and noted, 'Can you imagine what this man would have been like if somebody had loved him?'. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p295]
- Nixon displayed classic symptoms of the shadow side, including explosive anger, paranoia about enemies, desires for revenge, and anti-Semitic statements that contradicted his public Quaker values. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p291-292]
- On June 23, 1972, Nixon approved using the CIA to obstruct the FBI investigation into the Watergate break-in, recorded on the infamous tapes. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p293]
- Nixon resigned on August 9, 1974, after the Supreme Court ordered release of the tapes containing his direct involvement in the cover-up. [raw/books/psychology/The Laws of Human Nature - Robert Greene.pdf#p295]

## Inferences

- Inference: Nixon's unloved, needy childhood created a split between his public persona of strength and his repressed vulnerable self.
- Inference: The secret tapes represented an unconscious desire for punishment, as the shadow side sought release through self-destruction.
