# Benjamin Libet

## Summary

Benjamin Libet was a neuroscientist at the University of California, San Francisco who performed influential experiments in the early 1980s on the brain basis of voluntary action. His experiments measuring readiness potentials before voluntary actions became one of the most famous and controversial studies in consciousness science, suggesting unconscious brain activity precedes conscious intention.

## Key facts

- Benjamin Libet performed experiments at UCSF in the early 1980s on the brain basis of voluntary action, measuring readiness potentials using EEG. [raw/books/neuroscience/Being You _ A New Science of Consciousness - Seth, Anil.pdf#p203]
- Libet found that the readiness potential was identifiable hundreds of milliseconds before conscious intention to move after averaging across trials. [raw/books/neuroscience/Being You _ A New Science of Consciousness - Seth, Anil.pdf#p203]
- Libet proposed the controversial idea of 'free won't'—that conscious inhibition could intervene between the urge and action—but Seth notes this doesn't work because conscious inhibition is no more miraculous than conscious intention. [raw/books/neuroscience/Being You _ A New Science of Consciousness - Seth, Anil.pdf#p204]
- Benjamin Libet conducted experiments showing readiness potential begins ramping up before conscious awareness of intention to move. [raw/books/neuroscience/Free Agents _ How Evolution Gave Us Free Will - Kevin J_ Mitchell.pdf#p199]
- Libet concluded: 'The brain evidently decides to initiate or prepare to initiate the act at a time before there is any reportable subjective awareness'. [raw/books/neuroscience/Free Agents _ How Evolution Gave Us Free Will - Kevin J_ Mitchell.pdf#p201]
- Later work by Uri Maoz, Liad Mudrik and colleagues showed readiness potential only appears in arbitrary, inconsequential decisions, not deliberative ones. [raw/books/neuroscience/Free Agents _ How Evolution Gave Us Free Will - Kevin J_ Mitchell.pdf#p205]

## Inferences

- Inference: The Libet experiments have been widely overinterpreted - they relate only to arbitrary decisions 'on a whim' and not to consequential deliberative choices.
