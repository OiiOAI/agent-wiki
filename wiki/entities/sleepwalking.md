# Sleepwalking

## Summary

Sleepwalking (somnambulism) arises from deep N3 sleep and involves surprisingly complex behaviors while the person remains unaware of their surroundings. Up to 80 percent of adult sleepwalkers remember 'mini-dreams' related to their activities. EEG studies reveal a brain that looks both awake and asleep simultaneously, with activity in some regions turned down (like sleep) and others turned up to levels seen in emotionally driven waking behavior.

## Key facts

- Sleepwalking arises out of deep N3 sleep, with sleepwalkers capable of complex behaviors including cooking, eating, driving, and even wielding weapons. [raw/books/neuroscience/When Brains Dream - Antonio Zadra.pdf#p224]
- Up to 80 percent of adult sleepwalkers remember 'mini-dreams' related to their sleepwalking activities. [raw/books/neuroscience/When Brains Dream - Antonio Zadra.pdf#p224]
- EEG studies reveal that during sleepwalking, brain activity in some regions is turned down like sleep while activity in other regions is turned up to levels normally seen in emotionally driven waking behavior. [raw/books/neuroscience/When Brains Dream - Antonio Zadra.pdf#p225]
- The coexistence of deep sleep and wakefulness can be observed up to 20 seconds before a sleepwalking episode actually begins. [raw/books/neuroscience/When Brains Dream - Antonio Zadra.pdf#p225]
- Sleepwalking affects 2 to 4 percent of the general adult population. [raw/books/neuroscience/When Brains Dream - Antonio Zadra.pdf#p224]

## Inferences

- Inference: Sleepwalking represents a unique state where sleep and wakefulness coexist across different brain regions, suggesting that consciousness and behavioral control are more distributed than previously assumed.
