# H.M. (Henry Molaison)

## Summary

H.M. (Henry Molaison, 1926–2008) was a patient who suffered severe bilateral medial temporal lobe damage from epilepsy surgery in 1953. The surgery, performed by William Scoville, successfully relieved his seizures but left him with profound anterograde amnesia—he could not form new long-term memories. However, he retained short-term memory, pre-surgical long-term memories, and could learn motor skills through implicit memory. H.M. was studied by Brenda Milner for nearly fifty years and became the most famous case in neuroscience, fundamentally transforming understanding of memory systems.

## Key facts

- At age nine, H.M. was knocked down by a bicycle rider, sustaining a head injury that led to epilepsy, which worsened until he had up to ten blackouts and one major seizure weekly. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p140]
- Scoville removed the inner surface of H.M.'s medial temporal lobe on both sides, along with the hippocampus, which relieved his seizures but caused devastating memory loss. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p140]
- H.M. could retain new information as long as his attention was not diverted, but a minute or two after his attention shifted, he could not remember the previous subject or anything he thought about it. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p140]
- H.M. could never remember having performed the mirror-tracing task on an earlier day, yet his performance improved from day to day. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p142]

## Inferences

- Inference: H.M.'s preserved ability to learn motor skills while lacking conscious memory demonstrated that the hippocampus is not the storage site for all types of memory, only for converting short-term to long-term explicit memories.
