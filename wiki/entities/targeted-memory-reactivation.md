# Targeted Memory Reactivation

## Summary

Targeted memory reactivation (TMR) is a technique pioneered by Ken Paller that allows researchers to hack the sleep process by triggering the reactivation of specific memories during sleep. By playing sounds associated with learned information while subjects are in deep sleep, researchers have shown that memories can be enhanced without waking the subject. TMR has been used to improve learning of video games, new languages, creative problem-solving, and even reduce implicit bias.

## Key facts

- Ken Paller pioneered the technique of targeted memory reactivation (TMR) to hack sleep by triggering reactivation of specific memories during sleep. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p180]
- In TMR experiments, subjects learn information while sounds play in the background (e.g., a picture with its associated sound), then those sounds are replayed during sleep. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p181]
- After waking, subjects are unaware that sounds were played during their nap, yet the sounds affected their memories for what they had learned the previous day. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p181]
- TMR has been shown to improve learning of video games, new languages, and stimulate creative approaches to unsolvable problems. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p181]
- TMR has been used to reactivate memories that contradict racial and gender stereotypes, which could reduce implicit bias. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p181]
- Simona Ghetti used TMR with toddlers (2-3 years old) and found hippocampal activity spiked when songs previously heard during play sessions were played during naps. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p181]
- In Ghetti's study, the amount of hippocampal activity during sleep was directly related to how much information the child had learned about the stuffed animals during play sessions. [raw/books/neuroscience/Why We Remember_ Unlocking Memory's Power - Charan Ranganath.pdf#p182]

## Inferences

- Inference: Even in babies, the sleeping brain is highly receptive to the outside world, suggesting TMR could be a valuable tool for studying memory development.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The full extent of what can be achieved through TMR is still being explored.
