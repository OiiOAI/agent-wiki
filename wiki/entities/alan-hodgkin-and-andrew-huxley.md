# Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley

## Summary

British physiologists Alan Hodgkin (1914–1998) and Andrew Huxley (1917–2016) who formulated the ionic hypothesis explaining the mechanisms of the action potential. Working with the squid's giant axon, they discovered that sodium ions rushing into the cell produce the upstroke of the action potential while potassium ions leaving the cell produce the downstroke.

## Key facts

- Hodgkin and Huxley discovered that the action potential reaches +40 millivolts at its peak, not zero as Bernstein had predicted, indicating selective rather than generalized permeability breakdown. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p97]
- They proposed voltage-gated ion channels with 'gates' that open and close in rapid succession during an action potential. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p99]
- The upstroke of the action potential results from sodium ion influx; the downstroke results from potassium ion efflux. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p99]
- They shared the 1963 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for their discoveries concerning the ionic mechanisms involved in excitation and inhibition in the peripheral and central portions of the nerve cell membrane. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p100]
- Hodgkin studied the squid's giant axon at the Plymouth marine biological station in 1939, where J.Z. Young had discovered this axon suitable for intracellular recording. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p97]
