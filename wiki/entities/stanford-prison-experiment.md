# Stanford Prison Experiment

## Summary

The Stanford Prison Experiment (1971) randomly assigned 24 male volunteers to be 'guards' or 'prisoners' in a simulated prison. Guards rapidly became abusive; prisoners became passive or rebelled. The experiment, originally planned for two weeks, was halted after six days due to escalating brutality. Zimbardo's 'bad barrel' theory argues that situational forces can turn ordinary people into perpetrators of evil.

## Key facts

- The Stanford Prison Experiment was halted after 6 days (planned for 14) when guards' abuse and prisoners' distress became extreme. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p476]
- Zimbardo himself served as 'superintendent,' likely creating demand characteristics that pushed guards toward more extreme behavior than situational forces alone would have. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p478]
- The BBC Prison Study (Reicher & Haslam, 2001) found prisoners organized resistance and created a power-sharing commune—contradicting the SPE's 'bad barrel' interpretation. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p479]
- Zimbardo claimed Reicher and Haslam were 'scientifically irresponsible' and 'fraudulent'—an unusually strong academic dispute. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p480]
- Some guards and prisoners were psychological wrecks afterward; no human-subjects committee would approve the study today. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p478]
- The Stanford Prison Experiment was funded by the U.S. military, which was interested in improving military prisons. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p1171]
- Nearly all prisoners had indicated at the study's beginning they would rather be a prisoner than a guard, and many had volunteered to learn what prison would be like. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p1172]
- Many prisoners and guards were deeply distressed afterward, having seen how readily they were broken into passivity. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p1172]
- Zimbardo's recent work examines defiance of unjust authority rather than mere obedience. [raw/books/psychology/Behave_ The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst - Robert M Sapolsky; Michael Goldstrom; Overdrive Inc.pdf#p1174]

## Inferences

- Inference: The Abu Ghraib prison abuse scandal made the SPE urgently relevant decades later, reinforcing its place in psychological and public discourse despite methodological controversies.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Whether the SPE's outcome was due to situational forces ('bad barrel') or selection effects (volunteers scoring higher on authoritarianism) remains debated.
- Uncertain: The low base rate of independent replications makes it unclear whether the SPE's findings are robust.
