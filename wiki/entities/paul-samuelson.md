# Paul Samuelson

## Summary

Paul Samuelson was an American economist and Nobel laureate (1970) who made foundational contributions to expected utility theory. He famously engaged with a friend who accepted a series of favorable gambles but refused a single one, defending this position as potentially rational. Samuelson proved mathematically that a consistent utility maximizer who rejects one favorable gamble should reject the series—though he later acknowledged this conclusion was practically absurd, revealing a tension between normative theory and reasonable intuition.

## Key facts

- Samuelson asked a friend whether he would accept a 50-50 bet to lose $100 or win $200; the friend refused the single bet but agreed to 100 repetitions, reasoning that repeated favorable gambles eliminate risk. Samuelson proved this violated the axioms of expected utility, though the conclusion conflicted with common sense. [raw/books/strategy/Thinking, Fast and Slow (Cover Baru) - Daniel Kahneman.pdf#p435]
- Samuelson was present at the 1952 Paris conference where Maurice Allais demonstrated his paradox and initially did not notice the inconsistency in his own choices until Allais pointed it out, illustrating that even Nobel laureate economists were not immune to theory-induced blindness.
