# Lyova Zazetsky

## Summary

Lyova Zazetsky was a young Russian lieutenant who sustained a bullet wound to the left side of his brain in the battle of Smolensk in March 1943. His detailed diary 'I'll Fight On' documented his struggle to overcome the loss of his ability to understand relationships between symbols, logic, cause and effect, and spatial relationships. His case was studied by Aleksandr Luria over thirty years.

## Key facts

- Lyova Zazetsky was a young Russian lieutenant injured at the battle of Smolensk in 1943 when poorly equipped Russians faced the invading Nazi war machine. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p43]
- He sustained a bullet wound to the head with massive damage on the left side, deep inside his brain, at the junction of the temporal, occipital, and parietal lobes. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p43-p44]
- The shrapnel lodged in the part of the brain that helped him understand relationships between symbols, leaving him unable to understand logic, cause and effect, or spatial relationships. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p43]
- He couldn't distinguish his left from his right, couldn't understand grammar elements dealing with relationships, and prepositions like 'in,' 'out,' 'before,' 'after,' 'with,' and 'without' became meaningless. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p43]
- He could not comprehend a whole word, understand a whole sentence, or recall a complete memory because doing any of those things required relating symbols. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p43]
- His frontal lobes were spared, giving him the capacity to recognize his defects and the wish to overcome them. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p43]
- He could write but not read, because writing is intentional while reading is largely perceptual. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p43]
- He began a fragmentary diary called 'I'll Fight On' that swelled to three thousand pages. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p43]
- He wrote, 'I was killed March 2, 1943, but because of some vital power of my organism, I miraculously remained alive'. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p43]
- He wrote that 'mother's daughter' and 'daughter's mother' sounded the same to him, and he couldn't understand 'bigger' and 'smaller'. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p44]
- He described his experience as being 'in a fog all the time' with 'hazy visions that suddenly appear and just as suddenly disappear'. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p44]
- Luria observed him over thirty years and witnessed his relentless fight 'to live, not merely exist'. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p44]

## Inferences

- Inference: Zazetsky's case demonstrated that while the brain can be damaged in specific ways affecting specific mental functions, the patient can remain aware of and fight against those deficits [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p43-p44]
- Inference: His writing ability surviving while reading ability was lost suggests these functions are processed by different brain regions and pathways [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p43]
