# Fyodor Dostoevsky

## Summary

Russian novelist and writer (1821-1881) whom Freud analyzed extensively in his 1928 essay 'Dostoevsky and Parricide'. Freud identified four facets of Dostoevsky's personality: the creative artist, the neurotic, the moralist, and the sinner. Freud classified him as a masochist whose destructive drive was directed inward rather than outward, and argued that his so-called epilepsy was actually hysterical. His ambivalent Oedipal relationship to his father was seen as central to his neurosis.

## Key facts

- Dostoevsky's place in literature is not far behind Shakespeare, with The Brothers Karamazov being the most magnificent novel ever written. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p6707]
- Dostoevsky's strong destructive drive, which might easily have made him a criminal, was in his actual life directed mainly against his own person (inwards instead of outwards), finding expression as masochism and a sense of guilt. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p6709]
- Dostoevsky's so-called epilepsy was probably hysterical epilepsy—a symptom of his neurosis rather than organic brain disease—linked to his Oedipal conflicts. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p6710]
- The boy's relation to his father is ambivalent, combining hatred seeking to get rid of the father as a rival with tenderness, and leading to identification with the father. ## Inferences
