# Erin Wamsley

## Summary

Erin Wamsley is a dream researcher who conducted groundbreaking experiments on the connection between dreaming and sleep-dependent memory processing. Working in Robert Stickgold's lab, she designed experiments using virtual mazes that demonstrated that participants who reported dreams related to a task showed almost ten times more improvement after naps compared to those who reported no related dreams. Her research provided key evidence that dreaming is associated with sleep-dependent memory processing.

## Key facts

- Erin Wamsley joined Bob Stickgold's lab in 2007 to document the connection between dreaming and sleep-dependent memory processing. [raw/books/neuroscience/When Brains Dream - Antonio Zadra.pdf#p106]
- She designed a virtual maze experiment where participants who reported dreaming about the task showed almost ten times more improvement after naps—averaging 91 seconds faster at retest. [raw/books/neuroscience/When Brains Dream - Antonio Zadra.pdf#p107]
- Participants who had no memory of dreaming about the task took, on average, 1½ minutes longer to find their way out of the maze after naps. [raw/books/neuroscience/When Brains Dream - Antonio Zadra.pdf#p107]
- Wamsley found that dream reports related to the maze were not directly helping memory enhancement but rather predicting subsequent improvement, suggesting dreams serve a different function. [raw/books/neuroscience/When Brains Dream - Antonio Zadra.pdf#p107]
- Her study using the Alpine Racer II video game found that dream incorporations became more indirect over time, from direct game elements to sensations, locations, or themes related to the game. [raw/books/neuroscience/When Brains Dream - Antonio Zadra.pdf#p131]
- Erin Wamsley completed her PhD on REM and nonREM dreaming at City University of New York under John Antrobus. [raw/books/neuroscience/When brains dream _ understanding the science and mystery of - Antonio Zadra.pdf#p102]
- Wamsley designed a virtual maze learning experiment demonstrating that participants who dreamed about the task showed almost ten times more improvement after naps. [raw/books/neuroscience/When brains dream _ understanding the science and mystery of - Antonio Zadra.pdf#p103]
- Her research showed that dreams about the maze contained loosely related content (bat caves, seeking lost items) rather than direct replays of the task. [raw/books/neuroscience/When brains dream _ understanding the science and mystery of - Antonio Zadra.pdf#p104]
- Wamsley's findings contradicted the assumption that dream collection would interrupt memory processing, as later studies confirmed that waking participants even five or six times had no effect on memory evolution.
