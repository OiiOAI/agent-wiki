# Cheryl Schiltz

## Summary

Cheryl Schiltz is a woman who developed severe vestibular damage after receiving excessive doses of the antibiotic gentamicin following a hysterectomy in 1997. She experienced perpetual falling sensations and lost 95-98% of her vestibular function. Her case became central to demonstrating Paul Bach-y-Rita's sensory substitution approach, where she wore a tongue-based device that replaced her vestibular apparatus. Through repeated use, she experienced a residual effect that gradually extended from seconds to hours, then days, and eventually to four months without the device.

## Key facts

- Cheryl Schiltz lost her vestibular function after a postoperative infection following a hysterectomy in 1997 was treated with gentamicin. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p15]
- She had only about 2 percent of vestibular function remaining after the damage. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p16]
- She lost her job as an international sales representative and lives on a disability check of $1,000 a month. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p13]
- She was one of a small group called 'Wobblers'—people damaged by gentamicin who share this condition. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p15]
- The first time she wore the tongue display device, the sense of perpetual falling left her for the first time in five years. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p17]
- After wearing the device for twenty minutes, her residual effect lasted three hours and twenty minutes—ten times the wearing time. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p19]
- Over the next year, her residual effect progressed to multiple hours, to days, and then to four months, after which she no longer uses the device. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p20]
