# Parmenides of Elea

## Summary

Parmenides (fl. early fifth century BC) was a priest of Apollo who founded a school in Elea, a Greek colony in southern Italy. His surviving poem has a double structure—'The Way of Truth' versus 'The Way of Belief'—asserting that the phenomenal world is a deception. His central claim is that 'thought and being are the same thing': what can be thought must be, and contradiction indicates error. This led to denying the reality of motion and change, the impossibility of the void, and the conclusion that Being is timeless, undifferentiated, and unchanging. McGilchrist interprets this as the paradigmatic expression of left-hemisphere philosophy: prioritizing logical system over experience, refusing ambiguity or contradiction, and achieving certainty and stasis.

- Parmenides was a priest of Apollo in Elea whose poem's message is that the phenomenal world is a deception; 'thought and being are the same thing.'. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p377]
- He held that what can be thought must be, and what cannot be thought cannot exist; contradiction indicates error. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p377]
- This led to denying motion and change: 'All that exists' cannot move because it would move into the void, a logical impossibility. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p377]
- McGilchrist sees this philosophy as 'showing its allegiance to the world of the left hemisphere': prioritizing a logical system over truth to phenomena, refusing ambiguity or contradiction, and achieving certainty and stasis.
