# Phineas P. Gage

## Summary

Phineas P. Gage (1823–1861) was a railroad construction foreman in Vermont who survived a dramatic brain injury in 1848 when an iron tamping bar was driven through his skull. His subsequent profound personality changes—despite intact intelligence, language, and motor abilities—provided the first evidence that specific brain damage could impair social behavior and decision-making while preserving other cognitive functions. His case became foundational for understanding the neural basis of rationality and emotion.

## Key facts

- On September 13, 1848, an iron tamping bar (13.25 pounds, 3 feet 7 inches long) was driven through Gage's left cheek, through the base of his skull, traversing the front of his brain and exiting through the top of his head. [raw/books/neuroscience/Descartes' error _ emotion, reason, and the human brain.pdf#p24]
- Gage survived the accident and was able to speak and walk immediately afterward, though he lost vision in his left eye. [raw/books/neuroscience/Descartes' error _ emotion, reason, and the human brain.pdf#p26]
- After the accident, Gage's character underwent radical changes: he became fitful, irreverent, indulged in gross profanity, showed little deference for fellows, was impatient of restraint, and was capricious and vacillating—friends said 'Gage was no longer Gage.'. [raw/books/neuroscience/Descartes' error _ emotion, reason, and the human brain.pdf#p28]
- Gage's employers refused to take him back, not because of lack of physical ability but because of his new character—his 'mind so changed' that they could not give him his place. [raw/books/neuroscience/Descartes' error _ emotion, reason, and the human brain.pdf#p28]
- Modern neuroimaging reconstruction by Hanna Damasio confirmed the iron damaged the ventromedial prefrontal regions bilaterally while sparing motor and language cortices. [raw/books/neuroscience/Descartes' error _ emotion, reason, and the human brain.pdf#p52]
- Gage died on May 21, 1861, at age 38, likely from status epilepticus. [raw/books/neuroscience/Descartes' error _ emotion, reason, and the human brain.pdf#p30]

## Inferences

- Inference: The fact that Gage's decision-making was impaired while his language, memory, and calculation abilities remained intact suggests that separate brain systems handle different aspects of rationality, and that social decision-making depends on brain regions distinct from those supporting language or basic cognition.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: It is uncertain whether Gage experienced the full emotional blunting described in modern patients like Elliot, as the historical record does not provide detailed documentation of his subjective emotional state.
