# William Donald Hamilton

## Summary

William Donald Hamilton (1936-2000) was a British evolutionary biologist whose 1964 papers on kin selection revolutionized social biology. He formalized the conditions under which altruism evolves: rB > C (relatedness times benefit exceeds cost), providing the theoretical foundation for understanding nepotism, parental care, and eusociality as gene-level adaptations.

## Key facts

- Hamilton's two papers of 1964 are described as 'among the most important contributions to social ethology ever written,' despite being neglected by ethologists for years. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p120]
- Hamilton realized that genes for altruism could spread if they caused bodies to help copies of themselves in relatives, with the mathematical precision of rB > C. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p120]
- Hamilton calculated that saving ten first cousins (r = 1/8) would genetically compensate for the death of an altruist, providing a quantitative framework for kin-selected altruism. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p124]
- Dawkins criticizes E.O. Wilson for treating kin selection as a special case of group selection, arguing it is properly understood as a consequence of gene selection. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p125]
- Dawkins notes that Wilson's definition of kin selection excluded offspring, which Dawkins considers a confusing limitation given that parental care is the paradigm example of kin-selected behavior. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p125]
- Hamilton proposed the 'selfish herd' model showing how aggregation could evolve from simple selfish behavior: each individual minimizes its own 'domain of danger' by positioning itself away from edges toward the center of the group, creating emergent bunching. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p204]
- Hamilton realized that at least in the ants, bees, and wasps, workers may be more closely related to the brood than the queen herself is, because of the haplodiploid sex determination system creating 75% sister relatedness. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p210]
- Hamilton's relatedness calculations in Hymenoptera explain worker sterility: a worker's genes benefit more from helping rear sisters (75% related) than from reproducing herself (50% related to offspring), making sterility evolutionarily advantageous. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p210]
- Hamilton's 1964 papers 'The genetical evolution of social behaviour' (I and II) are cited by number in the index and bibliography but his name did not appear in the index of two major ethology textbooks published in 1970. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p377]
- Hamilton suggested in 1972 a cyclic inbreeding theory for termites, which Bartz developed seven years later, though Hamilton himself forgot he had thought of it first. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p369]
- Hamilton's parasite theory of sexual selection suggests females choose males based on disease resistance, with parasites maintaining variation by constantly changing what 'best' means. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p360]
- Hamilton's two papers received an average citation rate of about 37 per year from 1964 onward, with exponential growth beginning around 1973-1974. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p377]

## Inferences

- Inference: Hamilton's work showed that the distinction between family and non-family is not hard-and-fast but a matter of mathematical probability, challenging naive 'group selection' intuitions.
- Inference: Hamilton's theory explains why parental care (certainty of relatedness) is more common than sibling care, even though genetic relatedness is identical.
- Inference: Dawkins' observation that Hamilton's ideas were dormant until the mid-1970s, then exploded, suggests a slow-burning exponential bandwagon rather than book-triggered spread.
