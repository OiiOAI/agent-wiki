# John Eccles

## Summary

John Eccles (1903–1997) was an Australian physiologist who was initially a strong advocate of electrical transmission in the central nervous system. During World War II, he worked with Bernard Katz and Stephen Kuffler in Sydney, Australia. As evidence mounted against electrical transmission, Eccles became dispirited until his encounter with philosopher Karl Popper in 1946, who convinced him that being proven wrong was a sign of good science. Eccles subsequently accepted the chemical hypothesis, confirmed that both excitatory and inhibitory synaptic transmission are chemically mediated, and shared the 1963 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Hodgkin and Huxley.

## Key facts

- Eccles initially championed electrical transmission in the central nervous system, arguing it was too quick to be chemical. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p105]
- Eccles hypothesized that the synaptic potential has two components: an initial rapid component mediated electrically and a prolonged residual component mediated by a chemical transmitter. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p108]
- Karl Popper encouraged Eccles to embrace being proven wrong, arguing that science proceeds through cycles of conjecture and refutation. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p109]
- Eccles confirmed that both excitatory and inhibitory synaptic transmission are chemically mediated in the spinal cord. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p111]

## Inferences

- Inference: Eccles's intellectual transformation illustrates the importance of falsification in science and how scientific controversies can have merit on both sides, as electrical transmission was later found to occur at some synapses.
