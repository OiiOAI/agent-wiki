# Anaximander

## Summary

Anaximander of Miletus (c. 610–546 BC), pupil of Thales, advanced beyond his teacher by positing the apeiron ('unbounded' or 'indefinite') as the originary principle. Unlike water or other elements, the apeiron cannot be qualified and contains opposing principles without their mutual annihilation. McGilchrist interprets Anaximander's insights—as opposed to his pupil Anaximenes who returned to an elemental arch—as insights into the right-hemisphere world: the primacy of the indefinite, and the arch as process rather than thing.

## Key facts

- Anaximander was Thales' pupil who posited the apeiron as the 'unbounded' or 'indefinite' originary principle from which all things arise. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p373]
- He recognized that no qualified element like water could fulfill the condition of containing opposing principles without mutual annihilation. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p373]
- Opposing principles within the apeiron 'pay retribution to one another for their trespasses on each other, according to an inescapable logic in things.'. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p373]
- McGilchrist sees Anaximander's insights as 'insights into the right-hemisphere world,' unlike his pupil Anaximenes who returned to an elemental arch. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p373]
