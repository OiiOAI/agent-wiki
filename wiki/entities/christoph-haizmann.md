# Christoph Haizmann

## Summary

Christoph Haizmann (c. 1637–1700) was a Bavarian painter who in 1677 was brought to the shrine of Mariazell seeking deliverance from a pact with the Devil he claimed to have signed nine years earlier. Freud analyzed his case as a classic demonological neurosis—a hypochondriacal neurosis of an earlier century dressed in supernatural garb—published as 'A Seventeenth-Century Demonological Neurosis' (1923). Haizmann was ultimately admitted as Brother Chrysostomus to the Brothers Hospitallers, where he died in 1700.

## Key facts

- Christoph Haizmann was a Bavarian painter brought to Mariazell on September 5, 1677, with a letter of introduction from the village priest of Pottenbrunn, near Vienna. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p5957]
- He reported having signed a pact with the Devil nine years earlier (September 24, 1668), in a state of melancholic depression following his father's death and uncertainty about his livelihood as a painter. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p5963]
- He suffered from visions, convulsions, and 'absences'—symptoms Freud interpreted as neurotic rather than genuinely demonic. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p5960]
- The Devil appeared to him initially in the form of an 'honest elderly citizen' with a brown beard, red cloak, black hat, and a black dog; in later visions the Devil acquired horns, eagle's claws, bat's wings, and two pairs of female breasts. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p5967]
- After initial relief at Mariazell, he suffered renewed attacks in Vienna (October 1677–January 1678), during which he experienced visions of cavaliers, beautiful women, and eventually figures purporting to be Christ and the Virgin Mary. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p5982]
- He died in 1700 in the Monastery of the Brothers Hospitallers at Neustadt on the Moldau, having been admitted to the Order as Brother Chrysostomus. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p5961]

## Inferences

- Inference: Freud interpreted the two bonds (one in ink, one in blood) as reflecting a neurotic ambivalence toward the father figure, with the Devil serving as a direct father substitute.
- Inference: Freud hypothesized that Haizmann's inhibition in work after his father's death might reflect 'deferred obedience'—his father may have opposed his wish to become a painter.
- Inference: The Devil's female breasts were interpreted by Freud as representing both a feminine (passive, receptive) attitude toward the father and displacement from the mother.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The exact dating of the two bonds is disputed among the original sources—the village priest's letter, the Abbot Franciscus's deposition, and the compiler's preface do not agree on whether the first bond was signed in 1668 or 1669.
