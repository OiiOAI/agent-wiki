# Evolution of Cephalopods

## Summary

The evolution of cephalopods spans over 500 million years, from a cap-shaped mollusk ancestor to the diverse modern forms. Key transitions include: gaining buoyant gas-filled shells for floating, inventing jet propulsion, developing tentacles from the foot for grasping, and the loss of external shells in octopuses. This evolutionary path led to the only mollusks with complex active bodies capable of rapid, flexible behavior.

## Key facts

- Cephalopods began as small cap-shaped shellfish similar to limpets, with the shell growing into a horn shape over evolutionary time. [raw/books/neuroscience/Other Minds_ The Octopus, the Sea, and the - Peter Godfrey - Smith.pdf#p38]
- Making the shell buoyant by filling it with gas enabled some cephalopods to rise from the sea floor and enter the water column, where they became predators. [raw/books/neuroscience/Other Minds_ The Octopus, the Sea, and the - Peter Godfrey - Smith.pdf#p38]
- The foot differentiating into tentacles for grasping, combined with jet propulsion through a siphon, enabled cephalopods to become fearsome predators. [raw/books/neuroscience/Other Minds_ The Octopus, the Sea, and the - Peter Godfrey - Smith.pdf#p39]
- Sometime before the age of dinosaurs, some cephalopods began giving up their shells, enabling more freedom of movement at the cost of increased vulnerability. [raw/books/neuroscience/Other Minds_ The Octopus, the Sea, and the - Peter Godfrey - Smith.pdf#p40]
- The nautilus is the sole surviving shelled cephalopod, with a coiled shell that has changed little from 200 million years ago. [raw/books/neuroscience/Other Minds_ The Octopus, the Sea, and the - Peter Godfrey - Smith.pdf#p40]

## Inferences

- Inference: The loss of the shell in octopuses created a unique body without hard parts, enabling unprecedented shape-changing ability but requiring elaborate neural control.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The last common ancestor of modern cephalopods is not known.
- Uncertain: The oldest octopus fossil at 290 million years is uncertain—described as 'just one specimen, and little more than a smudge on a rock.'
