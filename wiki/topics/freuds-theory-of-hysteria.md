# Freud's Theory of Hysteria

## Summary

Freud's theory of hysteria, developed jointly with Breuer, posits that hysterical symptoms are ideogenic phenomena caused by psychical mechanisms including conversion, defence, and symbolization. The theory holds that incompatible ideas are excluded from conscious association through defence or hypnoid states, their affects converted into somatic symptoms that provide the patient relief from unbearable mental pain at the cost of physical illness and splitting of consciousness.

## Key facts

- Moebius defined hysteria as consisting of all pathological phenomena caused by ideas; Freud and Breuer accepted that only a portion of hysterical phenomena are ideogenic, while others arise from non-psychical causes. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p166-167]
- The fundamental pathological change enabling ideas as well as non-psychological stimuli to produce pathological effects lies in an abnormal excitability of the nervous system. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p191]
- Sick-nursing plays a significant part in the prehistory of hysteria because nurses accumulate impressions capable of affect which are hardly sufficiently perceived and have not been weakened by abreaction, creating material for retention hysteria. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p161-162]

## Inferences

- Inference: The theory integrates observations from multiple cases (Anna O., Fräulein Elisabeth von R., Frau Cäcilie M.) into a coherent framework where hysterical symptoms are meaningful expressions of unconscious psychical conflict.
