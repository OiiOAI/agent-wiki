# Evolution of Nervous Systems

## Summary

The evolution of nervous systems began approximately 700 million years ago in jellyfish-like animals. Two competing views explain their function: the sensory-motor view (linking perception to action) and the action-shaping view (coordinating body parts). Nervous systems evolved multiple times in parallel, with cephalopods developing a distributed architecture very different from the centralized vertebrate design. The Cambrian explosion dramatically accelerated nervous system evolution due to predator-prey dynamics.

## Key facts

- Nervous systems may have first evolved around 700 million years ago in jellyfish-like soft-bodied animals, based entirely on genetic evidence since there are no fossils this old. [raw/books/neuroscience/Other Minds_ The Octopus, the Sea, and the - Peter Godfrey - Smith.pdf#p25]
- Neurons cost a great deal of energy; in humans, nearly a quarter of food energy is spent just keeping the brain running. [raw/books/neuroscience/Other Minds_ The Octopus, the Sea, and the - Peter Godfrey - Smith.pdf#p22]
- The first nervous systems may have served primarily to coordinate actions rather than to link sensory input to motor output. [raw/books/neuroscience/Other Minds_ The Octopus, the Sea, and the - Peter Godfrey - Smith.pdf#p34]
- The Cambrian explosion saw a great increase in the importance of sensory-motor functions as predation became common. [raw/books/neuroscience/Other Minds_ The Octopus, the Sea, and the - Peter Godfrey - Smith.pdf#p34]
- Detlev Arendt proposed that nervous systems originated twice in the same animals—at different body locations, with a motion-controlling system eventually marching up to meet light-sensitive organs that became eyes. [raw/books/neuroscience/Other Minds_ The Octopus, the Sea, and the - Peter Godfrey - Smith.pdf#p35]

## Inferences

- Inference: The Cambrian 'information revolution' driven by image-forming eyes created evolutionary pressure for complex internal processing of sensory information.
- Inference: The vertebrate and cephalopod nervous system designs diverged from a common worm-like ancestor with simple clustering of neurons, leading to radically different architectures.
