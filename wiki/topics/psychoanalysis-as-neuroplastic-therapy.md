# Psychoanalysis as Neuroplastic Therapy

## Summary

This chapter presents evidence that psychoanalysis is a form of neuroplastic therapy that produces physical changes in the brain. Through the case of Mr. L., who overcame forty years of depression after treatment from age 58 to 62, the book demonstrates how talk therapy can alter gene expression, strengthen synaptic connections, and reorganize neural networks, effectively treating characterological problems once thought fixed.

## Key facts

- Mr. L. suffered recurring depressions for over forty years and relationship difficulties; his analysis lasted from age fifty-eight to sixty-two and rid him of forty years of symptoms while changing important aspects of his character. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p221]
- Psychoanalysis helps patients put unconscious procedural memories into words and context, plastically retranscribing them into conscious explicit memories so patients no longer need to relive or reenact them. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p235]
- The aim of the talking cure from a neurobiological viewpoint is 'to extend the functional sphere of influence of the prefrontal lobes,' which are less active during trauma reliving and flashbacks. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p238]
- Brain scans before and after psychotherapy show both that the brain plastically reorganizes itself in treatment and that the more successful the treatment, the greater the change. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p238]
- A study of depressed patients treated with interpersonal psychotherapy showed prefrontal brain activity normalized with treatment. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p239]
- Dr. Susan Vaughan argued that the talking cure works by 'talking to neurons,' and an effective psychotherapist is a 'microsurgeon of the mind' who helps patients make needed alterations in neuronal networks. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p226]
- Freud discovered that transferences of early traumatic scenes could be altered if pointed out when activated, allowing underlying neuronal networks and associated memories to be retranscribed and changed. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p231]
- Mr. L. experienced cycles of separations, dreams, depressions, and insights—the repetition or 'working through' required for long-term neuroplastic change—to wire new neurons together and weaken old neuronal links. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p237]

## Inferences

- Inference: The success of Mr. L.'s treatment at age 58-62 demonstrates that character change remains possible in older adults despite Freud's concerns about reduced plasticity.
