# Historical Debate on Brain Localization

## Summary

The question of whether mental functions are localized to specific brain regions or distributed across the cortex was debated for over a century. Franz Joseph Gall championed localization but used flawed methods (skull bumps/phrenology). Pierre Flourens argued for equipotentiality (all regions equal). Broca and Wernicke's studies of aphasia patients provided evidence for specific language areas. Karl Lashley's law of mass action held that memory depends on amount rather than location of cortex. Brenda Milner's work on H.M. ultimately disproved Lashley by showing that specific memory functions require specific brain structures.

## Key facts

- Gall proposed that specific mental faculties are controlled by distinct regions of cortex but his method of studying skulls was flawed and led to phrenology. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p131]
- Flourens concluded that the cortex is equipotential—all regions can perform any function and only the size of removed area matters, not location. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p133]
- Broca found that language production is localized to the left frontal lobe (Broca's area) in all patients studied with speech deficits. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p134]
- Lashley's law of mass action held that memory impairment correlates with the size of cortical area removed, not its specific location. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p137]
- Milner's work on H.M. disproved Lashley by showing that specific memory deficits result from damage to specific structures (hippocampus and medial temporal lobe). [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p141]
