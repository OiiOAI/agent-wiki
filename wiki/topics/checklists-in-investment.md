# Checklists in Investment

## Summary

The application of checklist methodology to investment decision-making by value investors seeking to counteract cognitive biases and improve systematic analysis.

## Key facts

- Mohnish Pabrai, managing a $500 million portfolio, developed a written checklist of about 70 items to guard against repeated investment mistakes, including checking whether revenues might be overstated due to boom conditions. [raw/books/strategy/The checklist manifesto _ how to get things right - Atul Gawande.pdf#p117-118]
- An anonymous investor created detailed checklists with pause points including a Day Three Checklist requiring verification of key financial statements for the previous ten years, including checking footnotes on cash flow statements. [raw/books/strategy/The checklist manifesto _ how to get things right - Atul Gawande.pdf#p118]
- Investor checklists increased up-front work time but paradoxically made the overall decision process faster and more efficient, allowing teams to sort prospects more quickly and move on. [raw/books/strategy/The checklist manifesto _ how to get things right - Atul Gawande.pdf#p119]
- Geoff Smart's study of 51 venture capitalists found that the 'Airline Captain' checklist-driven approach had a 10% likelihood of later needing to fire senior management, versus over 50% for other decision-making styles. [raw/books/strategy/The checklist manifesto _ how to get things right - Atul Gawande.pdf#p121]
- Airline Captain-style investors had a median 80% return on investment compared to 35% or less for other styles, and only one in eight investors naturally took this approach. [raw/books/strategy/The checklist manifesto _ how to get things right - Atul Gawande.pdf#p121-122]

## Inferences

- Inference: The resistance of investors to checklists mirrors resistance in medicine—those who most need systematic aids are often least inclined to use them.
