# Insiders and Outsiders

## Summary

A chapter examining how different societies distinguish between insiders and outsiders, with particular focus on how guilds, cultural attitudes, and national identity shape economic behavior and international relations.

## Key facts

- Medieval guilds were responsible for developing trademarks, seals, and early brand names. [raw/books/sociology/Trust - Francis Fukuyama.pdf#p435]
- Compulsory public education was introduced in Massachusetts in 1852 and was adopted by virtually all U.S. states by World War I. [raw/books/sociology/Trust - Francis Fukuyama.pdf#p435]
- Universal public education was not introduced in Britain until 1880 and was not made free until 1891. [raw/books/sociology/Trust - Francis Fukuyama.pdf#p435]
- Alfred Chandler linked British failure to exploit entrepreneurial opportunities in key industries of the second industrial revolution to the family-oriented nature of British business. [raw/books/sociology/Trust - Francis Fukuyama.pdf#p436]
- Average annual working hours were 1,604 for Germany and 2,197 for Japan. [raw/books/sociology/Trust - Francis Fukuyama.pdf#p437]

## Inferences

- Inference: The guilds' development of trademarks and seals suggests that early forms of trust-based commercial institutions emerged within closed membership groups.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The extent to which German culture has changed post-WWII remains controversial, with evidence cited both for and against significant cultural transformation.
