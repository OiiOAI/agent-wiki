# Evolution of the Nervous System

## Summary

The evolution of the nervous system spans approximately 700 million years and was driven by the requirements of actively moving creatures. A nervous system is only necessary for multicellular creatures that can orchestrate active movement—sessile organisms evolved successfully without one. The sea squirt demonstrates this principle: its larval form has a simple brain for swimming, but upon becoming sessile as an adult, it digests most of its own brain. Nervous system evolution involved the segregation of sensory and motor functions, the appearance of interneurons, and cephalization.

## Key facts

- A nervous system is only necessary for multicellular creatures that can orchestrate and express active movement; sessile organisms such as plants evolved successfully without one. [raw/books/neuroscience/I of the Vortex _ From Neurons to Self - Rodolfo Riascos Llinás.pdf#p21]
- The sea squirt (tunicate) larval form is free-swimming with a brain-like ganglion of approximately 300 cells, but upon becoming sessile, digests most of its own brain, including its notochord. [raw/books/neuroscience/I of the Vortex _ From Neurons to Self - Rodolfo Riascos Llinás.pdf#p23]
- In early evolution, sensory and contractile functions were combined in single cells; these later segregated into distinct sensory and motor elements, with interneurons later interposed between them. [raw/books/neuroscience/I of the Vortex _ From Neurons to Self - Rodolfo Riascos Llinás.pdf#p17]

## Inferences

- Inference: The evolution of the nervous system represents the embedding of motricity—pulling properties from the outside immediately inside through intrinsic oscillatory properties and electrical coupling, moving them up the neuraxis into the encephalizing brain.
