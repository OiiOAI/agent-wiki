# Social Insects and Eusociality

## Summary

Social insects (ants, bees, wasps, and termites) represent the most extreme examples of altruism and cooperation in the animal kingdom, with sterile worker castes dedicated to supporting reproductive individuals. The book explains that this apparent altruism is compatible with selfish gene theory once the unusual Hymenoptera sex determination system is understood: workers may be more closely related to siblings (75%) than to potential offspring (50%), making sterility genetically advantageous. The topic covers the evolution of worker sterility, queen-worker dynamics, and brood parasitism (cuckoo behavior).

## Key facts

- In social insect colonies, most individuals are sterile workers; the 'germ line' (immortal gene continuity) flows through a minority of reproductive individuals—the analogues of our reproductive cells, while sterile workers are analogues of body cells like liver or muscle. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p209]
- Kamikaze behavior in honey bees is not astonishing once we accept that workers are sterile: suicide in the interests of caring for other individuals is incompatible with bearing one's own offspring, so it rarely evolves in fertile individuals but is costless for sterile workers. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p209]
- Honeyguide chicks use a sharp hooked beak to kill foster siblings immediately upon hatching, eliminating competition for food—a ruthless tactic comparable to but more extreme than cuckoo behavior. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p168]
- Common cuckoos hatch before foster siblings and systematically eject eggs from the nest by balancing them on their back between wing-stubs and walking up the side until they topple out, ensuring they have sole access to parental resources. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p168]
- The book suggests that brood parasitism (cuckoo-like behavior) may have driven the evolution of host defenses, explaining why some species' nests contain eggs that look dramatically different from the parents' own eggs—they have been selected to be recognizable as foreign. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Selfish Gene_ 40th Anniversary edition - Richard Dawkins.pdf#p168]

## Inferences

- Inference: The evolution of eusociality required both the haplodiploid relatedness asymmetry AND the presence of appropriate ecological conditions (stable nesting sites, defense against predators) that made grouping advantageous—relatedness alone was not sufficient.
- Inference: Brood parasites face their own evolutionary arms race with hosts, selecting for ever more sophisticated mimicry and earlier hatching, while hosts evolve better discrimination abilities.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Whether baby swallow 'fratricide' (throwing eggs from nests) actually occurs in nature—the Spanish observation was made in an experimental context with magpies and may not reflect natural behavior.
