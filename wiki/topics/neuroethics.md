# Neuroethics

## Summary

Neuroethics addresses the ethical implications of advances in neuroscience, including cognitive enhancement, brain privacy, and the use of memory-enhancing drugs. The Dana Foundation sponsored a 2002 symposium to stimulate discussion of these issues as new drugs and brain imaging techniques raise questions about personal responsibility and free will.

## Key facts

- The DANA Foundation stimulated studies in neuroethics in 2002 by sponsoring a symposium entitled 'Neuroethics: Mapping the Field' that brought together scientists, philosophers, lawyers, and clergy. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p342]
- Steven Hyman stated that 'matters ranging from brain privacy to enhancement of mood and memory should be matters of vigorous discussion, and ideally those discussions will mature before continued scientific advances force societies to respond.'. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p343]
- Kandel and colleagues published 'Neurocognitive Enhancement: What Can We Do and What Should We Do?' in Nature Reviews Neuroscience in 2004. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p343]
- Kandel argues that healthy young people are capable of studying and learning on their own without chemical memory enhancers, and that studying well is without a doubt the best cognitive enhancer for those capable of learning. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p342]

## Inferences

- Inference: As neuroscience advances, ethical frameworks must evolve to address questions about cognitive enhancement, brain privacy, and the implications of new pharmacological treatments that were inconceivable in previous decades.
