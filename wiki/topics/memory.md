# Memory

## Summary

Memory is an essential component of everything humans do, encompassing many different processes that allow experiences to be encoded, stored, and retrieved. Henry Molaison's case revolutionized understanding of memory by demonstrating that it is not a single process but a collection of many different processes, each occupying different brain circuits. Key distinctions include short-term vs. long-term memory, declarative vs. nondeclarative memory, and working memory. Memory enables continuity from moment to moment, day to day, and year to year, and shapes our identity through personal narratives constructed from our history.

## Key facts

- Memory is not a single event or snapshot but engages many parts of the brain in parallel. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p17]
- Henry's case demonstrated that memory does not reside in one spot in the brain. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p17]
- The brain needs three steps for memory: encode raw data into brain-compatible format, store for later use, and retrieve from storage. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p17]
- Memory provides continuity from moment to moment, morning to evening, day to day, and year to year. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p16]
- Memory influences quality of life and helps shape our identity through personal narratives. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p16]
- Henry's case answered more questions about memory than the entire previous century of scientific research. [raw/books/neuroscience/Permanent Present Tense _ The Unforgettable Life of the - Suzanne Corkin.pdf#p19]
