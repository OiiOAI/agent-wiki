# Raising Emotionally Agile Children

## Summary

This chapter (Chapter 10) presents strategies for raising emotionally agile children in an age of intense competition and parental overprotectiveness. Key principles include: modeling emotional agility by practicing it oneself; helping children show up to all their feelings including difficult ones; promoting autonomy rather than control; teaching empathy through autonomous acts of generosity; and using emotional coaching that combines acknowledging feelings with brainstorming solutions. The chapter emphasizes that emotional agility acts as a 'vaccine' helping children develop flexibility and resilience to flourish even during hard times.

## Key facts

- Emotional agility is like a vaccine that helps inoculate kids against being overwhelmed by the moments of unpleasantness that life has in store for them. [raw/books/psychology/Emotional Agility - Susan David.pdf#p220]
- Parents' focus on achievement promotes a very narrowly defined concept of success focused on getting a certain kind of job that will allow earning a certain level of income. [raw/books/psychology/Emotional Agility - Susan David.pdf#p219]
- According to projections, 65 percent of today's elementary-school-age kids may end up doing work that hasn't even been invented yet. [raw/books/psychology/Emotional Agility - Susan David.pdf#p219]
- Overparenting can lead to contingent self-esteem—the belief that one's worth must be earned—which manifests in students who fall apart when they underperform. [raw/books/psychology/Emotional Agility - Susan David.pdf#p219]
- The most effective way to teach children emotional agility is by practicing it yourself. [raw/books/psychology/Emotional Agility - Susan David.pdf#p225]
- By simply letting our children know that we see them in full, clearly and without judgment, we signal that we accept and validate their emotional experience. [raw/books/psychology/Emotional Agility - Susan David.pdf#p226]
- Display rules are lessons we teach kids about what is and is not an appropriate emotional response—extreme examples include 'Buck up! Big boys don't cry.'. [raw/books/psychology/Emotional Agility - Susan David.pdf#p225]
- When children exhibit a reduction in emotional intensity, it often occurs when a parent is emotionally present. [raw/books/psychology/Emotional Agility - Susan David.pdf#p226]
- Children who feel free to experience the full range of emotions without fear learn that emotions pass, emotions are not scary, and emotions are teachers. [raw/books/psychology/Emotional Agility - Susan David.pdf#p227]
- Encouraging autonomy helps kids develop a 'why' to walk—a set of values that is their own, separate from rewards and requirements. [raw/books/psychology/Emotional Agility - Susan David.pdf#p230]
