# Industrial Concentration

## Summary

A measure of the degree to which an industry is dominated by a few large firms. Fukuyama discusses Scherer and Ross's findings that American companies in many sectors are larger than optimal scale predictions suggest, and cites evidence that similar industries produce similar levels of industrial concentration across different countries.

## Key facts

- According to Scherer and Ross (1990), American companies in many sectors are actually larger than optimal scale alone would predict, as shown in table 4.6 indicating that average market share of three largest firms exceeds minimum efficient scale in cigarettes, fabric weaving, paints, shoes, steel, storage batteries, and other products. [raw/books/sociology/Trust - Francis Fukuyama.pdf#p453]
- Scherer and Ross (1990), pp. 141-146, propose that market structure may be determined by sheer historical chance, where industries starting with equal-sized firms produce widely differing sizes over time from random factors. [raw/books/sociology/Trust - Francis Fukuyama.pdf#p453]
- Caves in Schmalensee and Willig (1989), p. 1234, notes that similar industries produce similar levels of industrial concentration in different countries, implying that industrial structures become more homogeneous as countries move up the technological-developmental ladder. [raw/books/sociology/Trust - Francis Fukuyama.pdf#p453]

## Inferences

- Inference: Fukuyama seems to challenge the deterministic view that development automatically homogenizes industrial structures, arguing instead that different societies will excel in different sectors based on their organizational capabilities rather than level of development.
