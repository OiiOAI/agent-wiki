# Synaptic Pruning

## Summary

Synaptic pruning is the process in adolescence during which synaptic connections and neurons that have not been used extensively suddenly die off. An immature brain has 50 percent more synapses than an adult brain, making early intervention for learning disabilities particularly important.

## Key facts

- In an immature brain the number of connections among neurons, or synapses, is 50 percent greater than in the adult brain. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p51]
- When we reach adolescence, a massive 'pruning back' operation begins in the brain. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p51]
- Synaptic connections and neurons that have not been used extensively suddenly die off — a classic case of 'use it or lose it'. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p51]
- It is probably best to strengthen weakened brain areas while extra cortical real estate is available during childhood. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p52]
- Younger children often progress more quickly through brain exercises than adolescents because of this extra cortical capacity. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p51]
- Brain changes from training can occur late in life, though they do not develop as rapidly in older animals as in younger ones. [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p52]

## Inferences

- Inference: Early identification and treatment of learning disabilities is critical because the window for optimal brain exercise effectiveness narrows during adolescence [raw/books/neuroscience/The Brain That Changes Itself - Norman Doidge.pdf#p51-p52]
