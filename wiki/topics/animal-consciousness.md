# Animal Consciousness

## Summary

Animal consciousness refers to the possibility that non-human animals have conscious experiences. Seth argues that consciousness and intelligence are not the same thing, and that consciousness is more closely tied to being alive than to being intelligent. While all mammals likely have some form of consciousness due to shared brain mechanisms, there are dramatic differences in self-experience across species. Octopuses represent an independently evolved experiment in consciousness, with highly distributed nervous systems and remarkable cognitive abilities despite diverging from humans 600 million years ago.

## Key facts

- Consciousness and intelligence are not the same thing; using intelligence as a litmus test for consciousness commits errors of anthropocentrism and anthropomorphism. [raw/books/neuroscience/Being You _ A New Science of Consciousness - Seth, Anil.pdf#p217]
- Seth believes all mammals are conscious based on shared mechanisms including six-layered cortex, interconnected thalamus, deep brainstem, and common sleep-wake dynamics. [raw/books/neuroscience/Being You _ A New Science of Consciousness - Seth, Anil.pdf#p219]
- Mirror self-recognition develops in humans around 18-24 months, but monkeys consistently fail the mirror test despite being conscious with some kind of selfhood. [raw/books/neuroscience/Being You _ A New Science of Consciousness - Seth, Anil.pdf#p222]
- The most recent common ancestor of humans and octopuses lived about 600 million years ago—the octopus mind is an independently created evolutionary experiment. [raw/books/neuroscience/Being You _ A New Science of Consciousness - Seth, Anil.pdf#p224]
- Octopuses have about 500 million neurons, with about three-fifths located in their arms rather than their central brain, suggesting consciousness may be more distributed. [raw/books/neuroscience/Being You _ A New Science of Consciousness - Seth, Anil.pdf#p224]
- Octopuses are color-blind but can change color to match their surroundings through chromatophores controlled by the brain, even though the central brain might not know what its skin is doing. [raw/books/neuroscience/Being You _ A New Science of Consciousness - Seth, Anil.pdf#p226]
- Octopuses experience taste-based self-recognition through chemicals secreted in their skin, allowing discrimination of self from other without knowing where their body is in space. [raw/books/neuroscience/Being You _ A New Science of Consciousness - Seth, Anil.pdf#p227]
- The nematode worm with 302 neurons is difficult to ascribe meaningful conscious status to, and single-celled paramecia 'don't make the grade.'. [raw/books/neuroscience/Being You _ A New Science of Consciousness - Seth, Anil.pdf#p230]

## Inferences

- Inference: What it is like to be an octopus may not include experience of body ownership in anything like the human sense, due to their decentralized nervous system and arm autonomy.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: Whether fish are conscious remains unclear despite adaptive responses to painful events; insects may show pain-related behaviors but evidence is suggestive rather than conclusive.
