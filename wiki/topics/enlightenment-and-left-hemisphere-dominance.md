# The Enlightenment and Left Hemisphere Dominance

## Summary

The Enlightenment (roughly 17th–18th century) represents a period of left hemisphere ascendancy in Western culture. McGilchrist identifies its key features: emphasis on rationality over reason, clarity and explicitness, certainty and 'transmissibility,' symmetry and stasis, equality through categorical abstraction, and the devitalization of nature and the body. The Enlightenment's three core propositions—that all genuine questions can be answered, that answers can be taught, and that all answers must be compatible—came to dominate not just philosophy but life itself. This led to boredom, the 'uncanny,' and ultimately required Romanticism's compensatory return to right hemisphere values.

## Key facts

- The Enlightenment's three propositions were: all genuine questions can be answered; all answers are knowable and can be taught; all answers must be compatible with one another. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p463]
- The emphasis on 'light' in 'Enlightenment' suggests clarity and precision, but also the banishment of darker emotions; from 1681 King Lear was performed for 150 years with a happy ending. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p465]
- Symmetry in poetry, music, architecture, prose and thought was the ultimately guiding aesthetic principle of the Enlightenment, allied to stasis and equality—qualities of the left hemisphere. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p473]
- In portraiture of the Enlightenment, 'faces generally are represented more symmetrically than in any other Western style,' making such portraiture 'once seen, never remembered.'. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p473]
- The left hemisphere achieves equality through categorical abstraction: once items are classified, every member of the category can be substituted by any other, but categories themselves are arranged hierarchically. [raw/books/neuroscience/THE MASTER AND HIS EMISSARY - Iain McGilchrist.pdf#p474]

## Inferences

- Inference: The Enlightenment's devitalization and the rise of boredom represent the cost of left hemisphere dominance: vitality was seen as mediated by external novelty rather than internally generated meaning.
- Inference: The French Revolution's descent into violence exemplifies the paradox that the left hemisphere can only pursue its ideals negatively, dismantling rather than creating.
