# Huntington disease

## Summary

Huntington disease is an autosomal dominant neurodegenerative disorder caused by expansion of a CAG repeat in the huntingtin gene on chromosome 4. It typically strikes in early or middle adulthood and is characterized by chorea, cognitive impairment, and affective disturbance. The pathological hallmark is degeneration of the striatum with preferential loss of medium spiny neurons. Juvenile-onset cases show more rapid progression with rigidity and dystonia. The disease involves a toxic gain of function from the expanded polyglutamine tract in huntingtin.

## Key facts

- Huntington disease is an autosomal dominant disorder affecting 5 to 10 people per 100,000, caused by expansion of a translated CAG repeat encoding a glutamine tract in the huntingtin protein. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p1052]
- Normal or wild-type alleles have 6 to 34 repeats, whereas disease-associated alleles typically have 36 or more repeats. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p1052]
- The pathological hallmark of Huntington disease is degeneration of the striatum, with the caudate nucleus being more affected than the putamen. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p1052]
- Huntington disease appears to be a true dominant disease; patients homozygous for the condition do not differ significantly from heterozygous siblings. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p1052]
- The expanded glutamine tract causes huntingtin protein to gain toxic function in addition to its normal function. [raw/books/neuroscience/Principles of Neural Science, Fifth Edition (Principles of - Eric R_ Kandel; James H_ Schwartz; Thomas M_ Jessell; Steven.pdf#p1052]

## Inferences

- Inference: The loss of inhibitory medium spiny neurons reduces inhibition of external pallidum neurons, leading to excessive pallidal activity that inhibits the subthalamic nucleus, which may account for choreiform movements.
