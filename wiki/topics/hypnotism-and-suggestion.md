# Hypnotism and Suggestion

## Summary

Hypnotism and suggestion were central topics in Freud's early psychological work (1888-1892). The Nancy school, led by Bernheim and Liébeault, argued that all hypnotic phenomena derive from suggestion. The Salpêtrière school, led by Charcot, maintained that some hypnotic phenomena are physiological. Freud's position evolved: initially supporting Charcot, he later aligned more closely with Bernheim while still recognizing the genuine somatic phenomena of hypnosis. Freud eventually abandoned hypnosis as a therapeutic procedure around 1896, developing psychoanalysis in its place.

## Key facts

- The Nancy school defines hypnosis as a special psychical state in which suggestibility is increased. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p129]
- Suggestion consists in the brain accepting an idea presented to it from outside without exercising criticism upon it, although it has the material for such criticism at its disposal. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p130]
- The school of Nancy holds that all phenomena of hypnotism have the same origin: they arise from a suggestion, a conscious idea introduced into the brain of the hypnotized person by an external influence. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p83]
- The Salpêtrière school maintains that the mechanism of some manifestations of hypnotism is based upon physiological changes—displacements of excitability in the nervous system occurring without the participation of consciousness. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p83]
- Delboeuf expressed the proposition: 'There is no such thing as hypnosis, there are only varying types and degrees of suggestibility.'. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p131]
- Bernheim achieves somnambulism in ten out of twenty patients during a ward round, including those admitted only one or two days previously. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p135]
- Freud states that the principal value of Bernheim's work lies in proving the relations linking hypnotic phenomena with ordinary processes of waking and sleeping. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p81]
- The technique of hypnotizing is just as difficult a medical procedure as any other. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p115]
- Freud maintained that hypnosis should be abandoned around 1896, having not used it therapeutically for about eight years. [raw/books/psychology/The Revised Standard Edition of The Complete Psychological - Sigmund Freud, James Strachey , Mark Solms.pdf#p68]

## Inferences

- Inference: Freud's shift from hypnosis to psychoanalysis reflects his growing dissatisfaction with suggestion as an unexplained mechanism and his search for deeper psychical causation.
- Inference: The controversy between Nancy and Salpêtrière schools represented a broader debate about whether psychological phenomena could be reduced to psychical (suggestion) or required physiological explanation.
- Inference: Freud's interest in hypnosis led directly to his development of the concept of the unconscious, which appears to be his first published use of the term 'the unconscious' in this very material.

## Uncertainties

- Uncertain: The exact moment Freud began using Breuer's cathartic method with hypnosis is not precisely dated; it was certainly used by May 1889 or possibly a year earlier.
