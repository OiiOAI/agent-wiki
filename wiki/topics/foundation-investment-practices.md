# Foundation Investment Practices

## Summary

Munger's critique of foundation investment practices argues that most foundations suffer from excessive complexity, too many layers of consultants, and high frictional costs that effectively 'febezzle' foundation assets. He recommends following Berkshire Hathaway's approach of concentrated, passive investment in a few admired domestic corporations with minimal turnover.

## Key facts

- Even with unleveraged stock picking, total cost of all investment management plus frictional costs can easily reach three percent of foundation net worth per annum. [raw/books/strategy/Poor Charlie's Almanack_ The Wit and Wisdo - Charles T. Munger, Peter D. Kaufman, Ed We.pdf#p333]
- Munger argues it can be rational for a family or foundation to remain ninety percent concentrated in one equity, as the Woodruff foundations did with Coca-Cola. [raw/books/strategy/Poor Charlie's Almanack_ The Wit and Wisdo - Charles T. Munger, Peter D. Kaufman, Ed We.pdf#p340]
- Munger identifies three choices for foundations: indexing with minimal costs; imitating Berkshire Hathaway with few stocks and virtually zero turnover; or investing in fancy limited partnerships. [raw/books/strategy/Poor Charlie's Almanack_ The Wit and Wisdo - Charles T. Munger, Peter D. Kaufman, Ed We.pdf#p336-338]

## Inferences

- Inference: Munger predicts that if foundations make their investment style more like Berkshire Hathaway's, they will be unlikely to regret it in the long term.
