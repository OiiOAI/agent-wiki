# Late Development Hypothesis

## Summary

A theory in economic history and development economics arguing that countries industrializing late can achieve rapid growth through institutional mechanisms that compensate for absent market conditions, such as state-directed banking, large-scale enterprise, and technology transfer. The hypothesis has been articulated by Alexander Gerschenkron, Ronald Dore, and Chalmers Johnson in comparative studies of industrialization in Germany, Japan, and other nations.

## Key facts

- Alexander Gerschenkron articulated the late development hypothesis in his work Economic Backwardness in Historical Perspective (Cambridge: Harvard University Press, 1962). [raw/books/sociology/Trust - Francis Fukuyama.pdf#p453]
- Ronald Dore discussed the late development hypothesis in relation to industrial relations in Japan and elsewhere. [raw/books/sociology/Trust - Francis Fukuyama.pdf#p453]
- Chalmers Johnson developed the late development hypothesis in MITI and the Japanese Miracle (Stanford: Stanford University Press, 1982), p. 19. [raw/books/sociology/Trust - Francis Fukuyama.pdf#p453]
- Fukuyama argues that different societies will excel in different sectors dependent not on their level of development but rather on each society's ability to generate large-scale organizations. [raw/books/sociology/Trust - Francis Fukuyama.pdf#p453]

## Inferences

- Inference: Fukuyama extends the late development hypothesis by connecting it to social capital theory, arguing that the ability to generate large-scale organizations depends on underlying trust levels and spontaneous sociability in a society.
