# Unconscious Versus Conscious Emotional Processing

## Summary

Kandel's collaboration with Amit Etkin and Joy Hirsch explored how conscious and unconscious perceptions of emotional stimuli are processed differently in the brain. Using pictures of fearful versus neutral faces from Peter Ekman's catalog, they found that unconscious perception of fearful faces activated the basolateral nucleus of the amygdala in proportion to a person's baseline anxiety, while conscious perception activated the central nucleus regardless of anxiety level. This biologically validated Freud's idea that unconscious mental processes are part of the brain's information processing system.

## Key facts

- Unconscious perception of fearful faces activated the basolateral nucleus of the amygdala in direct proportion to a person's background anxiety—the higher the anxiety, the greater the response, with low-anxiety people showing no response. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p395]
- Conscious perception of fearful faces activated the dorsal region of the amygdala containing the central nucleus, regardless of a person's background anxiety. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p395]
- The findings support the psychoanalytic idea that unconscious emotion effects are exerted most dramatically when stimuli are left to imagination rather than perceived consciously; once confronted consciously, even anxious people can accurately appraise threats. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p396]
- The discovery of a correlation between background anxiety and unconscious neural processes validates biologically the Freudian idea that unconscious mental processes are part of the brain's system of information processing. [raw/books/neuroscience/In Search of Memory_ The Emergence of a Ne - Eric R. Kandel.pdf#p396]
